Polar and Cartesian Coordinates

How far, and in which direction

Instead of "across $x$, up $y$", a point can be located by its distance $r$ from the origin O (the pole, 極) and the angle $\theta$ that OP makes with the positive $x$-axis (the initial line, 始線). $(r, \theta)$ are the polar coordinates (極座標).

An equation in $r$ and $\theta$ is a polar equation (極方程式). Circles through O and curves that wind around O often have much simpler polar equations than $xy$-equations.

Point P: $(x, y)$ and $(r, \theta)$, with $r \ge 0$

$$x = r\cos\theta, \qquad y = r\sin\theta, \qquad r = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2}$$

For $r > 0$, $\theta$ is fixed by $\cos\theta = \dfrac{x}{r}$, $\sin\theta = \dfrac{y}{r}$ (both signs matter). Usually take $0 \le \theta < 2\pi$; adding $2n\pi$ gives the same point.

  1. To change a polar equation to $x$, $y$: multiply through to make $r\cos\theta$, $r\sin\theta$ and $r^2$ appear. Then replace them by $x$, $y$ and $x^2 + y^2$.
  2. Example: $r = 2\cos\theta$. Multiply by $r$: $r^2 = 2r\cos\theta$, so $x^2 + y^2 = 2x$.
  3. That is $(x - 1)^2 + y^2 = 1$: the circle with centre $(1, 0)$ and radius $1$, which passes through O.

Example 1

Write the polar point $\left(6, \dfrac{2\pi}{3}\right)$ in $xy$-coordinates.

Show solution
  1. $x = 6\cos\dfrac{2\pi}{3} = -3$, $\;y = 6\sin\dfrac{2\pi}{3} = 3\sqrt{3}$.

$(-3, 3\sqrt{3})$

Example 2

Write $(-2, 2)$ and $(1, -\sqrt{3})$ in polar coordinates ($0 \le \theta < 2\pi$).

Show solution
  1. $(-2, 2)$: $r = 2\sqrt{2}$, $\cos\theta = -\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}$, $\sin\theta = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}$, so $\theta = \dfrac{3\pi}{4}$ (second quadrant).
  2. $(1, -\sqrt{3})$: $r = 2$, $\cos\theta = \dfrac{1}{2}$, $\sin\theta = -\dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}$, so $\theta = \dfrac{5\pi}{3}$.

$\left(2\sqrt{2}, \dfrac{3\pi}{4}\right)$ and $\left(2, \dfrac{5\pi}{3}\right)$

Example 3

Write the line $x + y = 2$ and the circle $x^2 + y^2 = 4y$ as polar equations.

Show solution
  1. $r\cos\theta + r\sin\theta = 2$. Using $\cos\theta + \sin\theta = \sqrt{2}\cos\left(\theta - \dfrac{\pi}{4}\right)$: $r\cos\left(\theta - \dfrac{\pi}{4}\right) = \sqrt{2}$.
  2. $r^2 = 4r\sin\theta$, so $r = 4\sin\theta$ (this also includes O).

$r\cos\left(\theta - \dfrac{\pi}{4}\right) = \sqrt{2}$ and $r = 4\sin\theta$

Example 4

Write the polar equation $r = \dfrac{2}{1 - \cos\theta}$ in $x$ and $y$, and name the curve.

Show solution
  1. $r - r\cos\theta = 2$, so $r = x + 2$.
  2. Square: $x^2 + y^2 = x^2 + 4x + 4$.

$y^2 = 4(x + 1)$, a parabola with focus O (the case $e = 1$ of note 04)

Don't find $\theta$ from $\dfrac{y}{x}$ alone: $(-2, 2)$ and $(2, -2)$ give the same ratio but opposite directions. Use the signs of both $\cos\theta$ and $\sin\theta$ (or a sketch) to choose the quadrant.
Use polar coordinates when the problem is about distance from O or about angles: circles through O, rotations, spirals, and conics with a focus at O. Use $x$ and $y$ for straight lines and rectangles.
  • $x = r\cos\theta$, $y = r\sin\theta$, $r^2 = x^2 + y^2$.
  • $\theta$ from $\cos\theta = \dfrac{x}{r}$ and $\sin\theta = \dfrac{y}{r}$ together.
  • Polar equation to $xy$: multiply by $r$ (or rearrange), then substitute.
