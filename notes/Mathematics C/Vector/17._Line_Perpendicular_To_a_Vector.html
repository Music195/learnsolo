Line Perpendicular To a Vector

Every step along the line is at right angles to $\vec{n}$

A line through A can also be fixed by a nonzero vector $\vec{n}$ perpendicular to it, called a normal vector (法線ベクトル). P is on the line exactly when the step $\overrightarrow{AP}$ is perpendicular to $\vec{n}$, that is $\vec{n} \cdot \overrightarrow{AP} = 0$.

In components this is $a(x - x_1) + b(y - y_1) = 0$. Read backwards: in any line $ax + by + c = 0$, the coefficients give a normal vector $(a, b)$.

Through A$(\vec{a}) = (x_1, y_1)$, normal $\vec{n} = (a, b) \ne \vec{0}$

$$\vec{n} \cdot (\vec{p} - \vec{a}) = 0 \iff a(x - x_1) + b(y - y_1) = 0$$

The line $ax + by + c = 0$ has normal vector $(a, b)$ and direction vector $(b, -a)$.

  1. P = A, or $\overrightarrow{AP} \perp \vec{n}$; either way $\vec{n} \cdot \overrightarrow{AP} = 0$.
  2. $\overrightarrow{AP} = (x - x_1, y - y_1)$, so $\vec{n} \cdot \overrightarrow{AP} = a(x - x_1) + b(y - y_1)$.
  3. Conversely, any point with $a(x - x_1) + b(y - y_1) = 0$ has $\overrightarrow{AP} \perp \vec{n}$ (or P = A), so it is on the line.

Example 4

Find the angle between the lines $x - 3y + 1 = 0$ and $2x - y - 4 = 0$ (the acute one).

Show solution
  1. Normals $(1, -3)$ and $(2, -1)$; the angle between the lines equals the angle between the normals, or $180°$ minus it.
  2. $\cos\theta = \dfrac{2 + 3}{\sqrt{10}\sqrt{5}} = \dfrac{5}{5\sqrt{2}} = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}$.

$45°$

Don't mix up the two vectors of a line: in $ax + by + c = 0$, $(a, b)$ is the normal, and the direction is $(b, -a)$.
Two lines are perpendicular exactly when their normal vectors are: $a_1a_2 + b_1b_2 = 0$. No slopes needed, so vertical lines cause no trouble.
  • $\vec{n} \cdot (\vec{p} - \vec{a}) = 0$, i.e. $a(x - x_1) + b(y - y_1) = 0$.
  • $ax + by + c = 0$ has normal $(a, b)$, direction $(b, -a)$.
  • Angles between lines come from their normal vectors.
