Counting and Probability

Five problems on permutations, combinations, the complement rule and repeated trials. Try each one first. Open the hint if you are stuck, then compare with the full solution.

Problem 1

Five people, including A and B, stand in a row. In how many ways can they stand with A and B next to each other?

Uses: Permutation Count, Multiplication Rule

Show hint

Tie A and B together as one block. Then the block itself can be in two orders.

Show solution
  1. The block AB plus 3 others is $4$ items: $4! = 24$ orders.
  2. Inside the block: AB or BA, $2$ ways.
  3. $24 \cdot 2 = 48$.

$48$ ways

Problem 2

A committee of 2 boys and 2 girls is chosen from 5 boys and 4 girls. How many committees are possible?

Uses: Combination Count

Show hint

Choose the boys and the girls separately, then multiply.

Show solution
  1. Boys: ${}_5\mathrm{C}_2 = 10$. Girls: ${}_4\mathrm{C}_2 = 6$.
  2. $10 \cdot 6 = 60$.

$60$ committees

Problem 3

How many different arrangements are there of the seven letters of SUCCESS?

Uses: Permutation with Identicals

Show hint

Count the repeated letters: S appears 3 times and C twice. Divide $7!$ by the orders of the identical letters.

Show solution
  1. S × 3, C × 2, U × 1, E × 1.
  2. $\dfrac{7!}{3!\,2!} = \dfrac{5040}{12} = 420$.

$420$ arrangements

Problem 4

A die is rolled 3 times. Find the probability that at least one 6 appears.

Uses: Complement Probability, Independent Probability

Show hint

“At least one” is easiest through the complement: no 6 at all.

Show solution
  1. P(no 6 in 3 rolls) $= \left(\dfrac{5}{6}\right)^3 = \dfrac{125}{216}$.
  2. P(at least one 6) $= 1 - \dfrac{125}{216} = \dfrac{91}{216}$.

$\dfrac{91}{216}$

Problem 5

A die is rolled 4 times. Find the probability that a 1 or a 2 comes up at least twice.

Uses: Binomial Probability, Complement Probability

Show hint

Each roll gives 1 or 2 with probability $\dfrac{1}{3}$. Subtract the cases “0 times” and “exactly once” from $1$.

Show solution
  1. 0 times: $\left(\dfrac{2}{3}\right)^4 = \dfrac{16}{81}$.
  2. Exactly once: ${}_4\mathrm{C}_1 \cdot \dfrac{1}{3} \cdot \left(\dfrac{2}{3}\right)^3 = \dfrac{32}{81}$.
  3. At least twice: $1 - \dfrac{16}{81} - \dfrac{32}{81} = \dfrac{33}{81} = \dfrac{11}{27}$.

$\dfrac{11}{27}$
