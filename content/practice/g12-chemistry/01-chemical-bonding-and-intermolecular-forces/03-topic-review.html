Problem 1

Write the electron configuration of potassium ($Z = 19$). What charge does its ion carry? (Type the charge, e.g. 1 or 2.)

Uses: Basic Concepts to Understand Chemical Bonding, Ionic Bonding

Show hint

After $3p^6$ the next electron goes into $4s$, not $3d$.

Show solution
  1. $\mathrm{K}: 1s^2\,2s^2\,2p^6\,3s^2\,3p^6\,4s^1$.
  2. Losing the single $4s$ electron gives $\mathrm{K^+}$ (charge $+1$) with the argon configuration.

$\mathrm{K^+}$, charge $+1$

Problem 2

Using $\mathrm{Mg} = 1.2$ and $\mathrm{O} = 3.5$, find $\Delta\mathrm{EN}$ for magnesium oxide. Is it ionic? Explain why MgO has a much higher melting point than NaCl. (Type $\Delta\mathrm{EN}$.)

Uses: Ionic Bonding

Show hint

Compare with $1.8$; then compare the ion charges.

Show solution
  1. $\Delta\mathrm{EN} = 3.5 - 1.2 = 2.3 > 1.8$: ionic.
  2. MgO is made of $\mathrm{Mg^{2+}}$ and $\mathrm{O^{2-}}$; NaCl of $\mathrm{Na^+}$ and $\mathrm{Cl^-}$. Doubly charged, smaller ions attract much more strongly, so far more energy is needed to break the lattice.

$\Delta\mathrm{EN} = 2.3$: ionic, with stronger $2+/2-$ attractions

Problem 3

How many valence electrons are there in the ammonium ion, $\mathrm{NH_4^+}$? What is its shape? (Type the number of electrons.)

Uses: Covalent Bonding, Lewis Structures and Molecular Shapes

Show hint

Add the valence electrons of the atoms, then subtract one for the positive charge.

Show solution
  1. $5 + 4 \times 1 - 1 = 8$ electrons $= 4$ bonding pairs, no lone pairs.
  2. Shape: tetrahedral, $109.5^\circ$. (The fourth N–H bond is a dative bond from the lone pair of $\mathrm{NH_3}$ to $\mathrm{H^+}$.)

$8$ electrons; tetrahedral

Problem 4

Use the metallic bonding model to explain why metals (i) conduct electricity and (ii) can be hammered into sheets without breaking.

Uses: Metallic Bonding

Show hint

Think of positive ions in a "sea" of delocalised electrons.

Show solution
  1. (i) The delocalised electrons are free to move through the whole lattice, so they carry charge when a voltage is applied.
  2. (ii) When layers of ions slide over each other, the electron sea still holds them together: no bonds are broken, so the metal bends instead of shattering (unlike an ionic crystal, where like charges would meet).

Mobile delocalised electrons carry charge and keep holding the sliding layers together

Problem 5

Boron trichloride, $\mathrm{BCl_3}$, has three bonding pairs and no lone pairs on boron. Give its shape and bond angle. Using $\mathrm{B} = 2.0$ and $\mathrm{Cl} = 3.0$, explain why the molecule is non-polar. (Type the angle in degrees.)

Uses: Covalent Bonding, Lewis Structures and Molecular Shapes, Polarity and Intermolecular Forces

Show hint

Three electron pairs spread to the corners of a triangle.

Show solution
  1. Shape: trigonal planar, Cl–B–Cl $= 120^\circ$.
  2. $\Delta\mathrm{EN} = 1.0$, so each B–Cl bond is polar, but the three equal dipoles at $120^\circ$ add to zero: the molecule is non-polar.

Trigonal planar, $120^\circ$; the three bond dipoles cancel

Problem 6

Water boils at $100^\circ\mathrm{C}$ but hydrogen sulphide, $\mathrm{H_2S}$, boils at about $-60^\circ\mathrm{C}$, although $\mathrm{H_2S}$ is the heavier molecule. Find $\Delta\mathrm{EN}$ for O–H and S–H ($\mathrm{O} = 3.5$, $\mathrm{S} = 2.5$, $\mathrm{H} = 2.1$) and explain the difference. (Type $\Delta\mathrm{EN}$ for O–H.)

Uses: Polarity and Intermolecular Forces

Show hint

Which molecule can form hydrogen bonds?

Show solution
  1. O–H: $3.5 - 2.1 = 1.4$, strongly polar. S–H: $2.5 - 2.1 = 0.4$, nearly non-polar.
  2. Water molecules are held by hydrogen bonds (H on O, lone pairs on O), which need much more energy to break. $\mathrm{H_2S}$ has only weak dipole–dipole and London forces.

$\Delta\mathrm{EN}(\mathrm{O{-}H}) = 1.4$: water forms hydrogen bonds; $\mathrm{H_2S}$ cannot
