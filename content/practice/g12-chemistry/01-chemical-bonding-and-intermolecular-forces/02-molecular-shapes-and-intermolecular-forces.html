Molecular Shapes, Intermolecular Forces and Metallic Bonding

Six problems on Lewis structures and lone pairs, VSEPR shapes and bond angles, bond polarity from $\Delta\mathrm{EN}$, intermolecular forces and metallic bonding. Try each one first. Open the hint if you are stuck, then compare with the full step-by-step solution.

Problem 1

Draw the Lewis structure of water. How many lone pairs are on the oxygen atom? (Type the number.)

Uses: Covalent Bonding, Lewis Structures and Molecular Shapes

Show hint

Lone pairs $= \dfrac{\text{total valence electrons} - 2 \times \text{bonds}}{2}$.

Show solution
  1. Valence electrons: $6 + 2 \times 1 = 8$. Two O–H bonds use $4$.
  2. Remaining $8 - 4 = 4$ electrons $= 2$ lone pairs, both on oxygen.

$2$ lone pairs on O

Problem 2

What is the H–C–H bond angle in methane, $\mathrm{CH_4}$, and what is its shape? (Type the angle in degrees, to one decimal place.)

Uses: Covalent Bonding, Lewis Structures and Molecular Shapes

Show hint

Four bonding pairs and no lone pairs on carbon spread as far apart as possible.

Show solution
  1. Four electron pairs point to the corners of a regular tetrahedron.
  2. Angle $= 109.5^\circ$; shape: tetrahedral.

$109.5^\circ$, tetrahedral

Problem 3

Ammonia, $\mathrm{NH_3}$, has three bonding pairs and one lone pair on nitrogen. Name its shape and give the H–N–H bond angle. Why is it less than in methane? (Type the angle in degrees.)

Uses: Covalent Bonding, Lewis Structures and Molecular Shapes

Show hint

A lone pair repels more strongly than a bonding pair.

Show solution
  1. Four electron pairs are arranged tetrahedrally, but only three hold atoms: the shape is trigonal pyramidal.
  2. The lone pair pushes the bonds closer together: $107^\circ$ instead of $109.5^\circ$. (With two lone pairs, water closes further to $104.5^\circ$.)

Trigonal pyramidal, $107^\circ$

Problem 4

Find $\Delta\mathrm{EN}$ for the H–Cl bond ($\mathrm{H} = 2.1$, $\mathrm{Cl} = 3.0$) and for the C–H bond ($\mathrm{C} = 2.5$). Classify each bond. (Type $\Delta\mathrm{EN}$ for H–Cl.)

Uses: Polarity and Intermolecular Forces

Show hint

$0$: non-polar; between $0$ and $1.8$: polar covalent; above $1.8$: ionic. Very small differences behave as almost non-polar.

Show solution
  1. H–Cl: $3.0 - 2.1 = 0.9$, polar covalent, with $\delta-$ on Cl.
  2. C–H: $2.5 - 2.1 = 0.4$, only slightly polar, so hydrocarbons behave as non-polar molecules.

H–Cl $0.9$ (polar covalent); C–H $0.4$ (nearly non-polar)

Problem 5

Draw the Lewis structure of carbon dioxide. How many lone pairs does the molecule have in total? Explain why $\mathrm{CO_2}$ is non-polar although its bonds are polar. (Type the number of lone pairs.)

Uses: Covalent Bonding, Lewis Structures and Molecular Shapes, Polarity and Intermolecular Forces

Show hint

Valence electrons: $4 + 2 \times 6$. Carbon forms two double bonds.

Show solution
  1. Total $= 16$ electrons; $\mathrm{O{=}C{=}O}$ uses $4$ bonds $= 8$ electrons.
  2. Remaining $8$ electrons $= 4$ lone pairs, two on each oxygen.
  3. The molecule is linear ($180^\circ$): the two C=O dipoles are equal and opposite, so they cancel.

$4$ lone pairs; linear, so the bond dipoles cancel

Problem 6

Which of these substances have hydrogen bonds between their molecules: $\mathrm{CH_4}$, $\mathrm{NH_3}$, $\mathrm{HF}$, $\mathrm{HCl}$, $\mathrm{H_2O}$, $\mathrm{CH_3OH}$? (Type how many.)

Uses: Polarity and Intermolecular Forces

Show hint

A hydrogen bond needs H bonded directly to N, O or F, and a lone pair on N, O or F of a neighbouring molecule.

Show solution
  1. $\mathrm{NH_3}$ (N–H), $\mathrm{HF}$ (F–H), $\mathrm{H_2O}$ (O–H) and $\mathrm{CH_3OH}$ (O–H): yes.
  2. $\mathrm{CH_4}$: H is on C, only London forces. $\mathrm{HCl}$: Cl is too large and not electronegative enough, so dipole–dipole forces only.
  3. Answer: $4$. These four boil far higher than their size alone suggests.

$4$: $\mathrm{NH_3}$, $\mathrm{HF}$, $\mathrm{H_2O}$, $\mathrm{CH_3OH}$
