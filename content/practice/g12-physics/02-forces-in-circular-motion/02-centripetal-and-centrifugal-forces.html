Centripetal and Centrifugal Forces

Six problems on the real inward centripetal force, the apparent outward centrifugal force in a rotating frame, and everyday examples. Try each one first. Open the hint if you are stuck, then compare with the full step-by-step solution.

Problem 1

An athlete of weight $790\ \mathrm{N}$ runs round a curve of radius $5.0\ \mathrm{m}$ at $6.0\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$. Find the centripetal force and name what supplies it. (Type $F_c$ in N.)

Uses: Centripetal and Centrifugal Forces

Show hint

Find the mass from the weight, $m = w/g$.

Show solution
  1. $m = \dfrac{790}{9.8} = 80.61\ \mathrm{kg}$.
  2. $F_c = \dfrac{mv^2}{r} = \dfrac{80.61 \times 36}{5.0} \approx 580\ \mathrm{N}$.
  3. It is supplied by friction between the shoes and the track.

$F_c \approx 580\ \mathrm{N}$, supplied by friction

Problem 2

In the athlete's own (rotating) frame of reference she feels pushed outward. What is the size of this centrifugal force, and is it a real force?

Uses: Centripetal and Centrifugal Forces

Show hint

In the rotating frame an apparent force $-m\vec a_c$ is added so that the forces balance.

Show solution
  1. The centrifugal force has the same size as the centripetal force, $\approx 580\ \mathrm{N}$, but points outward.
  2. It is not a real force: no object exerts it. It appears only because her frame is accelerating (non-inertial).

About $580\ \mathrm{N}$ outward; a fictitious (apparent) force

Problem 3

A $60\ \mathrm{kg}$ passenger sits in a car taking a flat curve of radius $30\ \mathrm{m}$ at $12\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$. What sideways force does the seat (and door) exert on the passenger? Which way does the passenger seem to be thrown, and why? (Type the force in N.)

Uses: Centripetal and Centrifugal Forces

Show hint

From the road (inertial frame) the passenger needs a real inward force $mv^2/r$.

Show solution
  1. $F = \dfrac{mv^2}{r} = \dfrac{60 \times 144}{30} = 288\ \mathrm{N}$, inward.
  2. Her body tends to keep moving in a straight line (inertia), so relative to the car she seems thrown outward; in the car's frame this is described by a centrifugal force of $288\ \mathrm{N}$.

$288\ \mathrm{N}$ inward; she seems thrown outward because of inertia

Problem 4

A washing-machine drum of radius $0.25\ \mathrm{m}$ spins at $1200\ \mathrm{rpm}$. Find the centripetal force on a $0.10\ \mathrm{kg}$ wet sock. Why does the water leave the clothes through the holes? (Type the force in N to the nearest 10 N.)

Uses: Centripetal and Centrifugal Forces, Centripetal Acceleration

Show hint

$\omega = 1200 \times 2\pi/60 = 40\pi\ \mathrm{rad\,s^{-1}}$ and $F = mr\omega^2$.

Show solution
  1. $F = m r \omega^2 = 0.10 \times 0.25 \times (40\pi)^2 = 395\ \mathrm{N}$.
  2. The drum wall pushes the sock inward, but water drops at the holes have nothing to give them this force, so they carry on in a straight line (tangentially) and leave the drum.

$F \approx 390\ \mathrm{N}$; water escapes along the tangent because no inward force acts on it

Problem 5

A skater turning in a circle leans inward. Explain this using forces. If the skater's speed is $8.0\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ on a circle of radius $10\ \mathrm{m}$, at what angle to the vertical must the skater lean? (Type the angle in degrees, to 3 significant figures.)

Uses: Centripetal and Centrifugal Forces, Forces Causing Centripetal Acceleration

Show hint

The ice pushes up (normal force) and inward (friction). Their resultant must pass through the skater's centre of mass.

Show solution
  1. Friction gives the centripetal force $mv^2/r$; the normal force balances $mg$. Leaning lines the body up with the resultant, so there is no turning effect that would tip the skater over.
  2. $\tan\theta = \dfrac{mv^2/r}{mg} = \dfrac{v^2}{rg} = \dfrac{64}{98} = 0.653$, so $\theta \approx 33.1^\circ$.

Lean so the resultant of friction and normal force passes through the centre of mass: $\theta \approx 33.1^\circ$

Problem 6

A $50\ \mathrm{kg}$ child sits $2.0\ \mathrm{m}$ from the centre of a merry-go-round turning once every $5.0\ \mathrm{s}$. The coefficient of static friction is $0.40$. Will the child slide? What is the shortest period before sliding starts? (Type the shortest period in s, to 3 significant figures.)

Uses: Centripetal and Centrifugal Forces, Forces Causing Centripetal Acceleration

Show hint

Needed force $mr\omega^2$ with $\omega = 2\pi/T$; the most friction can give is $\mu mg$.

Show solution
  1. $\omega = \dfrac{2\pi}{5.0} = 1.257\ \mathrm{rad\,s^{-1}}$; needed $F = 50 \times 2.0 \times 1.257^2 = 158\ \mathrm{N}$.
  2. Maximum friction $= 0.40 \times 50 \times 9.8 = 196\ \mathrm{N}$, more than $158\ \mathrm{N}$, so the child does not slide.
  3. Sliding starts when $mr\omega^2 = \mu mg \implies \omega = \sqrt{\mu g / r} = \sqrt{0.40 \times 9.8 / 2.0} = 1.40\ \mathrm{rad\,s^{-1}}$ (the mass cancels).
  4. $T_{\min} = \dfrac{2\pi}{\omega} \approx 4.49\ \mathrm{s}$. Turning faster than once every $4.49$ s, the child slides.

No (needs $158\ \mathrm{N}$, friction can give $196\ \mathrm{N}$); sliding begins if $T < 4.49\ \mathrm{s}$
