Renewable Energy Sources — Solar and Wind

Concept — Radiant Solar and Kinetic Wind Power

Solar and wind energies are the most rapidly expanding, abundant, and mature renewable technologies on Earth:

  • 1. Solar Energy: Radiant light and heat emitted continuously by nuclear fusion in the Sun's core:
    • Solar Constant: The extraterrestrial solar irradiance perpendicular to the sun's rays at the top of the atmosphere is approximately $I_{\text{sc}} \approx 1350\mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}$ ($429\mathrm{Btu\,h^{-1}\,ft^{-2}}$).
    • Insolation at Earth's Surface: Due to atmospheric scattering and cloud absorption (~54%), ground-level irradiance on a clear summer solar noon is approximately $1000\mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}$.
    • Solar Conversion Technologies:
      • Photovoltaic (PV) Cells: Semiconductor p-n junction diodes that convert photons directly into DC electrical energy via the photovoltaic effect. Photons with energy $E = h\nu \ge E_g$ excite valence electrons across the bandgap, creating electron-hole pairs that flow as current in an external circuit.
      • Solar Thermal Heating: Flat-plate collectors absorb sunlight using dark absorber plates bonded to insulated copper tubing, circulating solar-heated water into insulated storage tanks.
      • Concentrated Solar Power (CSP): Uses computer-controlled tracking mirrors (heliostats or parabolic troughs) to focus sunlight onto a central receiver, generating high-temperature steam that spins a turbine generator.
  • 2. Wind Energy: The kinetic energy of vast air masses set in motion by differential solar heating of Earth's atmosphere and planetary rotation:
    • Wind Turbine Operation: Moving air hits aerodynamic airfoil blades, producing aerodynamic lift that turns a low-speed rotor shaft. A gearbox accelerates the rotational speed into a high-speed generator, outputting AC electricity.
    • Cubic Wind Speed Dependence: Available wind power scales with the cube of wind speed ($P \propto v^3$). Doubling wind speed increases harvestable wind power by a factor of $2^3 = 8$.
Core Formulas — Solar and Wind Power Calculations
$$P_{\text{wind}} = \frac{1}{2}\rho A v^3 \qquad\qquad E_{\text{solar}} = I \cdot A \cdot t \qquad\qquad \lambda_{\max} = \frac{h c}{E_g}$$
  • $P_{\text{wind}}$ = total kinetic power of the incident wind stream ($\mathrm{W}$)
  • $\rho$ = air density ($\approx 1.2\text{ to }1.3\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$ at sea level)
  • $A = \pi r^2$ = swept rotor blade circle area ($\mathrm{m^2}$), where $r$ is blade length
  • $v$ = upstream wind velocity ($\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$)
  • $I$ = solar irradiance / insolation ($\mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}$ or $\mathrm{kWh\,m^{-2}\,day^{-1}}$)
  • $E_g$ = semiconductor bandgap energy ($\mathrm{J}$ or $\mathrm{eV}$, where $1\mathrm{eV} = 1.6 \times 10^{-19}\mathrm{J}$)
  • $h = 6.63 \times 10^{-34}\mathrm{J\,s}$ (Planck's constant); $c = 3.0 \times 10^8\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$
Derivation — Kinetic Power Harvested by a Wind Turbine

Consider a parcel of air of density $\rho$ moving at uniform speed $v$ through an imaginary disc of swept rotor area $A$ perpendicular to the wind stream:

  1. In time $t$, the wind advances a distance $\Delta x = v t$. The volume of air that passes through area $A$ is: $$V = A \Delta x = A v t$$
  2. The total mass of this air cylinder is: $$m = \rho V = \rho A v t$$
  3. The kinetic energy $E_k$ contained within this moving air mass is: $$E_k = \frac{1}{2} m v^2 = \frac{1}{2} (\rho A v t) v^2 = \frac{1}{2}\rho A t v^3$$
  4. Dividing kinetic energy by time $t$ gives the incident wind power $P$: $$P = \frac{E_k}{t} = \frac{1}{2}\rho A v^3$$

Textbook Worked Examples — Solar and Wind Energy Calculations

Example 4.2: A home requires $62\text{ kWh}$ of heat on a winter day to maintain a constant indoor temperature of $20^\circ\text{C}$. Assume that the temperature of hot water from the collector outlet is $60^\circ\text{C}$.
(i) How much collector surface area does it need for a solar heating system that has a $20\%$ efficiency?
(ii) How large does the storage tank have to be to store this much energy?
(The average solar radiation per day in winter is about $6.5\text{ kWh\,m}^{-2}$; specific heat of water $c = 4184\text{ J\,kg}^{-1}\text{K}^{-1}$).

Solution:
Given:
Daily heat demand $\Delta Q = 62\text{ kWh}$.
Solar insolation $= 6.5\text{ kWh\,m}^{-2}\,\text{day}^{-1}$; Efficiency $\eta = 20\% = 0.20$.

(i) Thermal energy captured per square meter per day: $$\text{Heat delivered per m}^2 = 6.5 \times 0.20 = 1.3\text{ kWh\,m}^{-2}$$ Required collector surface area: $$\text{Area} = \frac{\text{Total heat required}}{\text{Heat produced per m}^2} = \frac{62\text{ kWh}}{1.3\text{ kWh\,m}^{-2}} \approx 47.7\text{ m}^2$$ (ii) Temperature difference: $$\Delta T = T_{\text{hot}} - T_{\text{cold}} = 60^\circ\text{C} - 20^\circ\text{C} = 40^\circ\text{C} = 40\text{ K}$$ Convert $62\text{ kWh}$ to Joules ($1\text{ kWh} = 3.6 \times 10^6\text{ J}$): $$\Delta Q = 62 \times 3.6 \times 10^6\text{ J} = 2.232 \times 10^8\text{ J}$$ Using $\Delta Q = m c \Delta T$: $$m = \frac{\Delta Q}{c \Delta T} = \frac{2.232 \times 10^8\text{ J}}{(4184\text{ J\,kg}^{-1}\text{K}^{-1})(40\text{ K})} = \frac{2.232 \times 10^8}{167\,360} \approx 1333.6\text{ kg}$$ Since $1\text{ kg}$ of water has volume $1\text{ liter}$, the storage tank volume must be approximately $1334\text{ liters}$ (about $352.4\text{ gallons}$).

Example 4.3: Determine the input kinetic power of the wind if the wind speed is $20\text{ m\,s}^{-1}$ and the blade length is $20\text{ m}$. The density of air is $1.3\text{ kg\,m}^{-3}$.

Solution:
Given: $v = 20\text{ m\,s}^{-1}$, blade radius $r = 20\text{ m}$, $\rho = 1.3\text{ kg\,m}^{-3}$.
Swept rotor area: $$A = \pi r^2 = 3.142 \times (20)^2 = 3.142 \times 400 = 1256.8\text{ m}^2$$ Using the wind power equation: $$P = \frac{1}{2}\rho A v^3 = \frac{1}{2}(1.3)(1256.8)(20)^3 = 0.65 \times 1256.8 \times 8000$$ $$P \approx 6.535 \times 10^6\text{ W} = 6.535\text{ MW}$$

Reviewed Exercises:

1. The band gap of Gallium Arsenide (GaAs) is $1.4\text{ eV}$. Calculate the maximum wavelength of light that can induce photovoltaic generation in a GaAs solar cell.
Bandgap energy in Joules: $$E_g = 1.4\text{ eV} \times 1.6 \times 10^{-19}\text{ J\,eV}^{-1} = 2.24 \times 10^{-19}\text{ J}$$ Using $E_g = \frac{h c}{\lambda_{\max}}$: $$\lambda_{\max} = \frac{h c}{E_g} = \frac{(6.63 \times 10^{-34}\text{ J\,s})(3.0 \times 10^8\text{ m\,s}^{-1})}{2.24 \times 10^{-19}\text{ J}} \approx 8.88 \times 10^{-7}\text{ m} = 888\text{ nm}$$ Photons with wavelength $\lambda \le 888\text{ nm}$ (near-infrared and all visible light) possess sufficient energy to excite electrons into the conduction band.

2. A $100\text{ ft} \times 50\text{ ft}$ building has a flat roof. What is the average solar energy received by the roof in a month ($30\text{ days}$)? (The winter solar insolation is $1600\text{ Btu\,ft}^{-2}\,\text{day}^{-1}$).
Roof area $A = 100 \times 50 = 5000\text{ ft}^2$.
Total solar energy in 30 days: $$E = 5000\text{ ft}^2 \times 1600\text{ Btu\,ft}^{-2}\,\text{day}^{-1} \times 30\text{ days} = 2.4 \times 10^8\text{ Btu}$$
3. Calculate the solar energy received by a standard hot water collector of dimensions $1\text{ m} \times 2\text{ m}$ over one hour at noon, if irradiance stays constant at $800\text{ W\,m}^{-2}$.
Collector area $A = 1 \times 2 = 2\text{ m}^2$. Time $t = 1\text{ h} = 3600\text{ s}$.
$$E = I \cdot A \cdot t = 800\text{ W\,m}^{-2} \times 2\text{ m}^2 \times 3600\text{ s} = 5.76 \times 10^6\text{ J} = 5.76\text{ MJ} = 1.6\text{ kWh}$$

Key Takeaways
  • The solar constant is $\approx 1350\mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}$ at top of atmosphere and $\approx 1000\mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}$ at sea level.
  • Photovoltaic cells use p-n semiconductor junctions to convert absorbed photon energy directly into DC electricity.
  • Wind power incident on a rotor is $P = \frac{1}{2}\rho A v^3$ and scales with the cube of wind speed ($v^3$).
  • Solar thermal heating stores energy in hot water using flat-plate collectors governed by $\Delta Q = m c \Delta T$.
