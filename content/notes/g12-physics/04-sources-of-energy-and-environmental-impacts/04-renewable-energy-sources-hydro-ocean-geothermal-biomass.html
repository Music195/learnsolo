Renewable Energy Sources — Hydro, Ocean, Geothermal, and Biomass

Concept — Water, Earth, and Bio-Based Energy Systems

Beyond wind and direct sunlight, nature provides powerful continuous energy flows derived from gravity, oceanic motion, subterranean heat, and biological cycles:

  • 1. Hydroelectric Power (Hydropower): Harnesses the gravitational potential energy of water stored at an elevated head:
    • Impoundment Facility: A large reservoir dam stores river water at high elevation. Water descends under gravity through high-pressure steel conduits called penstocks to rotate hydraulic turbines coupled to electrical generators.
    • Diversion (Run-of-River) Facility: Diverts a portion of natural river flow through a canal or penstock along the river's natural gradient without requiring a massive storage dam or flooding upstream land.
    • Pumped Storage Hydropower (PSH): Functions as a colossal grid energy storage battery. During off-peak surplus periods, water is pumped from a lower basin to an upper reservoir. During peak electrical demand, water flows back down through reversible turbines to generate power.
  • 2. Energy from the Ocean:
    • Tidal Energy: Generated by the gravitational pull of the Moon and Sun causing diurnal high and low tides. Harnesses water level differences by erecting a tidal barrage across coastal estuaries equipped with bi-directional turbines.
    • Wave Energy: Captures the mechanical kinetic and potential energy of surface ocean waves generated by offshore wind shear using oscillating water columns, floating buoys, and hinged flap attenuators.
    • Ocean Thermal Energy Conversion (OTEC): Exploits the vertical temperature gradient ($\Delta T \ge 20^\circ\mathrm{C}$) between warm sunlit tropical surface waters ($\approx 25^\circ\mathrm{C}$) and ice-cold deep ocean water ($\approx 4^\circ\mathrm{C}$ pumped from $1\mathrm{km}$ depth). In a closed cycle, warm surface water evaporates a low-boiling-point working fluid (ammonia, boiling point $-33.3^\circ\mathrm{C}$) to drive a turbine, and deep cold seawater condenses the vapor back into liquid.
  • 3. Geothermal Energy: Taps into the Earth's primordial internal heat and radioactive decay from molten magma deep within the crust. Deep production wells (1–3 miles deep) extract superheated steam and brine to turn steam turbines for baseload power. Cooled water is reinjected deep into permeable rock formations to sustain the reservoir.
  • 4. Biomass Energy: Solar energy chemically stored in plant and animal organic matter through photosynthesis. Key conversion routes:
    • Direct Combustion: Burning firewood, agricultural residues (rice husks, bagasse), and pellets in industrial boilers.
    • Thermochemical (Pyrolysis & Gasification): High-temperature heating in pressurized gasifiers with controlled oxygen to produce syngas ($\mathrm{CO} + \mathrm{H_2}$) and bio-oil.
    • Chemical Transesterification: Reacting vegetable oils and animal fats with alcohol to produce renewable biodiesel.
    • Biological Digestion & Fermentation: Anaerobic digestion of sewage and animal manure to generate biogas ($\approx 60\%\mathrm{CH_4}$); fermentation of sugars to produce bioethanol.
Core Formulas — Hydroelectric Output Power
$$P = \eta \, \rho \, Q \, g \, h = \eta \, \left(\frac{m}{t}\right) \, g \, h$$
  • $P$ = useful electrical power output delivered by the plant ($\mathrm{W}$ or $\mathrm{MW}$)
  • $\eta$ = overall turbine-generator efficiency ($0 < \eta \le 1$, typically $0.60\text{ to }0.90$)
  • $\rho$ = density of water ($1000\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$)
  • $Q = \frac{V}{t}$ = volumetric discharge rate through penstocks ($\mathrm{m^3\,s^{-1}}$)
  • $\frac{m}{t} = \rho Q$ = mass flow rate of falling water ($\mathrm{kg\,s^{-1}}$)
  • $g = 9.8\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$ = acceleration due to gravity
  • $h$ = effective hydrostatic pressure head (vertical elevation fall, $\mathrm{m}$)
Interactive Demo — Hydroelectric Dam: $P = \eta\rho Q g h$

Where does a hydroelectric station's energy come from, and what makes one dam more powerful than another?

Change the head $h$ (how far the water falls), the flow $Q$ (cubic metres per second) and the turbine–generator efficiency $\eta$. Each kilogram of water gives up $gh$ joules of potential energy; the station turns a fraction $\eta$ of it into electricity.

Textbook Worked Examples — Hydropower and Ocean Systems

Example 4.4: At a hydroelectric power plant, the water pressure head is at a height of $300\text{ m}$ and the water flow available is $100\text{ m}^3\,\text{s}^{-1}$. If the turbine-generator efficiency is $60\%$, estimate the electric power available from the plant. Take $g = 9.8\text{ m\,s}^{-2}$ and $\rho = 1000\text{ kg\,m}^{-3}$.

Solution:
Given:
Head $h = 300\text{ m}$, volume flow rate $Q = \frac{V}{t} = 100\text{ m}^3\,\text{s}^{-1}$, efficiency $\eta = 60\% = 0.60$.
Using the hydropower formula: $$P = \eta \, \rho \, Q \, g \, h$$ $$P = 0.60 \times 1000\text{ kg\,m}^{-3} \times 100\text{ m}^3\,\text{s}^{-1} \times 9.8\text{ m\,s}^{-2} \times 300\text{ m}$$ $$P = 0.60 \times 980\,000 \times 300 = 176\,400\,000\text{ W} = 176.4\text{ MW}$$

Reviewed Exercises:

1. A hydroturbine receives water from a reservoir at an elevation of $100\text{ m}$ above it. What is the minimum water flow in $\text{kg\,s}^{-1}$ to produce a steady turbine output of $50\text{ MW}$ if the turbine-generator efficiency is $75\%$?
Given: $h = 100\text{ m}$, $P = 50\text{ MW} = 50 \times 10^6\text{ W}$, $\eta = 0.75$, $g = 9.8\text{ m\,s}^{-2}$.
$$P = \eta \left(\frac{m}{t}\right) g h \implies \frac{m}{t} = \frac{P}{\eta g h}$$ $$\frac{m}{t} = \frac{50 \times 10^6\text{ W}}{0.75 \times 9.8\text{ m\,s}^{-2} \times 100\text{ m}} = \frac{50 \times 10^6}{735} \approx 68\,027\text{ kg\,s}^{-1} \quad (68.03\text{ tonnes\,s}^{-1})$$
2. Why are wind and solar energies currently more widespread than ocean energy?
Ocean installations (tidal barrages, wave converters, OTEC) face extreme engineering challenges: highly corrosive saltwater environments, severe storm damage, high upfront civil construction capital costs, and biofouling by marine organisms. Wind and solar technologies have standardized mass-manufacturing supply chains, lower maintenance costs, and can be deployed inland near consumer demand.

3. What working fluid is typically utilized in closed-cycle OTEC systems?
Closed-cycle OTEC plants typically use pure ammonia ($\mathrm{NH_3}$) because of its low boiling point (approximately $-33.3^\circ\mathrm{C}$ at $1\mathrm{atm}$), allowing it to readily vaporize when heated by $25^\circ\mathrm{C}$ surface seawater.

Key Takeaways
  • Hydroelectric power delivers $P = \eta \rho Q g h$, scaling directly with hydrostatic head $h$ and discharge flow $Q$.
  • Pumped storage facilities store grid electricity during off-peak hours and discharge during peak demand.
  • Ocean energy includes tides (lunar gravitation), waves (surface wind shear), and OTEC (surface-to-deep temperature gradient).
  • Geothermal plants tap steam and pressurized hot water from tectonic magma reservoirs.
  • Biomass energy can be harnessed through direct combustion, gasification (syngas), transesterification (biodiesel), and anaerobic digestion (biogas).
