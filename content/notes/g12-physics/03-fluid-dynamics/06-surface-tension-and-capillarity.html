Surface Tension and Capillarity

Concept — Surface Energy and Intermolecular Forces

Liquids exhibit striking surface phenomena governed by the balance of intermolecular forces:

  • Surface Tension ($\gamma$): The tendency of a liquid surface to contract into the minimum possible surface area, behaving like a stretched elastic skin or membrane:
    • Molecular Mechanism: Liquid molecules in the bulk are surrounded symmetrically by neighboring molecules and experience balanced cohesive attractions in all directions. Surface molecules, having no liquid neighbors above, experience an unbalanced net inward pull toward the liquid bulk. Work must be done to bring molecules to the surface, creating surface potential energy.
    • Everyday Examples: Spherical shape of falling raindrops and soap bubbles (a sphere minimizes surface area for a given volume), water striders walking on water, steel needles and paperclips floating on water without sinking despite having a higher density.
  • Cohesion vs. Adhesion:
    • Cohesive Forces: Intermolecular attractions between molecules of the same substance (e.g. water-water attraction, mercury-mercury attraction).
    • Adhesive Forces: Intermolecular attractions between molecules of different substances (e.g. water-glass attraction).
  • Capillary Action (Capillarity): The spontaneous rise or depression of a liquid column inside a narrow-bore tube (capillary) or porous medium:
    • Wetting Liquids (e.g., Water on Glass): Adhesive force exceeds cohesive force ($\text{Adhesion} > \text{Cohesion}$). The liquid wets the glass, forms a concave meniscus with an acute contact angle ($\theta < 90^\circ$), and rises inside the capillary ($h > 0$).
    • Non-wetting Liquids (e.g., Mercury on Glass): Cohesive force exceeds adhesive force ($\text{Cohesion} > \text{Adhesion}$). The liquid does not wet the glass, forms a convex meniscus with an obtuse contact angle ($\theta > 90^\circ$), and is depressed below the outside level ($h < 0$).
Core Formulas — Surface Tension and Jurin's Law
$$\gamma = \frac{F}{l} \qquad\qquad h = \frac{2\gamma \cos\theta}{\rho g r}$$
  • $\gamma$ = surface tension of the liquid ($\mathrm{N\,m^{-1}}$)
  • $F$ = surface tension force acting along contact perimeter ($\mathrm{N}$)
  • $l$ = effective contact length ($\mathrm{m}$):
    • Single edge / perimeter (solid circular disc): $l = 2\pi r$
    • Two rims / surfaces (wire ring, hollow loop): $l = 2 \times (2\pi r) = 4\pi r$
    • Floating needle or wire of length $L$: $l = 2 L$ (two contact margins)
    • Soap film on a wire slider of length $L$: $l = 2 L$ (two liquid surfaces)
  • $h$ = capillary rise height ($\mathrm{m}$) — note that $h \propto \frac{1}{r}$ (narrower tubes produce higher rise)
  • $\theta$ = contact angle between liquid meniscus and solid tube wall
  • $\rho$ = liquid density ($\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$); $r$ = internal radius of capillary bore ($\mathrm{m}$)
Derivation — Height of Capillary Rise

Consider a narrow cylindrical glass tube of internal radius $r$ dipped vertically into a wetting liquid of density $\rho$ and surface tension $\gamma$ with contact angle $\theta$:

  1. The liquid-solid contact boundary forms a circle of perimeter $C = 2\pi r$. Along this boundary, surface tension pulls upward and inward along the tangent to the meniscus at angle $\theta$ to the vertical wall.
  2. The total upward vertical force supporting the elevated column is: $$F_{\text{upward}} = (\gamma \cdot 2\pi r) \cos\theta$$
  3. This upward surface tension force is held in equilibrium by the downward gravitational weight of the liquid cylinder of height $h$: $$W = m g = \rho V g = \rho (\pi r^2 h) g$$
  4. Equating upward and downward forces ($F_{\text{upward}} = W$): $$2\pi r \gamma \cos\theta = \pi r^2 \rho g h$$
  5. Cancelling $\pi r$ on both sides and solving for $h$: $$h = \frac{2\gamma \cos\theta}{\rho g r}$$

Textbook Worked Examples — Surface Tension and Capillarity

Example 3.11: A horizontal circular loop of wire has a diameter of $5\text{ cm}$ and is lowered into a sample of crude oil. The additional force required to pull the loop out of the oil is $0.04\text{ N}$. Calculate the surface tension of the crude oil.

Solution:
Diameter $d = 5\text{ cm} \implies$ radius $r = 2.5\text{ cm} = 2.5 \times 10^{-2}\text{ m}$.
Circumference $l = 2\pi r = 2 \times 3.142 \times 2.5 \times 10^{-2} \approx 0.16\text{ m}$.
Because a hollow wire ring pulls liquid from both its inside and outside rims, there are two contact rims ($L = 2l$): $$F = 2 \gamma l \implies \gamma = \frac{F}{2 l} = \frac{0.04\text{ N}}{2 \times 0.16\text{ m}} = 0.13\text{ N\,m}^{-1}$$

Example 3.12: Calculate the force required to pull a flat circular plate of radius $15\text{ cm}$ from the surface of a liquid with surface tension $0.053\text{ N\,m}^{-1}$.

Solution:
Radius $r = 15\text{ cm} = 15 \times 10^{-2}\text{ m}$, $\gamma = 0.053\text{ N\,m}^{-1}$.
Because the plate is solid flat, it has only one outer rim in contact with the surface: $$l = 2\pi r = 2 \times 3.142 \times 15 \times 10^{-2} = 94.26 \times 10^{-2}\text{ m} \approx 0.9426\text{ m}$$ $$F = \gamma l = 0.053\text{ N\,m}^{-1} \times 0.9426\text{ m} \approx 0.0499\text{ N} \approx 0.05\text{ N}$$

Example 3.13: A needle has a length of $3.2\text{ cm}$. When placed gently on the surface of water, the needle floats if it is not too heavy. What is the weight of the heaviest needle that can float? (Take surface tension of water $\gamma = 0.073\text{ N\,m}^{-1}$).

Solution:
Length $l = 3.2\text{ cm} = 3.2 \times 10^{-2}\text{ m}$, $\gamma = 0.073\text{ N\,m}^{-1}$.
The floating needle is supported along both sides of its length (2 edges in contact with the liquid surface): $$F = 2 \gamma l = 2 \times 0.073 \times 3.2 \times 10^{-2} \approx 4.67 \times 10^{-3}\text{ N}$$ Therefore, the maximum weight of the floating needle is $W_{\max} = 4.67 \times 10^{-3}\text{ N}$.

Example 3.14: When a capillary tube stands upright in a beaker of water, the water rises to height $h$. If the radius of the tube is $2\text{ mm}$, contact angle $\theta = 30^\circ$, density of water $\rho = 1000\text{ kg\,m}^{-3}$, and surface tension $\gamma = 0.072\text{ N\,m}^{-1}$, calculate $h$.

Solution:
Given: $r = 2\text{ mm} = 2 \times 10^{-3}\text{ m}$, $\theta = 30^\circ$, $\rho = 1000\text{ kg\,m}^{-3}$, $\gamma = 0.072\text{ N\,m}^{-1}$.
Using Jurin's capillary rise formula: $$h = \frac{2\gamma \cos\theta}{\rho g r} = \frac{2 \times 0.072 \times \cos 30^\circ}{1000 \times 9.8 \times 2 \times 10^{-3}} = \frac{0.144 \times 0.866}{19.6} \approx \frac{0.1247}{19.6} \approx 0.0064\text{ m} = 0.64\text{ cm}$$

Example 3.15: At a certain temperature, water has a surface tension of $0.072\text{ N\,m}^{-1}$. In a vertical tube of $3\text{ mm}$ diameter, the liquid rises $6\text{ mm}$ above the outside level. Calculate the contact angle $\theta$. Take $\rho = 1000\text{ kg\,m}^{-3}$ and $g = 9.8\text{ m\,s}^{-2}$.

Solution:
Given: $\gamma = 0.072\text{ N\,m}^{-1}$, $\rho = 1000\text{ kg\,m}^{-3}$, $h = 6\text{ mm} = 6 \times 10^{-3}\text{ m}$, diameter $d = 3\text{ mm} \implies r = 1.5 \times 10^{-3}\text{ m}$.
Rearranging Jurin's formula for $\cos\theta$: $$\cos\theta = \frac{h \rho g r}{2\gamma} = \frac{(6 \times 10^{-3}) \times 1000 \times 9.8 \times (1.5 \times 10^{-3})}{2 \times 0.072}$$ $$\cos\theta = \frac{0.0882}{0.144} = 0.6125 \implies \theta = \arccos(0.6125) \approx 52.23^\circ$$

Reviewed Exercises:

1. Water rises in a capillary tube, whereas mercury falls in the same tube. Explain.
For water in a glass tube, adhesive forces between water molecules and glass molecules exceed cohesive forces between water molecules ($\text{Adhesion} > \text{Cohesion}$). The meniscus curves concavely with an acute contact angle ($\theta < 90^\circ, \cos\theta > 0$), resulting in an upward net capillary force that lifts water.
For mercury in a glass tube, cohesive forces between mercury atoms far exceed adhesive forces with glass ($\text{Cohesion} > \text{Adhesion}$). The meniscus curves convexly with an obtuse contact angle ($\theta > 90^\circ, \cos\theta < 0$), resulting in a downward net force that depresses the mercury column.

2. A soap film is formed on a rectangular frame with a movable wire of length $7\text{ cm}$ dipping into soap solution. The frame hangs from one arm of a balance. An extra mass of $0.4\text{ g}$ must be placed in the opposite pan to balance the pull on the frame. Calculate the surface tension of the soap solution. (Take $g = 9.8\text{ m\,s}^{-2}$).
Wire length $l = 7\text{ cm} = 0.07\text{ m}$.
Balancing weight $F = m g = (0.4 \times 10^{-3}\text{ kg})(9.8\text{ m\,s}^{-2}) = 3.92 \times 10^{-3}\text{ N}$.
Because a soap film has two liquid-air surfaces (front and back), the effective contact length is $2l$: $$F = 2 \gamma l \implies \gamma = \frac{F}{2 l} = \frac{3.92 \times 10^{-3}\text{ N}}{2 \times 0.07\text{ m}} = \frac{3.92 \times 10^{-3}}{0.14} = 0.028\text{ N\,m}^{-1}$$

Key Takeaways
  • Surface tension $\gamma = \frac{F}{l}$ ($\mathrm{N\,m^{-1}}$) acts along liquid surfaces due to unbalanced inward cohesive attractions.
  • Liquids with $\text{Adhesion} > \text{Cohesion}$ wet glass, form an acute contact angle ($\theta < 90^\circ$), and rise in capillaries.
  • Liquids with $\text{Cohesion} > \text{Adhesion}$ do not wet glass, form an obtuse contact angle ($\theta > 90^\circ$), and are depressed in capillaries.
  • Capillary rise height follows Jurin's law: $h = \frac{2\gamma\cos\theta}{\rho g r}$, where elevation is inversely proportional to tube radius $r$.
  • Objects with two contact surfaces (rings, floating needles, soap films) exert force $F = 2\gamma l$.
