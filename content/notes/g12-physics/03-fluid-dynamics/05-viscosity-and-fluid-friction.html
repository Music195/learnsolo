Viscosity and Fluid Friction

Concept — Internal Friction in Moving Fluids

Real fluids offer resistance to relative motion between adjacent fluid layers. This internal friction is called viscosity:

  • Microscopic Origin: Arises from cohesive intermolecular forces and momentum transfer between adjacent shearing laminae.
  • Physical Meaning: Measures a fluid's internal "thickness" or stickiness. High-viscosity fluids (honey, castor oil, glycerol) resist shear deformation and flow slowly; low-viscosity fluids (water, air, alcohol) flow readily.
  • Intensive Property: Viscosity depends on the material composition and state of the fluid, not on the total volume or mass present.
  • Effect of Temperature:
    • Liquids: Viscosity decreases rapidly as temperature increases ($\eta \downarrow$ as $T \uparrow$), because thermal agitation weakens cohesive intermolecular bonds, allowing layers to slip more easily.
    • Gases: Viscosity increases as temperature increases ($\eta \uparrow$ as $T \uparrow$), because higher molecular thermal speeds heighten the collision rate and momentum exchange between shearing layers.
  • Dynamic vs. Kinematic Viscosity:
    • Dynamic Viscosity ($\eta$): Resistance to flow under an applied shearing force. SI unit: $\mathrm{Pa\,s} = \mathrm{N\,s\,m^{-2}}$ (also called the poiseuille, $\mathrm{Pl}$). In the CGS system, $1\mathrm{poise} = 0.1\mathrm{Pa\,s}$, or $1\mathrm{Pa\,s} = 10\mathrm{poise}$.
    • Kinematic Viscosity ($\nu$): The ratio of dynamic viscosity to density: $\nu = \frac{\eta}{\rho}$ ($\mathrm{m^2\,s^{-1}}$), which describes flow under gravitational forces.
Core Formulas — Newton's Law of Viscosity and Stokes' Law
$$\tau = \frac{F}{A} = -\eta \frac{dv}{dy} \qquad\qquad F_{\text{drag}} = 6\pi \eta r v$$
  • $\tau = \frac{F}{A}$ = shear stress ($\mathrm{N\,m^{-2}}$ or $\mathrm{Pa}$)
  • $\frac{dv}{dy}$ = velocity gradient perpendicular to the flow direction ($\mathrm{s^{-1}}$)
  • $\eta$ = dynamic coefficient of viscosity ($\mathrm{Pa\,s}$ or $\mathrm{N\,s\,m^{-2}}$)
  • $F_{\text{drag}}$ = viscous retarding drag force on a sphere of radius $r$ moving at speed $v$ (Stokes' Law)
  • Terminal Velocity ($v_t$) of a Falling Sphere: $$v_t = \frac{2 g r^2 (\rho_1 - \rho_2)}{9\eta} \qquad\iff\qquad \eta = \frac{2 g r^2 (\rho_1 - \rho_2)}{9 v_t}$$ where $\rho_1$ is sphere density and $\rho_2$ is fluid density.
Derivation — Terminal Velocity of a Falling Sphere

Consider a small sphere of radius $r$ and density $\rho_1$ released from rest in a viscous liquid of density $\rho_2$ and viscosity $\eta$ under gravity:

  1. Three forces act on the falling sphere:
    • Downward gravitational weight: $W = m g = \rho_1 V g = \frac{4}{3}\pi r^3 \rho_1 g$
    • Upward Archimedes buoyant upthrust: $R = \rho_2 V g = \frac{4}{3}\pi r^3 \rho_2 g$
    • Upward viscous drag force: $F = 6\pi \eta r v$ (Stokes' law)
  2. As the sphere accelerates downward, its velocity increases, which in turn increases the drag force $F$. Eventually, upward forces exactly balance the downward weight: $$W = R + F$$ $$\frac{4}{3}\pi r^3 \rho_1 g = \frac{4}{3}\pi r^3 \rho_2 g + 6\pi \eta r v_t$$
  3. Rearranging for the viscous drag force: $$6\pi \eta r v_t = \frac{4}{3}\pi r^3 (\rho_1 - \rho_2) g$$
  4. Solving for the terminal steady velocity $v_t$: $$v_t = \frac{4\pi r^3 (\rho_1 - \rho_2) g}{3 \times 6\pi \eta r} = \frac{2 g r^2 (\rho_1 - \rho_2)}{9\eta}$$

Textbook Worked Examples — Viscosity and Stokes Drag

Example 3.9: A metal plate of area $2.5 \times 10^{-4}\text{ m}^2$ is placed on a $0.25 \times 10^{-3}\text{ m}$ thick layer of castor oil. If a force of $50\text{ N}$ is needed to move the plate with a velocity of $3 \times 10^{-2}\text{ m\,s}^{-1}$, calculate the coefficient of viscosity of castor oil.

Solution:
Given:
Area $A = 2.5 \times 10^{-4}\text{ m}^2$
Film thickness $\Delta y = 0.25 \times 10^{-3}\text{ m}$
Plate speed $\Delta v = 3 \times 10^{-2}\text{ m\,s}^{-1}$
Applied force $F = 50\text{ N}$

1. Calculate velocity gradient: $$\frac{\Delta v}{\Delta y} = \frac{3 \times 10^{-2}\text{ m\,s}^{-1}}{0.25 \times 10^{-3}\text{ m}} = 120\text{ s}^{-1}$$ 2. Shear stress: $$\tau = \frac{F}{A} = \frac{50\text{ N}}{2.5 \times 10^{-4}\text{ m}^2} = 200\,000\text{ N\,m}^{-2} = 2 \times 10^5\text{ Pa}$$ 3. Coefficient of dynamic viscosity: $$\eta = \frac{F / A}{\Delta v / \Delta y} = \frac{200\,000}{120} \approx 1.67 \times 10^3\text{ Pa\,s}$$

Example 3.10: Determine the radius of a raindrop falling through air with terminal velocity $9\text{ m\,s}^{-1}$. The viscosity of air is $1.8 \times 10^{-5}\text{ Pa\,s}$, density of water is $\rho_1 = 1000\text{ kg\,m}^{-3}$, and density of air is $\rho_2 = 1.21\text{ kg\,m}^{-3}$. Take $g = 9.8\text{ m\,s}^{-2}$.

Solution:
Given:
$v_t = 9\text{ m\,s}^{-1}$, $\eta = 1.8 \times 10^{-5}\text{ Pa\,s}$, $\rho_1 = 1000\text{ kg\,m}^{-3}$, $\rho_2 = 1.21\text{ kg\,m}^{-3}$.

From the terminal velocity equation: $$v_t = \frac{2 g r^2 (\rho_1 - \rho_2)}{9\eta} \implies r^2 = \frac{9 v_t \eta}{2 g (\rho_1 - \rho_2)}$$ $$r^2 = \frac{9 \times 9 \times 1.8 \times 10^{-5}}{2 \times 9.8 \times (1000 - 1.21)} = \frac{1.458 \times 10^{-3}}{19.6 \times 998.79} = \frac{1.458 \times 10^{-3}}{19\,576.28} \approx 7.448 \times 10^{-8}\text{ m}^2$$ Taking the square root: $$r = \sqrt{7.448 \times 10^{-8}} \approx 2.73 \times 10^{-4}\text{ m} = 0.273\text{ mm}$$

Reviewed Exercises:

1. On which factors does the viscosity of a liquid depend?
Viscosity depends on:

  • Nature of the liquid: Stronger cohesive intermolecular forces (e.g. hydrogen bonds in glycerol) produce higher viscosity.
  • Temperature: Viscosity decreases rapidly as temperature increases, because thermal energy overcomes intermolecular attraction.
  • Pressure: For most liquids, viscosity increases slightly under extreme pressures as molecules are pushed closer together.

2. How does temperature affect the viscosity of a fluid?
  • For liquids: An increase in temperature reduces viscosity markedly ($\eta$ decreases), making liquids flow more readily.
  • For gases: An increase in temperature increases viscosity ($\eta$ increases), because higher thermal speeds accelerate intermolecular collisions and momentum exchange between moving gas layers.

Key Takeaways
  • Viscosity is fluid friction arising from shearing between adjacent flowing layers.
  • Dynamic viscosity $\eta = \frac{\tau}{dv/dy}$ has SI unit $\mathrm{Pa\,s} = \mathrm{N\,s\,m^{-2}}$ ($1\mathrm{Pa\,s} = 10\mathrm{poise}$).
  • Heating lowers liquid viscosity but raises gas viscosity.
  • Stokes' law gives drag on a sphere as $F = 6\pi\eta r v$.
  • Terminal velocity occurs when downward weight is balanced by buoyancy and Stokes drag: $v_t = \frac{2gr^2(\rho_1 - \rho_2)}{9\eta}$.
