Equation of Continuity

Concept — Conservation of Mass in Fluid Flow

When an incompressible fluid flows steadily through a closed pipe or channel with varying cross-sectional area, mass can neither be created nor destroyed. The mass of fluid entering one end per unit time must equal the mass leaving the other end:

  • Volume Flow Rate ($Q$ or $\frac{V}{t}$): The volume of fluid passing through a given cross-section per second: $$\frac{V}{t} = A v \quad (\mathrm{m^3\,s^{-1}})$$ where $A$ is the cross-sectional area and $v$ is the average flow speed perpendicular to $A$.
  • Mass Flow Rate ($\frac{m}{t}$): The mass of fluid passing per second: $$\frac{m}{t} = \rho \frac{V}{t} = \rho A v \quad (\mathrm{kg\,s^{-1}})$$
  • Inverse Relation between Area and Velocity: Because volume flow rate is constant throughout the pipe:
    • Where the pipe is narrow (small $A$), the fluid must speed up (large $v$).
    • Where the pipe is wide (large $A$), the fluid slows down (small $v$).
    This explains why pinching a garden hose produces a fast jet of water, or why rivers rush swiftly through narrow gorges (defiles) and flow placidly through wide valleys.
Core Formulas — Continuity Relations
$$A_1 v_1 = A_2 v_2 \qquad\iff\qquad A v = \text{constant}$$
  • $A_1, A_2$ = cross-sectional areas at sections 1 and 2 ($\mathrm{m^2}$ or $\mathrm{cm^2}$)
  • $v_1, v_2$ = fluid speeds at sections 1 and 2 ($\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ or $\mathrm{cm\,s^{-1}}$)
  • For circular pipes of diameter $d$ or radius $r$: $A = \pi r^2 = \frac{\pi}{4} d^2$, hence: $$\frac{v_2}{v_1} = \frac{A_1}{A_2} = \left(\frac{r_1}{r_2}\right)^2 = \left(\frac{d_1}{d_2}\right)^2$$
  • When a single feeder pipe branches into $N$ identical smaller pipes / openings (e.g. capillaries or shower heads): $$A_1 v_1 = N A_2 v_2 = N \pi r_2^2 v_2$$
Derivation — Equation of Continuity

Consider an ideal, incompressible fluid of density $\rho$ flowing steadily through a pipe of changing cross-sectional area:

  1. In a small time interval $\Delta t$, the fluid at section 1 moves a distance $\Delta x_1 = v_1 \Delta t$. The volume of fluid that crosses section 1 is: $$\Delta V_1 = A_1 \Delta x_1 = A_1 v_1 \Delta t$$ The mass entering section 1 is $m_1 = \rho \Delta V_1 = \rho A_1 v_1 \Delta t$.
  2. During the same time interval $\Delta t$, an equal mass must exit through section 2. The fluid travels $\Delta x_2 = v_2 \Delta t$, occupying volume: $$\Delta V_2 = A_2 \Delta x_2 = A_2 v_2 \Delta t$$ The mass leaving section 2 is $m_2 = \rho \Delta V_2 = \rho A_2 v_2 \Delta t$.
  3. By the conservation of mass ($m_1 = m_2$): $$\rho A_1 v_1 \Delta t = \rho A_2 v_2 \Delta t$$
  4. Dividing both sides by $\rho \Delta t$ (since $\rho$ is constant for an incompressible fluid): $$A_1 v_1 = A_2 v_2 \qquad\text{or}\qquad \frac{V}{t} = A v = \text{constant}$$

Textbook Worked Examples — Continuity Applications

Example 3.1: A gardener uses a water pipe of $2.5\text{ cm}$ diameter. It takes $1\text{ min}$ to fill a $30\text{ litre}$ bucket. What is the initial speed of water coming out of the hose? Then a nozzle with an opening $0.5\text{ cm}^2$ is attached to the hose. Find the water speed coming out from the nozzle.

Solution:
Given:
Diameter $d = 2.5\text{ cm} \implies$ radius $r = 1.25\text{ cm}$.
Pipe cross-sectional area: $$A_{\text{pipe}} = \pi r^2 = \pi (1.25)^2 \approx 4.91\text{ cm}^2$$ Volume filled in $1\text{ min}$ ($60\text{ s}$): $$V = 30\text{ L} = 30 \times 1000\text{ cm}^3 = 30\,000\text{ cm}^3$$ Volume flow rate: $$\frac{V}{t} = \frac{30\,000\text{ cm}^3}{60\text{ s}} = 500\text{ cm}^3\,\text{s}^{-1} \quad\text{(constant throughout)}$$ 1. Initial speed from the hose: $$\frac{V}{t} = A_{\text{pipe}} v_{\text{pipe}} \implies v_{\text{pipe}} = \frac{V / t}{A_{\text{pipe}}} = \frac{500}{4.91} \approx 101.83\text{ cm\,s}^{-1} \approx 1.02\text{ m\,s}^{-1}$$ 2. When nozzle of area $A_{\text{nozzle}} = 0.5\text{ cm}^2$ is attached: $$A_{\text{pipe}} v_{\text{pipe}} = A_{\text{nozzle}} v_{\text{nozzle}}$$ $$4.91 \times 101.83 = 0.5 \times v_{\text{nozzle}} \implies v_{\text{nozzle}} = \frac{500}{0.5} = 1000\text{ cm\,s}^{-1} = 10\text{ m\,s}^{-1}$$

Example 3.2: What area must a heating duct have if air moving at $3\text{ m\,s}^{-1}$ along it can replenish the air every $15\text{ min}$ in a room of volume $300\text{ m}^3$? Assume the density of air remains constant.

Solution:
Let duct cross-sectional area be $A$.
Volume $V = 300\text{ m}^3$, time $t = 15\text{ min} = 15 \times 60 = 900\text{ s}$, speed $v = 3\text{ m\,s}^{-1}$.
Using the volume flow rate formula: $$\frac{V}{t} = A v \implies \frac{300}{900} = A \times 3 \implies \frac{1}{3} = 3 A \implies A = \frac{1}{9} \approx 0.11\text{ m}^2$$

Example 3.3: Water runs into a fountain, filling all the pipes, at a steady rate of $0.75\text{ m}^3\,\text{s}^{-1}$.
(i) How fast will it shoot out of a hole $4.5\text{ cm}$ in diameter?
(ii) At what speed will it shoot out if the diameter of the hole is three times as large?

Solution:
Volume flow rate $\frac{V}{t} = 0.75\text{ m}^3\,\text{s}^{-1}$.
(i) Radius $r_1 = \frac{4.5\text{ cm}}{2} = 2.25\text{ cm} = 0.0225\text{ m}$.
$$\frac{V}{t} = A_1 v_1 = \pi r_1^2 v_1 \implies 0.75 = 3.142 \times (0.0225)^2 \times v_1$$ $$0.75 = 3.142 \times 0.00050625 \times v_1 = 0.001591 \times v_1 \implies v_1 \approx 471.51\text{ m\,s}^{-1}$$ (ii) When diameter is 3 times as large ($d_2 = 3 d_1$), area is $3^2 = 9$ times as large ($A_2 = 9 A_1$): $$A_1 v_1 = A_2 v_2 \implies v_2 = \frac{A_1 v_1}{A_2} = \frac{v_1}{9} = \frac{471.51}{9} \approx 52.39\text{ m\,s}^{-1}$$

Example 3.4: A water tank of dimension $3\text{ m} \times 3\text{ m} \times 3\text{ m}$ has its base $10\text{ m}$ above the ground. It takes $1\text{ h}$ to fill.
(i) Find the power output of the pump to fill the tank.
(ii) Find the flow rate through the pipe and the speed of the water flow in the pipe which has a $5\text{ cm}$ radius.

Solution:
Volume of tank $V = 3 \times 3 \times 3 = 27\text{ m}^3$.
Mass of water $m = \rho V = 1000\text{ kg\,m}^{-3} \times 27\text{ m}^3 = 27\,000\text{ kg}$.
Height lifted to top of tank $h = 10\text{ m} + 3\text{ m} = 13\text{ m}$.
Time $t = 1\text{ h} = 3600\text{ s}$.
(i) Power output of pump: $$P = \frac{W}{t} = \frac{m g h}{t} = \frac{27\,000 \times 9.8 \times 13}{3600} = 955.5\text{ W}$$ (ii) Volume flow rate: $$\text{Flow rate} = \frac{V}{t} = \frac{27}{3600} = 7.5 \times 10^{-3}\text{ m}^3\,\text{s}^{-1}$$ Radius $r = 5\text{ cm} = 5 \times 10^{-2}\text{ m}$. Area $A = \pi r^2 = \pi (5 \times 10^{-2})^2 = 25\pi \times 10^{-4}\text{ m}^2 \approx 7.854 \times 10^{-3}\text{ m}^2$.
Using $\frac{V}{t} = A v$: $$7.5 \times 10^{-3} = \pi (5 \times 10^{-2})^2 v \implies v = \frac{7.5 \times 10^{-3}}{7.854 \times 10^{-3}} \approx 0.96\text{ m\,s}^{-1}$$

Reviewed Exercises:

1. Why does a river flow faster in passing a defile (narrow passage)?
By the equation of continuity, $A_1 v_1 = A_2 v_2 \implies v \propto \frac{1}{A}$. In a defile, the cross-sectional area $A$ of the river channel narrows significantly. Because the volume flow rate of water is conserved, the water velocity must increase to pass the same volume through the constricted cross-section.

2. A shower head has 20 circular openings, each with radius $1\text{ mm}$. The shower head is connected to a pipe with radius $0.8\text{ cm}$. If the speed of water in the pipe is $3\text{ m\,s}^{-1}$, what is its speed as it exits the shower-head openings?
Let pipe radius $r_1 = 0.8\text{ cm} = 8\text{ mm}$, speed $v_1 = 3\text{ m\,s}^{-1}$.
Pipe area $A_1 = \pi r_1^2 = \pi (8)^2 = 64\pi\text{ mm}^2$.
Total area of 20 openings each of radius $r_2 = 1\text{ mm}$:
$A_2 = 20 \times \pi r_2^2 = 20 \times \pi (1)^2 = 20\pi\text{ mm}^2$.
Applying continuity $A_1 v_1 = A_2 v_2$: $$64\pi \times 3 = 20\pi \times v_2 \implies v_2 = \frac{64 \times 3}{20} = \frac{192}{20} = 9.6\text{ m\,s}^{-1}$$

Key Takeaways
  • The equation of continuity $A_1 v_1 = A_2 v_2$ expresses the conservation of mass for an incompressible fluid.
  • Volume flow rate $\frac{V}{t} = A v$ is constant at every cross section along a closed streamline tube.
  • Flow velocity is inversely proportional to cross-sectional area: when a conduit narrows, flow speed increases proportionally.
  • For multiple identical outlets $N$, the relation becomes $A_1 v_1 = N A_2 v_2$.
