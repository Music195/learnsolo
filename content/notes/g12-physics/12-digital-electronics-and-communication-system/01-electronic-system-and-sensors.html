Electronic Systems and Sensor Components

Concept — The Three-Stage System Model and Solid-State Sensors

An electronic system is an engineered assembly of electrical and semiconductor components that processes electrical signals to perform monitoring, control, or communication functions. Every electronic system follows the universal three-stage block model:

  • Input Transducer (Sensor): Converts non-electrical physical quantities (light, temperature, sound, pressure) into proportional electrical voltage or current signals.
  • Processor / Controller: Amplifies, filters, compares, or performs digital logic operations on the input signal (e.g., operational amplifiers, microprocessors, logic gate arrays).
  • Output Transducer (Actuator): Converts processed electrical signals back into useful physical effects (sound from a loudspeaker, light from an LED/display, or motion from a motor).

Essential Semiconductor and Optoelectronic Devices

  • 1. Zener Diode: A heavily doped silicon p-n junction diode engineered to operate safely in the reverse-breakdown region:
    • In forward bias, it acts like a normal diode (threshold $\approx 0.7\mathrm{\,V}$).
    • In reverse bias, once the applied voltage reaches the calibrated Zener breakdown voltage ($V_Z$), the diode conducts reverse current ($I_Z$) while maintaining a remarkably constant potential drop across its terminals.
    • Application: Used as a shunt voltage regulator to maintain stable supply voltage across varying DC loads: $$V_{\text{load}} = V_Z \qquad\qquad I_S = \frac{V_{\text{in}} - V_Z}{R_S} = I_Z + I_{\text{load}}$$
  • 2. Light Emitting Diode (LED): A heavily doped, direct-bandgap compound semiconductor diode (e.g., $\mathrm{GaAs}$, $\mathrm{GaP}$, $\mathrm{GaN}$) operated in forward bias ($V_F \approx 1.8\text{--}3.3\mathrm{\,V}$, $I_F \approx 5\text{--}20\mathrm{\,mA}$):
    • Injected conduction electrons recombine with valence holes across the forbidden energy gap $E_g$, directly emitting photons with wavelength $\lambda = \frac{hc}{E_g}$.
    • Advantages: Low drive voltage, low power consumption, instantaneous response, high shock resistance, and operating lifetime $> 50{,}000\mathrm{\,hours}$.
  • 3. Photodiode: A semiconductor p-n junction designed with a transparent optical window operated in reverse bias:
    • Incident photons possessing energy $h\nu \ge E_g$ excite electron-hole pairs within the wide depletion layer. The internal junction electric field separates these carriers, producing a reverse photocurrent directly proportional to incident light intensity ($I_{\text{photo}} \propto \text{Illuminance}$).
    • Used in optical fiber communications, optical disc readers, and light meters.
  • 4. Light Dependent Resistor (LDR / Photoresistor): A non-junction photoconductive device made of cadmium sulphide ($\mathrm{CdS}$):
    • In Darkness: Very few free charge carriers; resistance is high ($R_{\text{dark}} \sim 1\text{--}10\mathrm{\,M}\Omega$).
    • In Bright Light: Photons excite electrons into the conduction band; resistance drops precipitously to $R_{\text{light}} \sim 100\text{--}1000\,\Omega$.
    • Used in potential divider circuits to automatically switch street lights on at dusk.
  • 5. Thermistor (NTC): A thermally sensitive resistor composed of sintered metal oxides exhibiting a Negative Temperature Coefficient (NTC) of resistance:
    • As temperature rises, thermal agitation frees abundant charge carriers, causing electrical resistance to decrease exponentially.
    • Used in digital clinical thermometers, fire detection sensors, and electronic thermostats.

Conceptual Analysis — Automatic Lighting Controller
Chapter 12 Exercise 1 (Textbook Page 220):
Which electrical component is used to control the automatic brightness of street lamps?
(i) LED   (ii) LDR   (iii) Zener Diode   (iv) Thermistor
Explain the working mechanism of the circuit.
Answer: (ii) LDR (Light Dependent Resistor). Working Mechanism:
  • The LDR is wired in series with a fixed resistor $R$ across a regulated DC power supply to form a potential divider.
  • During Daylight: Bright ambient light strikes the cadmium sulphide track of the LDR, freeing charge carriers and dropping its resistance to a low value ($R_{\text{LDR}} \sim \text{hundreds of }\Omega$). The voltage drop across the LDR falls below the turn-on threshold of a transistor or relay switch, keeping the street lamp turned OFF.
  • At Nightfall (Dusk): Darkness causes the LDR resistance to climb dramatically into the megohm range ($R_{\text{LDR}} \sim 1\text{--}10\mathrm{\,M}\Omega$). The potential difference across the LDR rises above the trigger threshold, energizing the switching transistor/relay and automatically illuminating the street lamp without human intervention.
Key Takeaways
  • Electronic systems follow the Input Sensor → Processor → Output Transducer model.
  • Zener diode: Operates in reverse breakdown to deliver a constant regulated output voltage $V_Z$.
  • LDR: Resistance falls in bright light; controls automatic street lighting circuits.
  • Thermistor: Negative temperature coefficient; resistance drops with temperature for thermostatic alarms.
