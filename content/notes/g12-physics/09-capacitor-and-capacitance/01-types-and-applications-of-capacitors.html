Types and Applications of Capacitors

Concept — Capacitive Energy Storage and Dielectric Classes

A capacitor (originally known as a condenser) is a passive electronic circuit component designed to store electrical potential energy in the electrostatic field established between two separated conductors:

  • Fundamental Construction: Two conducting plates or electrodes separated by a thin insulating medium called a dielectric (such as air, plastic film, ceramic, paper, mica, or an oxide layer).
  • Primary Classification:
    • Film Capacitors: Employ thin plastic films (e.g., polyester, polypropylene) as the dielectric. Non-polarized, working voltages up to $1500\mathrm{\,V}$, excellent tolerances ($10\%$ down to $0.01\%$), and high insulation resistance.
    • Ceramic Capacitors: Utilize ceramic disc or multilayer dielectric materials. Non-polarized, ideal for high-frequency applications from audio frequencies ($\mathrm{AF}$) to radio frequencies ($\mathrm{RF}$) due to low parasitic inductance and minimal dielectric loss.
    • Electrolytic Capacitors: Utilize an ultra-thin microscopic metal oxide film (aluminum or tantalum oxide) formed electrochemically on etched foil. They provide very high capacitance per unit volume. Polarized with distinct positive ($+$) and negative ($-$) terminals. Reversed polarity or overvoltage causes rapid dielectric breakdown, gas generation, and hazardous rupture/fire. Used for power supply filtering, ripple smoothing, and camera flash units.
    • Variable Capacitors: Feature interleaved sets of fixed stator plates and rotary rotor plates with air dielectric. Mechanical rotation alters the effective overlapping plate area, tuning the resonance frequency in radio and antenna RF circuits.
    • Supercapacitors (Ultracapacitors): Utilize electrostatic double-layer capacitance (EDLC) with nanostructured activated carbon electrodes, achieving hundreds or thousands of Farads. They bridge the gap between electrolytic capacitors and rechargeable chemical batteries: storing $10\text{--}100\times$ more energy than electrolytics, delivering charge and surge current rapidly, and enduring hundreds of thousands of charge-discharge cycles. Used in regenerative braking (buses, trains, cranes) and SRAM memory backup.
  • Circuit Functions:
    • DC Blocking & AC Coupling: Once charged to supply voltage, a capacitor presents infinite impedance to steady DC ($\omega = 0$), blocking it completely, while allowing time-varying alternating currents (AC) to pass freely through displacement current.
    • Power Supply Filtering: Smooths pulsating DC voltage output from diode rectifiers into steady DC.
    • Tuning & Resonant Circuits: Sets specific resonant frequencies $f_0 = \frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{LC}}$ in communication filters.

Conceptual Exercise — Supercapacitor vs. Battery
Comparative Analysis:
Why are supercapacitors preferred over chemical rechargeable lithium-ion batteries in regenerative braking systems of electric trains and city buses?
Key Factors:
  • Power Density & Fast Charge Rate: Regenerative braking generates massive electrical power surges over a few seconds. Batteries rely on slow chemical redox intercalation and cannot accept such huge charging currents without overheating. Supercapacitors store energy physically via electrostatic double layers and can absorb high currents instantaneously.
  • Cycle Life: Batteries degrade chemically after $1{,}000\text{--}3{,}000$ charge-discharge cycles. Supercapacitors withstand over $500{,}000$ to $1{,}000{,}000$ cycles with negligible loss of capacity, matching the mechanical lifespan of transit vehicles.
  • Efficiency: Electrostatic charge separation exhibits round-trip energy efficiency exceeding $95\%$, compared to $75\text{--}85\%$ for electrochemical batteries.
Key Takeaways
  • A capacitor stores electrical energy in the electric field between separated conductors.
  • Non-polarized types: Ceramic and film capacitors; can be connected in any orientation in AC or DC circuits.
  • Polarized types: Electrolytic capacitors (aluminum/tantalum) offer high capacitance in small volume; must strictly adhere to $(+)$ and $(-)$ terminal polarity.
  • Supercapacitors: Provide rapid charge/discharge and ultra-long cycle lifetimes for regenerative braking and memory backup.
