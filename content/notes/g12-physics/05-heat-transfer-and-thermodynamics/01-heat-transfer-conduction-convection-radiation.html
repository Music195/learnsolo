Heat Transfer — Conduction, Convection, and Radiation

Concept — The Three Mechanisms of Thermal Energy Transfer

Heat is thermal energy in transit between physical bodies as a direct consequence of a temperature difference ($T_1 \ne T_2$). There are three fundamental mechanisms by which heat is transferred:

  • 1. Heat Conduction (in Solids): Thermal energy is transferred internally through microscopic collisions of adjacent atoms/molecules and the migration of mobile free conduction electrons, without macroscopic translation of the material itself:
    • Thermal Conductors vs. Insulators: Metals (silver, copper, aluminum) contain high concentrations of free electrons that transport heat rapidly ($k$ is large). Insulators (wood, rubber, glass, air, styrofoam) have tightly bound electrons and exhibit low thermal conductivity ($k$ is small).
    • Fourier's Law: The rate of conductive heat flow $H_{\text{cond}}$ through a cross-sectional area $A$ is directly proportional to $A$ and the temperature gradient $\frac{\Delta T}{l}$.
  • 2. Heat Convection (in Fluids): Heat is transferred through bulk physical movement of a liquid or gas. When a fluid is heated, it expands, its density decreases, and buoyant forces cause the warmer fluid to rise while cooler, denser fluid sinks to take its place (forming convection currents):
    • Natural (Free) Convection: Driven solely by buoyant density differences (e.g. sea breezes, home radiators, atmospheric cloud formation).
    • Forced Convection: Fluid circulation is actively driven by mechanical blowers, fans, or coolant pumps (e.g. computer CPU fans, automobile car radiators).
  • 3. Heat Radiation (Electromagnetic Waves): Transfer of energy via electromagnetic waves (predominantly infrared). Radiation requires no material medium and travels across empty space at the speed of light ($c \approx 3 \times 10^8\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$), which is how solar energy reaches Earth:
    • Blackbody: An idealized physical body that absorbs $100\%$ of all incident electromagnetic radiation regardless of wavelength or angle of incidence, and acts as the most efficient emitter of thermal radiation at any given temperature ($e = 1$).
    • Emissivity ($e$): The ratio of total radiant energy emitted by a real surface to that emitted by a blackbody at identical temperature ($0 < e \le 1$). Dark, matte surfaces have $e \approx 1$ (good absorbers and emitters); polished, shiny, reflective surfaces have $e \ll 1$ (poor absorbers and emitters).
Core Formulas — Governing Equations of Heat Transfer
$$H_{\text{cond}} = k A \frac{T_2 - T_1}{l} \qquad H_{\text{conv}} = q A (T_s - T_f) \qquad H_{\text{rad}} = e \sigma A T^4$$
  • $H = \frac{\Delta Q}{\Delta t}$ = rate of heat transfer or heat current ($\mathrm{W} = \mathrm{J\,s^{-1}}$)
  • $k$ = thermal conductivity of material ($\mathrm{W\,m^{-1}\,K^{-1}}$)
  • $A$ = surface area perpendicular to heat flow ($\mathrm{m^2}$)
  • $l$ = slab thickness ($\mathrm{m}$); $\frac{T_2 - T_1}{l}$ = spatial temperature gradient ($\mathrm{K\,m^{-1}}$)
  • $q$ = convective heat transfer coefficient ($\mathrm{W\,m^{-2}\,K^{-1}}$)
  • $T_s, T_f$ = temperatures of surface and surrounding fluid ($\mathrm{K}$)
  • $e$ = surface emissivity ($0 < e \le 1$; dimensionless; $e = 1$ for a blackbody)
  • $\sigma = 5.685 \times 10^{-8}\mathrm{W\,m^{-2}\,K^{-4}}$ = Stefan-Boltzmann constant
  • Net Radiative Exchange: $$H_{\text{net}} = e \sigma A \left(T^4 - T_{\text{surr}}^4\right)$$
Interactive Demo — Conduction, Convection and Radiation

Heat always flows from hot to cold, but by three different routes. What carries the energy in each one?

Conduction: atoms at the hot end vibrate harder and pass energy along to their neighbours; nothing moves along the rod. Convection: the warm fluid itself moves, carrying energy with it. Radiation: electromagnetic waves carry energy, even through empty space; hotter bodies radiate much more ($\propto T^4$).

Textbook Worked Examples — Conduction, Convection, and Radiation

Example 5.1: A person walking at a regular speed generates heat at the rate of $0.07\text{ W}$. If the surface area of the body is $1.5\text{ m}^2$ and heat is generated $0.03\text{ m}$ below the skin, what should be the temperature difference between the skin and the interior of the body if heat is to be conducted to the skin surface? Take $k = 5 \times 10^{-3}\text{ W\,m}^{-1}\text{K}^{-1}$.

Solution:
Given:
$H_{\text{cond}} = 0.07\text{ W}$, $A = 1.5\text{ m}^2$, $l = 0.03\text{ m}$, $k = 5 \times 10^{-3}\text{ W\,m}^{-1}\text{K}^{-1}$.
Using Fourier's conduction formula: $$H_{\text{cond}} = k A \frac{\Delta T}{l} \implies \Delta T = \frac{H_{\text{cond}} \cdot l}{k A}$$ $$\Delta T = \frac{0.07 \times 0.03}{(5 \times 10^{-3}) \times 1.5} = \frac{0.0021}{0.0075} = 0.28\text{ K} = 0.28^\circ\text{C}$$ Physiological Note: Conduction through biological tissue alone is very slow; the human body relies primarily on convective heat transport via warm blood circulation to regulate core body temperature.

Example 5.2: A new computer microchip with a surface area of $1\text{ cm}^2$ generates $10\text{ W}$ of heat. Determine the convective heat transfer coefficient $q$ required to keep the chip's temperature within $20^\circ\text{C}$ above ambient room temperature. Can this chip be cooled with natural air convection (where $q_{\text{air}} \approx 2.5\text{ to }25\text{ W\,m}^{-2}\text{K}^{-1}$), or will it require forced convection?

Solution:
Given:
$A = 1\text{ cm}^2 = 1 \times 10^{-4}\text{ m}^2$, $H_{\text{conv}} = -10\text{ W}$ (heat lost by chip), $\Delta T = T_s - T_f = 20\text{ K}$.
Using Newton's law of cooling: $$H_{\text{conv}} = q A (T_s - T_f) \implies q = \frac{H_{\text{conv}}}{A \Delta T} = \frac{10\text{ W}}{(1 \times 10^{-4}\text{ m}^2)(20\text{ K})} = \frac{10}{0.002} = 5000\text{ W\,m}^{-2}\text{K}^{-1}$$ Because $5000\text{ W\,m}^{-2}\text{K}^{-1}$ is vastly greater than the maximum coefficient for still air ($25\text{ W\,m}^{-2}\text{K}^{-1}$), the chip cannot be cooled by natural air convection and strictly requires forced convection (e.g. high-speed fans with heat sinks or liquid coolant circulation).

Example 5.3: In a room, the temperature of water in an electric kettle is $80^\circ\text{C}$ while room temperature is $30^\circ\text{C}$. The surface area of the kettle is $0.02\text{ m}^2$ and its emissivity is $e = 0.9$. Find the net rate of heat loss due to radiation. ($\sigma = 5.685 \times 10^{-8}\text{ W\,m}^{-2}\text{K}^{-4}$).

Solution:
Absolute temperatures:
$T_{\text{kettle}} = 80 + 273 = 353\text{ K}$
$T_{\text{room}} = 30 + 273 = 303\text{ K}$
1. Rate of heat radiation emitted by kettle: $$H_{\text{out}} = e \sigma A T_2^4 = 0.9 \times (5.685 \times 10^{-8}) \times 0.02 \times (353)^4 \approx 15.89\text{ W}$$ 2. Rate of heat radiation absorbed from room: $$H_{\text{in}} = e \sigma A T_1^4 = 0.9 \times (5.685 \times 10^{-8}) \times 0.02 \times (303)^4 \approx 8.63\text{ W}$$ 3. Net rate of radiative heat loss: $$H_{\text{net}} = H_{\text{out}} - H_{\text{in}} = 15.89\text{ W} - 8.63\text{ W} = 7.26\text{ W}$$

Reviewed Exercises:

1. Why is ceramic an ideal choice for electric stove cooktops?
Ceramic has moderate thermal conductivity: high enough to conduct heat directly upward from hidden heating elements into the bottom of a cooking pot, but low enough laterally to prevent heat from spreading dangerously across the entire countertop surface.

2. What is the relation between total emissive power of an object and of a blackbody at the same temperature?
The total emissive power $E$ of a real body is equal to the product of its emissivity $e$ and the emissive power $E_b$ of a blackbody at that temperature: $E = e E_b$, where $0 < e \le 1$.

Key Takeaways
  • Conduction transfers heat in solids via lattice vibrations and free electrons: $H_{\text{cond}} = k A \frac{\Delta T}{l}$.
  • Convection transfers heat in liquids and gases through fluid movement driven by buoyant density changes: $H_{\text{conv}} = q A \Delta T$.
  • Radiation transfers heat as electromagnetic waves across vacuum governed by Stefan-Boltzmann's law: $H = e \sigma A T^4$.
  • A blackbody is an ideal emitter and absorber with emissivity $e = 1$.
  • Net radiative transfer between a body and surroundings is $H_{\text{net}} = e \sigma A (T^4 - T_{\text{surr}}^4)$.
