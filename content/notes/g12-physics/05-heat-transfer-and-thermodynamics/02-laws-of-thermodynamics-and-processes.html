Laws of Thermodynamics and Thermodynamic Processes

Concept — Energy, Work, and Thermodynamic States

Thermodynamics analyzes energy transformations involving heat, work, and changes in the macroscopic state of matter:

  • The Zeroth Law of Thermodynamics (Concept of Temperature): If two physical systems $A$ and $B$ are each separately in thermal equilibrium with a third system $C$, then $A$ and $B$ are in thermal equilibrium with each other. This establishes temperature as the universal state property governing thermal equilibrium.
  • Internal Energy ($U$): The total microscopic kinetic and potential energy stored within all the constituent molecules of a system. For an ideal monatomic gas, intermolecular potential energy is zero, and internal energy depends solely on absolute temperature $T$: $$U = \frac{3}{2} n R T$$
  • The First Law of Thermodynamics: A formal statement of the law of conservation of energy applied to thermodynamic systems: $$\Delta U = Q - W$$
    • $Q > 0$ when heat is absorbed into the system; $Q < 0$ when heat is rejected.
    • $W > 0$ when work is done by the system (gas expands); $W < 0$ when work is done on the system (gas is compressed).
    • Important distinction: Heat ($Q$) and work ($W$) are energy in transit across boundaries; neither is "stored" as heat or work. Internal energy ($U$) is a state function stored inside the matter.
Core Formulas — The Four Fundamental Thermodynamic Processes
Process Constant Condition Work Done $W$ First Law Relation
Isobaric $p = \text{const}$ $W = p (V_f - V_i)$ $Q = \Delta U + p \Delta V = n C_p \Delta T$
Isochoric (Isometric) $V = \text{const}$ $W = 0$ $\Delta U = Q = n C_v \Delta T = \frac{3}{2} n R \Delta T$
Isothermal $T = \text{const}$ ($\Delta U = 0$) $W = n R T \ln\left(\frac{V_f}{V_i}\right)$ $Q = W$
Adiabatic $Q = 0$ ($p V^\gamma = \text{const}$) $W = -\Delta U = \frac{p_i V_i - p_f V_f}{\gamma - 1}$ $\Delta U = -W$
  • $n$ = number of moles; $R = 8.314\mathrm{J\,mol^{-1}\,K^{-1}}$ (universal gas constant)
  • $C_v = \frac{3}{2}R$ and $C_p = \frac{5}{2}R$ for ideal monatomic gases ($\gamma = \frac{C_p}{C_v} = \frac{5}{3} \approx 1.67$)

Textbook Worked Examples — First Law Calculations

Example 5.4: Find the change in internal energy of one mole of an ideal monatomic gas when its temperature changes from $0^\circ\text{C}$ to $100^\circ\text{C}$. Take $R = 8.3143\text{ J\,mol}^{-1}\text{K}^{-1}$.

Solution:
Given: $n = 1\text{ mol}$, $\Delta T = 100^\circ\text{C} - 0^\circ\text{C} = 100\text{ K}$, $R = 8.3143\text{ J\,mol}^{-1}\text{K}^{-1}$.
Using the ideal gas internal energy relation: $$\Delta U = \frac{3}{2} n R \Delta T$$ $$\Delta U = \frac{3}{2} (1\text{ mol})(8.3143\text{ J\,mol}^{-1}\text{K}^{-1})(100\text{ K}) = 1.5 \times 831.43 \approx 1.25 \times 10^3\text{ J}$$

Example 5.5: One gram of liquid water ($1\text{ cm}^3$) expands into $1671\text{ cm}^3$ of steam when boiled at atmospheric pressure ($1\text{ atm} = 1.013 \times 10^5\text{ Pa}$). The latent heat of vaporization is $L_v = 2265\text{ J\,g}^{-1}$. Compute: (i) the work done by the water, and (ii) the increase in internal energy.

Solution:
Given:
$m = 1\text{ g}$, $p = 1.013 \times 10^5\text{ Pa}$, $L_v = 2265\text{ J\,g}^{-1}$.
Change in volume: $$\Delta V = V_{\text{steam}} - V_{\text{water}} = 1671\text{ cm}^3 - 1\text{ cm}^3 = 1670\text{ cm}^3 = 1670 \times 10^{-6}\text{ m}^3$$ 1. Work done during isobaric phase change: $$W = p \Delta V = (1.013 \times 10^5\text{ Pa})(1670 \times 10^{-6}\text{ m}^3) \approx 169\text{ J}$$ 2. Heat absorbed to vaporize $1\text{ g}$: $$Q = m L_v = 1\text{ g} \times 2265\text{ J\,g}^{-1} = 2265\text{ J}$$ 3. Increase in internal energy: $$\Delta U = Q - W = 2265\text{ J} - 169\text{ J} = 2096\text{ J}$$ Interpretation: Of the $2265\text{ J}$ of heat supplied, $169\text{ J}$ is expended pushing back the atmosphere, while the remaining $2096\text{ J}$ overcomes intermolecular hydrogen bonds to separate liquid water into steam.

Example 5.6: In an isobaric process, a volume of gas expands by absorbing heat at a constant pressure of $2\text{ atm}$. If the volume increment is $0.5\text{ m}^3$, find the work done by the gas.

Solution:
Pressure $p = 2\text{ atm} = 2 \times 1.013 \times 10^5\text{ Pa} = 2.026 \times 10^5\text{ Pa}$.
$\Delta V = 0.5\text{ m}^3$.
$$W = p \Delta V = (2.026 \times 10^5\text{ Pa})(0.5\text{ m}^3) = 1.013 \times 10^5\text{ J}$$

Reviewed Exercises:

1. How do heat transfer and internal energy differ?
Heat transfer ($Q$) is energy in transit across a boundary due solely to a temperature difference; it ceases once thermal equilibrium is reached. Internal energy ($U$) is a state property representing the total microscopic kinetic and potential energy stored within the constituent atoms of a system.

2. When heat transfers into a system, is the energy stored as heat? Explain.
No. Once thermal energy crosses a system's boundary, it converts into the kinetic energy of random molecular motions and intermolecular potential energy, manifesting as an increase in internal energy ($\Delta U$), not as "stored heat."

3. Explain why the temperature of a gas increases when it is adiabatically compressed.
In an adiabatic compression, no heat can leave the system ($Q = 0$). By the first law ($\Delta U = Q - W = -W$), the external work done on the gas is positive (work done by the gas is negative, $W < 0$). Consequently, $\Delta U = -(-|W|) = +|W| > 0$. Because the internal energy of a gas is directly proportional to its temperature ($U \propto T$), this increase in internal energy raises the gas temperature.

Key Takeaways
  • Zeroth Law establishes thermal equilibrium and defines temperature.
  • First Law states conservation of energy: $\Delta U = Q - W$.
  • Internal energy of an ideal gas depends solely on temperature: $U = \frac{3}{2}nRT$.
  • Isobaric: $p = \text{const}$, $W = p\Delta V$.
  • Isochoric: $V = \text{const}$, $W = 0 \implies \Delta U = Q$.
  • Isothermal: $T = \text{const}$, $\Delta U = 0 \implies Q = W = nRT\ln(V_f/V_i)$.
  • Adiabatic: $Q = 0 \implies \Delta U = -W$; compression heats, expansion cools.
