Heat Engines and the Carnot Cycle

Concept — Converting Thermal Energy into Mechanical Work

A heat engine is a cyclic thermodynamic device that absorbs thermal energy from a high-temperature source, converts a portion of it into mechanical work, and discharges the residual waste heat to a low-temperature sink:

  • Cyclic Operation: Over any completed thermodynamic cycle, the working substance returns to its initial state, meaning the net change in internal energy is zero ($\Delta U = 0$). By the first law: $$W_{\text{net}} = Q_H - Q_C$$
  • Internal Combustion Engines:
    • Four-Stroke Gasoline Engine (Otto Cycle): Employs a spark plug to ignite a compressed gasoline-air mixture. Operates through six steps involving four piston strokes: (1) Intake stroke, (2) Compression stroke, (3) Constant-volume combustion explosion, (4) Power expansion stroke, (5) Valve exhaust release, and (6) Exhaust scavenging stroke.
    • Diesel Engine (Compression Ignition): Draws only pure air during the intake stroke and compresses it to extremely high pressure and temperature ($> 500^\circ\mathrm{C}$). Fine diesel fuel sprayed by an injector ignites spontaneously on contact without requiring spark plugs, achieving higher compression ratios and superior thermal efficiency.
  • The Carnot Heat Engine: Conceived by Sadi Carnot in 1824, an idealized hypothetical engine operating on an entirely reversible thermodynamic cycle between two constant-temperature heat reservoirs:
    • Carnot's Theorem: No engine operating between two given temperatures can be more efficient than a Carnot engine operating between the same reservoirs. All reversible engines operating between identical heat reservoirs possess the identical maximum efficiency.
    • Real engines suffer irreversibilities from friction, turbulence, and conduction across finite temperature gradients, so their actual efficiency is always substantially lower than $\eta_{\text{Carnot}}$.
Core Formulas — Thermal and Carnot Efficiencies
$$\eta_{\text{actual}} = \frac{W}{Q_H} = \frac{Q_H - Q_C}{Q_H} = 1 - \frac{Q_C}{Q_H} \qquad\qquad \eta_{\text{Carnot}} = 1 - \frac{T_C}{T_H}$$
  • $\eta$ = thermal efficiency ($0 \le \eta < 1$, or expressed as a percentage)
  • $W$ = net mechanical work output delivered per cycle ($\mathrm{J}$)
  • $Q_H$ = heat absorbed from the high-temperature reservoir per cycle ($\mathrm{J}$)
  • $Q_C$ = waste heat discharged into the low-temperature sink per cycle ($\mathrm{J}$)
  • $T_H, T_C$ = absolute temperatures of hot and cold reservoirs in Kelvin ($T(\mathrm{K}) = T(^\circ\mathrm{C}) + 273$)
  • Otto Cycle Theoretical Efficiency: $$\eta_{\text{Otto}} = 1 - \frac{1}{r_c^{\gamma - 1}}$$ where $r_c = \frac{V_{\max}}{V_{\min}}$ is the compression ratio and $\gamma = \frac{C_p}{C_v}$ ($\approx 1.4$ for air).
Derivation — The Four Stages of the Carnot Cycle

The Carnot cycle consists of four sequential reversible processes executed by an ideal gas working substance:

  1. Step 1: Reversible Isothermal Expansion ($A \to B$ at $T_H$): The gas absorbs heat $Q_H$ from the hot reservoir at constant temperature $T_H$. The gas expands from $V_A$ to $V_B$, performing positive work: $$Q_H = W_{AB} = n R T_H \ln\left(\frac{V_B}{V_A}\right)$$
  2. Step 2: Reversible Adiabatic Expansion ($B \to C$ from $T_H \to T_C$): The cylinder is thermally insulated ($Q = 0$). The gas expands further from $V_B$ to $V_C$, cooling from $T_H$ to $T_C$ while doing work at the expense of its internal energy: $$T_H V_B^{\gamma - 1} = T_C V_C^{\gamma - 1}$$
  3. Step 3: Reversible Isothermal Compression ($C \to D$ at $T_C$): The gas is in thermal contact with the cold reservoir at $T_C$. External work compresses the gas from $V_C$ to $V_D$, rejecting heat $Q_C$: $$Q_C = -W_{CD} = n R T_C \ln\left(\frac{V_C}{V_D}\right)$$
  4. Step 4: Reversible Adiabatic Compression ($D \to A$ from $T_C \to T_H$): Thermally insulated ($Q = 0$). External work compresses the gas from $V_D$ to $V_A$, raising its temperature back from $T_C$ to $T_H$: $$T_C V_D^{\gamma - 1} = T_H V_A^{\gamma - 1}$$
  5. Dividing the two adiabatic relations gives $\frac{V_B}{V_A} = \frac{V_C}{V_D}$, which proves: $$\frac{Q_C}{Q_H} = \frac{T_C}{T_H} \implies \eta_{\text{Carnot}} = 1 - \frac{T_C}{T_H}$$

Textbook Worked Examples — Heat Engine and Carnot Efficiency

Example 5.7: An engine takes in $9220\text{ J}$ of heat from a hot reservoir and performs $1750\text{ J}$ of mechanical work per cycle while operating between temperatures of $689^\circ\text{C}$ and $397^\circ\text{C}$.
(a) What is its actual thermal efficiency?
(b) What is its maximum theoretical efficiency?

Solution:
Given:
$Q_H = 9220\text{ J}$, $W = 1750\text{ J}$.
Reservoir temperatures in Kelvin:
$T_H = 689^\circ\text{C} + 273 = 962\text{ K}$
$T_C = 397^\circ\text{C} + 273 = 670\text{ K}$

(a) Actual efficiency: $$\eta_{\text{actual}} = \frac{W}{Q_H} = \frac{1750\text{ J}}{9220\text{ J}} \approx 0.1898 \approx 19.0\%$$ (b) Maximum theoretical (Carnot) efficiency: $$\eta_{\text{Carnot}} = 1 - \frac{T_C}{T_H} = 1 - \frac{670\text{ K}}{962\text{ K}} = 1 - 0.6965 = 0.3035 \approx 30.4\%$$

Chapter Exercises — Heat Engines:

Exercise 22: An automobile engine has an efficiency of $20\%$ and delivers an average of $23\,000\text{ J}$ of mechanical work per second during operation. (i) How much heat input is required per second? (ii) How much waste heat is discharged to the atmosphere per second?
Efficiency $\eta = 20\% = 0.20$, work rate $W = 23\,000\text{ J\,s}^{-1} = 23\text{ kW}$.
(i) Heat input per second: $$Q_H = \frac{W}{\eta} = \frac{23\,000\text{ J}}{0.20} = 115\,000\text{ J\,s}^{-1} = 115\text{ kW}$$ (ii) Waste heat discharged per second: $$Q_C = Q_H - W = 115\,000\text{ J} - 23\,000\text{ J} = 92\,000\text{ J\,s}^{-1} = 92\text{ kW}$$
Reviewed Exercise: Is a temperature difference necessary to operate a heat engine?
Yes. By the second law of thermodynamics (Kelvin-Planck statement), it is impossible for any cyclic engine to absorb heat from a single thermal reservoir and convert $100\%$ of it into mechanical work without discharging waste heat to a colder reservoir. If $T_H = T_C$, then $\eta_{\text{Carnot}} = 1 - 1 = 0$, and no net work can be produced.

Key Takeaways
  • A heat engine extracts heat $Q_H$ from $T_H$, performs work $W$, and exhausts waste heat $Q_C$ to $T_C$ with efficiency $\eta = W/Q_H$.
  • Over a completed cycle, internal energy change is zero ($\Delta U = 0$).
  • Four-stroke gasoline engines follow the Otto cycle with spark ignition; diesel engines use compression ignition without spark plugs.
  • The Carnot cycle consists of four reversible steps: 2 isothermal and 2 adiabatic processes.
  • Carnot efficiency $\eta_C = 1 - T_C/T_H$ provides the upper theoretical limit for any heat engine operating between $T_H$ and $T_C$.
