Nuclear Energy and Nuclear Reactors

Concept — Induced Fission, Chain Reactions, and Reactor Engineering

Nuclear reactions release millions of times more energy per unit mass than conventional chemical combustion through the conversion of nuclear binding energy into kinetic energy:

  • Induced Nuclear Fission: Discovered in 1938 by Otto Hahn and Fritz Strassmann, nuclear fission is the splitting of a heavy, unstable atomic nucleus into two intermediate-mass daughter nuclei when struck by a slow (thermal) neutron: $$_{92}^{235}\text{U} + {_0^1}\text{n} \longrightarrow {_{92}^{236}\text{U}^*} \longrightarrow {_{56}^{141}}\text{Ba} + {_{36}^{92}}\text{Kr} + 3\,{_0^1}\text{n} + Q$$
    • Energy Release ($Q \approx 200\mathrm{\,MeV}$ per event): The total mass of the fission products and emitted neutrons is less than the mass of the initial reactants. This mass defect $\Delta m$ is converted into kinetic energy and gamma rays according to Einstein's mass-energy equivalence ($E = \Delta m\,c^2$).
  • The Nuclear Chain Reaction: Each fission of a Uranium-235 nucleus releases an average of $2.5$ fast neutrons. If at least one of these secondary neutrons induces fission in a neighboring nucleus, a self-sustaining chain reaction occurs:
    • Subcritical ($k < 1$): Rate of neutron loss exceeds production; reaction dies out.
    • Critical ($k = 1.0$): Fission rate is perfectly steady; power output is constant (normal operating state of a nuclear power reactor).
    • Supercritical ($k > 1$): Exponential runaway neutron multiplication in microseconds, leading to uncontrollable thermal explosion (atomic bomb).
  • Essential Subsystems of a Thermal Nuclear Reactor:
    1. Fissionable Fuel Elements: Uranium dioxide ($\mathrm{UO_2}$) pellets enriched to $3\text{--}5\%\,^{235}\mathrm{U}$ (or Plutonium-239) hermetically sealed within zirconium alloy (zircaloy) fuel rods.
    2. Moderator: Slows down fast fission neutrons ($\sim 2\mathrm{\,MeV}$) through elastic collisions into thermal neutrons ($\sim 0.025\mathrm{\,eV}$), where the probability of causing fission in $^{235}\mathrm{U}$ is hundreds of times higher. Materials: Heavy water ($\mathrm{D_2O}$), high-purity graphite, or light water ($\mathrm{H_2O}$).
    3. Control Rods: Neutron-absorbing rods composed of cadmium or boron. Inserting control rods absorbs neutrons and slows the reaction; fully inserting them executes an emergency shutdown (SCRAM).
    4. Coolant: High-pressure liquid water, liquid sodium, or pressurized carbon dioxide gas circulating through the core to extract thermal energy, transferring it via heat exchangers to a secondary water loop that drives steam turbines.
    5. Radiation Shielding: A heavy steel reactor pressure vessel enclosed within a multi-meter thick biological shield of dense baryte concrete prevents ionizing radiation from leaking into the environment.
  • Environmental Impacts & Waste Management:
    • Clean Baseload Generation: Nuclear power emits zero carbon dioxide ($\mathrm{CO_2}$), sulphur dioxide, or greenhouse gases during operation, possessing extraordinary fuel energy density (fission of $1\mathrm{\,kg}$ of $^{235}\mathrm{U}$ yields energy equivalent to burning $2{,}000{,}000\mathrm{\,kg}$ of coal).
    • Nuclear Waste: High-level radioactive waste (spent fuel rods and long-lived transuranic actinides) requires secure storage in deep water cooling pools followed by vitrification in borosilicate glass and permanent interment in deep geological repositories ($> 500\mathrm{\,m}$ underground in geologically stable bedrock).
Core Formulas — Fission Energy and Mass Defect
$$Q = \Delta m \cdot c^2 = [m_{\text{reactants}} - m_{\text{products}}] \cdot c^2 \qquad\qquad 1\mathrm{\,u} = 931.5\mathrm{\,MeV} \qquad\qquad Q_{\text{total}} = N_{\text{fission}} \times 200\mathrm{\,MeV}$$
  • $Q$ = total energy liberated per fission reaction ($\mathrm{J}$ or $\mathrm{MeV}$)
  • $\Delta m$ = mass defect between reactants and products ($\mathrm{kg}$ or atomic mass units $\mathrm{u}$)
  • $c = 3.0 \times 10^8\mathrm{\,m/s}$ = speed of light
  • $k$ = effective neutron multiplication factor ($k = 1.0$ for critical steady state)

Worked Problem — Fission Energy and Coal Equivalency
Energy Density Comparison:
The complete fission of one nucleus of $^{235}\mathrm{U}$ releases $200\mathrm{\,MeV}$ of usable energy. Calculate the total thermal energy in Joules released by the complete fission of $1.0\mathrm{\,kg}$ of pure $^{235}\mathrm{U}$, and determine how many metric tons of bituminous coal ($3.0 \times 10^7\mathrm{\,J/kg}$) must be combusted to produce the identical energy.
Given Data:
  • Molar mass of $^{235}\mathrm{U} = 235\mathrm{\,g/mol} = 0.235\mathrm{\,kg/mol}$
  • Avogadro's number $N_A = 6.022 \times 10^{23}\mathrm{\,atoms/mol}$
  • $1\mathrm{\,eV} = 1.602 \times 10^{-19}\mathrm{\,J} \implies 200\mathrm{\,MeV} = 2.0 \times 10^8 \times 1.602 \times 10^{-19}\mathrm{\,J} = 3.204 \times 10^{-11}\mathrm{\,J}$
Step 1: Number of nuclei in $1.0\mathrm{\,kg}$ of $^{235}\mathrm{U}$: $$N = \frac{1.0\mathrm{\,kg}}{0.235\mathrm{\,kg/mol}} \times (6.022 \times 10^{23}\mathrm{\,mol^{-1}}) \approx 2.563 \times 10^{24}\mathrm{\,nuclei}$$ Step 2: Total energy released $Q_{\text{total}}$: $$Q_{\text{total}} = N \times (3.204 \times 10^{-11}\mathrm{\,J}) = (2.563 \times 10^{24}) \times (3.204 \times 10^{-11}\mathrm{\,J}) \approx 8.21 \times 10^{13}\mathrm{\,J}$$ Step 3: Equivalent mass of coal: $$M_{\text{coal}} = \frac{8.21 \times 10^{13}\mathrm{\,J}}{3.0 \times 10^7\mathrm{\,J/kg}} \approx 2.74 \times 10^6\mathrm{\,kg} = 2{,}740\mathrm{\,metric\ tons}$$ Conclusion:
Fissioning just $1\mathrm{\,kg}$ of Uranium-235 liberates $8.2 \times 10^{13}\mathrm{\,J}$, equivalent to burning $2{,}740\mathrm{\,tons}$ of coal.
Key Takeaways
  • Nuclear fission: Heavy nucleus absorbs a thermal neutron and splits, releasing $\sim 200\mathrm{\,MeV}$ and 2–3 neutrons.
  • Moderator: Slows fast neutrons down to thermal energies (heavy water, graphite, water).
  • Control rods: Boron or cadmium absorb excess neutrons to maintain critical equilibrium ($k=1$).
  • High energy density with zero direct $\mathrm{CO_2}$ emissions; necessitates deep geological waste disposal.
