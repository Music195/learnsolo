Radioactivity and Radioactive Decay

Concept — Spontaneous Nuclear Transmutation and Decay Kinetics

In 1896, Henri Becquerel discovered that uranium compounds spontaneously emit penetrating radiation. Subsequent research by Marie and Pierre Curie isolated polonium and radium, establishing the phenomenon of radioactivity:

  • Definition: Radioactivity is the spontaneous, uncontrolled nuclear disintegration of unstable atomic nuclei (predominantly heavy nuclides with atomic number $Z > 83$) accompanied by the emission of ionizing particles and electromagnetic rays.
  • Modes of Radioactive Decay:
    1. Alpha ($\alpha$) Decay: The unstable parent nucleus ejects an alpha particle — a tightly bound helium-4 nucleus ($_{2}^{4}\text{He}$ consisting of 2 protons and 2 neutrons): $$_{Z}^{A}X \longrightarrow {_{Z-2}^{A-4}}Y + {_{2}^{4}}\text{He}$$ Example: $_{92}^{238}\text{U} \longrightarrow {_{90}^{234}}\text{Th} + {_{2}^{4}}\text{He}$. Mass number decreases by 4; atomic number decreases by 2.
    2. Beta-Minus ($\beta^-$) Decay: Occurs in neutron-rich nuclei when an excess neutron converts into a proton, emitting a fast electron ($\beta^-$) and an electron antineutrino ($\bar{\nu}_e$): $$_{Z}^{A}X \longrightarrow {_{Z+1}^{A}}Y + {_{-1}^{0}}e^- + \bar{\nu}_e$$ Example: $_{6}^{14}\text{C} \longrightarrow {_{7}^{14}}\text{N} + {_{-1}^{0}}e^- + \bar{\nu}_e$. Mass number $A$ remains constant; atomic number $Z$ increases by 1.
    3. Beta-Plus ($\beta^+$) Decay: Occurs in proton-rich nuclei when a proton converts into a neutron, emitting a positron ($\beta^+$) and an electron neutrino ($\nu_e$): $$_{Z}^{A}X \longrightarrow {_{Z-1}^{A}}Y + {_{+1}^{0}}e^+ + \nu_e$$ Example: $_{6}^{11}\text{C} \longrightarrow {_{5}^{11}}\text{B} + {_{+1}^{0}}e^+ + \nu_e$.
    4. Gamma ($\gamma$) Decay: A daughter nucleus left in an excited metastable nuclear state releases excess excitation energy as high-energy electromagnetic gamma photons without changing its proton or mass number: $$_{Z}^{A}X^* \longrightarrow {_{Z}^{A}}X + \gamma$$
  • Radiation Properties Comparison:
    Radiation Identity & Charge Ionizing Power Penetrating Power & Shielding
    Alpha ($\alpha$) Helium nucleus ($_{2}^{4}\text{He}^{2+}$), $+2e$ Highest ($\sim 10^5\text{ pairs/mm}$) Lowest: Stopped by a sheet of paper or $5\mathrm{\,cm}$ of air
    Beta ($\beta$) Fast electron/positron, $\mp 1e$ Moderate ($\sim 10^2\text{ pairs/mm}$) Moderate: Stopped by a few millimeters of aluminum
    Gamma ($\gamma$) High-energy photon ($h\nu$), $0$ Lowest ($\sim 1\text{ pair/mm}$) Highest: Attenuated only by thick lead or dense concrete
  • Half-Life ($T_{1/2}$) and Activity ($A$): Radioactive decay is a purely stochastic Poisson process governed by the exponential decay law: $$N(t) = N_0 e^{-\lambda t} = N_0 \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{t / T_{1/2}} \qquad\qquad \lambda = \frac{\ln 2}{T_{1/2}} \approx \frac{0.693}{T_{1/2}}$$ Activity ($A = \lambda N$): The instantaneous rate of nuclear decays per second, measured in Becquerels ($1\mathrm{\,Bq} = 1\mathrm{\,decay/s}$) or Curies ($1\mathrm{\,Ci} = 3.7 \times 10^{10}\mathrm{\,Bq}$).
  • Practical Applications:
    • Medical Tracers: Technetium-99m ($T_{1/2} = 6\mathrm{\,h}$) and Iodine-131 ($T_{1/2} = 8\mathrm{\,days}$) enable non-invasive imaging while decaying rapidly to limit patient exposure.
    • Radiotherapy: Cobalt-60 ($T_{1/2} = 5.25\mathrm{\,years}$) gamma radiation destroys malignant cancer cells.
    • Radiometric Dating: Carbon-14 ($T_{1/2} = 5730\mathrm{\,years}$) measures the post-mortem decay in organic remains; Potassium-40 ($T_{1/2} = 1.3 \times 10^9\mathrm{\,years}$) dates ancient rocks.
Core Formulas — Radioactive Decay Kinetics
$$N(t) = N_0 \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^n \qquad\qquad n = \frac{t}{T_{1/2}} \qquad\qquad \lambda = \frac{0.693}{T_{1/2}} \qquad\qquad A(t) = A_0 \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^n$$
  • $N(t)$ = remaining quantity of undecayed parent nuclei after time $t$
  • $N_0$ = initial number of parent nuclei at $t = 0$
  • $n = t / T_{1/2}$ = elapsed number of half-lives
  • $T_{1/2}$ = characteristic half-life of the radioisotope ($\mathrm{s}$, $\mathrm{min}$, $\mathrm{days}$, or $\mathrm{years}$)
  • $\lambda$ = decay constant ($\mathrm{s^{-1}}$)
  • $A(t) = \lambda N(t)$ = instantaneous radioactive activity ($\mathrm{Bq}$)

Worked Example 13.2 — Remaining Mass After Successive Half-Lives
Textbook Example 13.2 (Page 228):
A radioisotope has a half-life of $20\mathrm{\,min}$. If there is initially $102.4\mathrm{\,g}$ of this isotope, how much of it will remain undecayed after $2\mathrm{\,hours}$?
Given Data:
  • Initial mass $m_0 = 102.4\mathrm{\,g}$
  • Half-life $T_{1/2} = 20\mathrm{\,min}$
  • Total elapsed time $t = 2\mathrm{\,h} = 2 \times 60\mathrm{\,min} = 120\mathrm{\,min}$
Step 1: Calculate the number of elapsed half-lives $n$: $$n = \frac{t}{T_{1/2}} = \frac{120\mathrm{\,min}}{20\mathrm{\,min}} = 6\mathrm{\,half\text{-}lives}$$ Step 2: Apply the half-life mass formula: $$m(t) = m_0 \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^n = 102.4\mathrm{\,g} \times \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^6$$ Since $2^6 = 64$: $$m(t) = \frac{102.4\mathrm{\,g}}{64} = 1.60\mathrm{\,g}$$ Conclusion:
After $2\mathrm{\,hours}$, exactly $1.60\mathrm{\,g}$ of the radioactive parent isotope remains undecayed (while $102.4 - 1.60 = 100.8\mathrm{\,g}$ has transmuted into daughter products).
Textbook Exercise — Radioactive Decay Fraction
Chapter 13 Exercise 8 (Textbook Page 247):
Cobalt-60 ($T_{1/2} = 5.25\mathrm{\,years}$) is widely utilized as a therapeutic radiation source in medical oncology. How long after a newly manufactured source is delivered will its radioactive activity have decreased to $\frac{1}{8}\mathrm{th}$ of its initial value?
Given Data:
  • $T_{1/2} = 5.25\mathrm{\,years}$
  • Activity ratio: $\frac{A(t)}{A_0} = \frac{1}{8}$
Step 1: Relate activity fraction to half-lives: $$\frac{A(t)}{A_0} = \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^n = \frac{1}{8} = \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^3$$ Therefore, the decay corresponds to $n = 3\mathrm{\,half\text{-}lives}$. Step 2: Calculate total elapsed time: $$t = n \times T_{1/2} = 3 \times 5.25\mathrm{\,years} = 15.75\mathrm{\,years}$$ Conclusion:
The activity drops to $\frac{1}{8}$ of its original rating after $15.75\mathrm{\,years}$.
Key Takeaways
  • Radioactivity is spontaneous nuclear decay with emission of $\alpha$ (helium nuclei), $\beta$ (electrons/positrons), and $\gamma$ (photons).
  • $\alpha$ particles have highest ionizing power; $\gamma$ rays have highest penetrating ability.
  • Remaining undecayed fraction: $N(t) = N_0(1/2)^{t/T_{1/2}}$.
  • Radioactive tracers require short half-lives to minimize biological radiation damage.
