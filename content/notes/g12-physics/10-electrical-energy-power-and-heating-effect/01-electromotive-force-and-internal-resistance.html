Electromotive Force and Internal Resistance

Concept — Energy Conversion in Voltage Sources

To sustain a continuous electric current through a resistive load, an active device must do work on charge carriers, transporting positive charges internally from lower to higher electric potential. Such devices are called sources of electromotive force (e.g., chemical batteries, photovoltaic cells, and electromagnetic generators):

  • Electromotive Force ($\mathcal{E}$ or $E$): The electromotive force is defined as the amount of energy converted into electrical form per unit positive charge as it moves completely round a closed circuit: $$E = \frac{W}{Q}$$
    • Unit: Volt ($\mathrm{V}$), equivalent to one Joule per Coulomb ($1\mathrm{\,V} = 1\mathrm{\,J/C}$).
    • Misnomer Warning: Despite its historical name, electromotive force is not a mechanical force measured in Newtons; it is an electrical potential difference (energy per unit charge).
  • Internal Resistance ($r$): Every real voltage source possesses inherent internal electrical resistance $r$ arising from the finite electrical conductivity of its electrolytes, electrode materials, chemical polarization, and thermal state. A real source is modeled as an ideal emf $E$ in series with an internal resistor $r$.
  • Circuit Equation: When a load resistor $R$ is connected across the source, a current $I$ circulates: $$E = V_{\text{external}} + V_{\text{internal}} = I R + I r = I(R + r)$$ $$\implies I = \frac{E}{R + r}$$
  • Terminal Voltage ($V$) vs. EMF ($E$): The potential difference measurable across the external terminals of the source: $$V = E - I r$$
    • Open-Circuit Condition ($I = 0$): When no current flows (switch open or measured with an ideal high-impedance voltmeter), $Ir = 0$, so terminal voltage equals the EMF ($V = E$).
    • Closed-Circuit / Discharging ($I > 0$): Terminal voltage is always less than EMF by the internal potential drop $Ir$ ("lost volts").
    • Charging Condition: When an external charger forces reverse current into the battery, $V = E + I r$, and terminal voltage exceeds the emf.
Core Formulas — EMF and Circuit Equations
$$I = \frac{E}{R + r} \qquad\qquad V = E - I r \qquad\qquad E = I(R + r)$$
  • $E$ = electromotive force of the source ($\mathrm{V}$)
  • $V$ = terminal potential difference (available voltage) ($\mathrm{V}$)
  • $I$ = circuit current ($\mathrm{A}$)
  • $R$ = external load resistance ($\Omega$)
  • $r$ = internal resistance of the battery ($\Omega$)
  • $Ir$ = internal potential drop / "lost volts" ($\mathrm{V}$)

Worked Example 10.1 — Determining Unknown EMF and Internal Resistance
Textbook Example 10.1 (Page 156):
When a battery is connected to a $2\,\Omega$ resistor, a current of $0.6\mathrm{\,A}$ flows through the circuit. When connected to a $7\,\Omega$ resistor, the current decreases to $0.2\mathrm{\,A}$. Find the electromotive force ($E$) and internal resistance ($r$) of the battery.
Given Data:
  • Case 1: $R_1 = 2\,\Omega$, $I_1 = 0.6\mathrm{\,A}$
  • Case 2: $R_2 = 7\,\Omega$, $I_2 = 0.2\mathrm{\,A}$
Step 1: Formulate the circuit equations: $$E = I_1(R_1 + r) = 0.6(2 + r) = 1.2 + 0.6r \qquad \text{--- (Eq. 1)}$$ $$E = I_2(R_2 + r) = 0.2(7 + r) = 1.4 + 0.2r \qquad \text{--- (Eq. 2)}$$ Step 2: Equate Eq. 1 and Eq. 2 to solve for $r$: $$1.2 + 0.6r = 1.4 + 0.2r$$ $$0.6r - 0.2r = 1.4 - 1.2$$ $$0.4r = 0.2 \implies r = \frac{0.2}{0.4} = 0.5\,\Omega$$ Step 3: Substitute $r = 0.5\,\Omega$ into Eq. 1 to find $E$: $$E = 1.2 + 0.6(0.5) = 1.2 + 0.3 = 1.5\mathrm{\,V}$$ Conclusion:
The electromotive force is $E = 1.5\mathrm{\,V}$ and the internal resistance is $r = 0.5\,\Omega$.
Worked Example 10.2 — Terminal Voltage Calculation
Textbook Example 10.2 (Page 156):
A battery of electromotive force $12\mathrm{\,V}$ and internal resistance $1\,\Omega$ is connected across a $5\,\Omega$ resistor. Calculate:
(a) The current in the circuit.
(b) The terminal potential difference across the battery.
Calculation:
  • (a) Current $I$: $$I = \frac{E}{R + r} = \frac{12\mathrm{\,V}}{5\,\Omega + 1\,\Omega} = \frac{12}{6} = 2.0\mathrm{\,A}$$
  • (b) Terminal Potential Difference $V$: $$V = E - I r = 12\mathrm{\,V} - (2.0\mathrm{\,A})(1\,\Omega) = 12 - 2 = 10\mathrm{\,V}$$ Alternatively, using Ohm's law on the external resistor: $$V = I R = (2.0\mathrm{\,A})(5\,\Omega) = 10\mathrm{\,V}$$
Key Takeaways
  • EMF ($E$) is energy converted into electrical form per coulomb ($E = W/Q$, in Volts). It is open-circuit voltage.
  • Real sources have internal resistance $r$; the circuit current is $I = \frac{E}{R + r}$.
  • Terminal voltage ($V = E - Ir$) is less than EMF due to the internal voltage drop $Ir$.
  • During external charging, current flows into the positive terminal, making $V = E + Ir > E$.
