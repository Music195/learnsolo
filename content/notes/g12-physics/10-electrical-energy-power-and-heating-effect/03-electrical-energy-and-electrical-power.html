Electrical Energy and Electrical Power

Concept — Energy Dissipation and Metering Standards

Electrical charges flowing through a potential difference undergo energy transformations, converting electrostatic potential energy into thermal, mechanical, or electromagnetic energy:

  • Electrical Energy ($W$): When a charge $Q$ passes through a circuit component across potential difference $V$, the work performed is: $$W = V Q$$ Since electric current is the charge transport rate ($Q = I t$), and by Ohm's law ($V = I R$): $$W = V I t = I^2 R t = \frac{V^2}{R} t$$
    • SI Unit: Joule ($\mathrm{J}$). $1\mathrm{\,J} = 1\mathrm{\,V}\cdot\mathrm{C} = 1\mathrm{\,W}\cdot\mathrm{s}$.
  • Electrical Power ($P$): The rate at which electrical energy is transformed or transferred per unit time: $$P = \frac{W}{t} = V I = I^2 R = \frac{V^2}{R}$$
    • SI Unit: Watt ($\mathrm{W}$). $1\mathrm{\,W} = 1\mathrm{\,J/s} = 1\mathrm{\,V}\cdot\mathrm{A}$.
    • Common engineering multiples: $\text{kilowatt } (1\mathrm{\,kW} = 10^3\mathrm{\,W})$ and $\text{megawatt } (1\mathrm{\,MW} = 10^6\mathrm{\,W})$.
  • Appliance Power Ratings: Every household appliance bears a rated nameplate (e.g., $220\mathrm{\,V},\,100\mathrm{\,W}$). Under rated voltage $V$: $$R = \frac{V^2}{P} \qquad\qquad I_{\text{rated}} = \frac{P}{V}$$ Key Relationship: For devices operating on the same supply voltage (such as domestic mains), a higher-wattage appliance draws more current and possesses a lower operating resistance ($R \propto 1/P$).
  • Commercial Unit of Electricity: Kilowatt-Hour ($\mathrm{kWh}$): Because the Joule is impractically tiny for domestic billing, utility companies meter electrical consumption in kilowatt-hours ($\mathrm{kWh}$), colloquially known as "units": $$1\mathrm{\,kWh} = 1000\mathrm{\,W} \times 3600\mathrm{\,s} = 3.6 \times 10^6\mathrm{\,J} = 3.6\mathrm{\,MJ}$$ $$\text{Meter Units (kWh)} = \frac{\text{Power in Watts} \times \text{Operating Time in Hours}}{1000}$$
Core Formulas — Energy, Power, and Electricity Metering
$$W = V I t = I^2 R t = \frac{V^2}{R} t \qquad\qquad P = V I = I^2 R = \frac{V^2}{R} \qquad\qquad \text{Units (kWh)} = \frac{P(\mathrm{W}) \times t(\mathrm{h})}{1000}$$
  • $W$ = electrical energy consumed or converted ($\mathrm{J}$)
  • $P$ = electrical power dissipation ($\mathrm{W}$ or $\mathrm{kW}$)
  • $V$ = potential difference across the load ($\mathrm{V}$)
  • $I$ = steady electric current ($\mathrm{A}$)
  • $R$ = load electrical resistance ($\Omega$)
  • $t$ = duration of operation ($\mathrm{s}$ or $\mathrm{h}$)
  • $1\mathrm{\,kWh} = 3.6 \times 10^6\mathrm{\,J} = 1\mathrm{\,unit}$ of commercial electricity

Worked Example 10.7 — Meter Reading in Kilowatt-Hours
Textbook Example 10.7 (Page 163):
If a $1200\mathrm{\,W}$ electric iron is used for $50\mathrm{\,min}$, by how many units does the electricity meter reading advance?
Given Data:
  • Power $P = 1200\mathrm{\,W} = 1.2\mathrm{\,kW}$
  • Time duration $t = 50\mathrm{\,min} = \frac{50}{60}\mathrm{\,h} = \frac{5}{6}\mathrm{\,h} \approx 0.833\mathrm{\,h}$
Calculation:
The number of electricity units consumed in kilowatt-hours is: $$\text{Units} = \text{Power in kW} \times \text{Time in hours}$$ $$\text{Units} = (1.2\mathrm{\,kW}) \times \left(\frac{5}{6}\mathrm{\,h}\right) = \frac{6.0}{6} = 1.0\mathrm{\,kWh} = 1.0\mathrm{\,unit}$$ Conclusion:
The electricity meter reading increases by exactly $1.0\mathrm{\,unit}$.
Worked Example 10.6 — Current and Resistance of Domestic Lamps
Textbook Example 10.6 (Page 162):
A $100\mathrm{\,W}$ electric lamp is connected to a $220\mathrm{\,V}$ mains supply.
(i) What current does it draw from the mains?
(ii) What is the operational resistance of the lamp filament?
Given Data:
  • $P = 100\mathrm{\,W}$
  • $V = 220\mathrm{\,V}$
Part (i): Electric current $I$: $$P = V I \implies I = \frac{P}{V} = \frac{100\mathrm{\,W}}{220\mathrm{\,V}} = \frac{5}{11}\mathrm{\,A} \approx 0.455\mathrm{\,A}$$ Part (ii): Hot filament resistance $R$: $$P = \frac{V^2}{R} \implies R = \frac{V^2}{P} = \frac{(220\mathrm{\,V})^2}{100\mathrm{\,W}} = \frac{48{,}400}{100} = 484\,\Omega$$ (Alternatively, $R = \frac{V}{I} = \frac{220\mathrm{\,V}}{0.4545\mathrm{\,A}} = 484\,\Omega$).
Key Takeaways
  • Electrical energy: $W = V I t = I^2 R t = \frac{V^2}{R}t$ ($\mathrm{J}$).
  • Electrical power: $P = V I = I^2 R = \frac{V^2}{R}$ ($\mathrm{W}$).
  • On parallel domestic mains ($220\mathrm{\,V}$), higher power appliances draw more current and have lower internal resistance ($R = V^2/P$).
  • $1\mathrm{\,unit} = 1\mathrm{\,kWh} = 3.6 \times 10^6\mathrm{\,J}$. Meter units consumed $= \frac{P(\mathrm{W}) \times t(\mathrm{h})}{1000}$.
