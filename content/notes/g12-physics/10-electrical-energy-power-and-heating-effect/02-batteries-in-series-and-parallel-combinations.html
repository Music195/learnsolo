Batteries in Series and Parallel Combinations

Concept — Multi-Source Circuit Architectures

To supply high terminal voltages or high sustained discharge currents beyond the capability of a single cell, multiple chemical batteries or galvanic cells are coupled in series aiding, series opposing, or parallel arrangements:

  • Batteries in Series Aiding: Cells are connected head-to-tail such that the positive terminal of one connects to the negative terminal of the next:
    • Total EMF: The emfs reinforce one another additively: $$E_{\text{total}} = E_1 + E_2 + \dots + E_n$$
    • Total Internal Resistance: Internal resistances sum directly in series: $$r_{\text{total}} = r_1 + r_2 + \dots + r_n$$
    • Circuit Current: $$I = \frac{\sum E_i}{R + \sum r_i} = \frac{E_1 + E_2}{R + r_1 + r_2}$$
  • Batteries in Series Opposing: When two cells are connected with opposing polarity (e.g., positive to positive or negative to negative):
    • The net emf is the algebraic difference between them (assuming $E_1 > E_2$): $$E_{\text{net}} = E_1 - E_2$$
    • Internal resistances still impede current and add together: $$r_{\text{total}} = r_1 + r_2$$
    • The stronger cell ($E_1$) drives charging current backward into the weaker cell ($E_2$): $$I = \frac{E_1 - E_2}{R + r_1 + r_2}$$
  • Identical Batteries in Parallel: When $n$ identical batteries (each with EMF $E$ and internal resistance $r$) are connected with all positive terminals joined and all negative terminals joined:
    • Equivalent EMF: Remains identical to that of a single cell: $$E_{\text{eq}} = E$$
    • Equivalent Internal Resistance: Significantly reduced by a factor of $n$: $$r_{\text{eq}} = \frac{r}{n}$$
    • Load Current & Branch Distribution: $$I = \frac{E}{R + \frac{r}{n}}$$ Each individual branch cell supplies only a fraction $I_{\text{cell}} = \frac{I}{n}$ of the total load current, greatly reducing battery heating and extending operating life.
Core Formulas — Battery Combinations
$$\text{Series Aiding: } I = \frac{E_1 + E_2}{R + r_1 + r_2} \qquad \text{Series Opposing: } I = \frac{E_1 - E_2}{R + r_1 + r_2} \qquad \text{Parallel: } I = \frac{E}{R + \frac{r}{n}}$$
Configuration Net EMF ($E_{\text{net}}$) Net Internal $r_{\text{net}}$ Best Used For
Series Aiding ($n$ identical) $n \cdot E$ $n \cdot r$ High voltage output (when $R \gg r$)
Series Opposing ($E_1 > E_2$) $E_1 - E_2$ $r_1 + r_2$ Battery charging circuits
Parallel ($n$ identical) $E$ $r / n$ Heavy current loads (when $R \ll r$) & longevity

Worked Example 10.4 — Series vs. Parallel Performance Comparison
Textbook Example 10.4 (Page 160):
Two batteries each having an EMF of $6\mathrm{\,V}$ and an internal resistance of $0.5\,\Omega$ are connected across a $5\,\Omega$ resistor:
(i) in series aiding.
(ii) in parallel.
(iii) in series opposing.
Find the current flowing through the resistor in each case.
Given Data:
  • $E_1 = 6\mathrm{\,V}, r_1 = 0.5\,\Omega$
  • $E_2 = 6\mathrm{\,V}, r_2 = 0.5\,\Omega$
  • Load resistance $R = 5\,\Omega$
Case (i): In Series Aiding: $$E_{\text{total}} = E_1 + E_2 = 6 + 6 = 12\mathrm{\,V}$$ $$r_{\text{total}} = r_1 + r_2 = 0.5 + 0.5 = 1.0\,\Omega$$ $$I_{\text{series}} = \frac{E_{\text{total}}}{R + r_{\text{total}}} = \frac{12\mathrm{\,V}}{5\,\Omega + 1.0\,\Omega} = \frac{12}{6} = 2.0\mathrm{\,A}$$ Case (ii): In Parallel: $$E_{\text{eq}} = E = 6\mathrm{\,V}$$ $$r_{\text{eq}} = \frac{r}{2} = \frac{0.5\,\Omega}{2} = 0.25\,\Omega$$ $$I_{\text{parallel}} = \frac{E_{\text{eq}}}{R + r_{\text{eq}}} = \frac{6\mathrm{\,V}}{5\,\Omega + 0.25\,\Omega} = \frac{6}{5.25} \approx 1.14\mathrm{\,A}$$ Case (iii): In Series Opposing: $$E_{\text{net}} = E_1 - E_2 = 6\mathrm{\,V} - 6\mathrm{\,V} = 0\mathrm{\,V}$$ $$r_{\text{total}} = r_1 + r_2 = 1.0\,\Omega$$ $$I_{\text{opposing}} = \frac{0\mathrm{\,V}}{5\,\Omega + 1.0\,\Omega} = 0\mathrm{\,A}$$
Textbook Exercise — Two Batteries in Series and Parallel
Chapter 10 Exercise 6 (Textbook Page 169):
When two $6\mathrm{\,V}$ batteries having the same internal resistance $r$ are connected in series aiding to a $5\,\Omega$ resistor, the current is $2\mathrm{\,A}$. When these same batteries are connected in parallel to another resistor $R'$, a current of $1.5\mathrm{\,A}$ flows. Find the resistance $R'$.
Step 1: Determine internal resistance $r$ from the series circuit: $$I_{\text{series}} = \frac{6 + 6}{5 + 2r} = \frac{12}{5 + 2r} = 2.0\mathrm{\,A}$$ $$12 = 2(5 + 2r) = 10 + 4r \implies 4r = 2 \implies r = 0.5\,\Omega$$ Step 2: Solve for $R'$ in the parallel circuit: In parallel, $E_{\text{eq}} = 6\mathrm{\,V}$ and $r_{\text{eq}} = \frac{r}{2} = \frac{0.5}{2} = 0.25\,\Omega$: $$I_{\text{parallel}} = \frac{E_{\text{eq}}}{R' + r_{\text{eq}}} = \frac{6}{R' + 0.25} = 1.5\mathrm{\,A}$$ $$R' + 0.25 = \frac{6}{1.5} = 4.0\,\Omega$$ $$R' = 4.0 - 0.25 = 3.75\,\Omega$$ Conclusion:
The second resistor has a resistance of $R' = 3.75\,\Omega$.
Key Takeaways
  • Series aiding: Emfs add ($E_{\text{tot}} = \sum E_i$); useful when high circuit voltage is demanded.
  • Series opposing: Emfs subtract ($E_{\text{tot}} = E_1 - E_2$); internal resistances always add. Used for charging batteries.
  • Parallel combination: Emfs remain equal to single cell ($E$); internal resistance drops to $r/n$. Provides high current capacity and extends cell operational lifespan.
