Interference of Light and Young's Double-Slit Experiment

Concept — Optical Superposition and Coherence

Wave interference is the physical modification in the spatial distribution of wave energy (intensity) when two or more propagating waves superimpose in the same medium:

  • Coherent Sources: To observe a steady, stationary interference pattern over time, the interfering light waves must be coherent:
    • They must have the same frequency (and wavelength).
    • They must maintain a constant phase difference over time ($\Delta\phi = \text{constant}$).
    • Independent conventional light sources (e.g., two light bulbs) cannot produce observable interference because individual atomic photon emissions are random and undergo phase jumps every $\approx 10^{-10}\mathrm{\,s}$.
  • Young's Double-Slit Experiment (1801): Thomas Young demonstrated the wave nature of light by dividing a single monochromatic wavefront through a pinhole into two closely spaced secondary slits $S_1$ and $S_2$:
    • According to Huygens' principle, $S_1$ and $S_2$ act as coherent secondary cylindrical/spherical wavelet emitters.
    • On an observing screen placed at distance $D$, the overlapping waves form an alternating pattern of bright and dark parallel bands called interference fringes.
  • Path Difference and Fringe Formation: Let $d$ be the slit separation and $\theta$ the angle of propagation to point $P$ on the screen. The optical path difference $\Delta r$ between the two rays is: $$\Delta r = S_2 P - S_1 P = d \sin\theta$$ For small angles $\theta$ where screen distance $D \gg d$, $\sin\theta \approx \tan\theta = \frac{y}{D}$.
  • Constructive Interference (Bright Fringes / Maxima): $$\Delta r = d \sin\theta = m\lambda \qquad\implies\qquad y_m = \frac{m\lambda D}{d} \quad (m = 0, \pm 1, \pm 2, \dots)$$ $m = 0$ corresponds to the central bright fringe.
  • Destructive Interference (Dark Fringes / Minima): $$\Delta r = d \sin\theta = \left(m + \frac{1}{2}\right)\lambda \qquad\implies\qquad y_m' = \frac{\left(m + \frac{1}{2}\right)\lambda D}{d} \quad (m = 0, \pm 1, \pm 2, \dots)$$
  • Fringe Spacing ($\Delta y$): The linear distance between any two adjacent bright fringes (or adjacent dark fringes) is constant across the small-angle region: $$\Delta y = y_{m+1} - y_m = \frac{\lambda D}{d}$$ Decreasing slit spacing $d$ or increasing screen distance $D$ widens the fringes. Longer wavelength (red light) yields wider fringe spacing than shorter wavelength (blue light).
Core Formulas — Young's Double Slit
$$d \sin\theta = m\lambda \quad (\text{Bright}) \qquad\qquad d \sin\theta = \left(m + \frac{1}{2}\right)\lambda \quad (\text{Dark}) \qquad\qquad \Delta y = \frac{\lambda D}{d}$$
  • $d$ = separation between the centers of the two slits ($\mathrm{m}$)
  • $D$ = perpendicular distance from slit plane to observation screen ($\mathrm{m}$)
  • $\lambda$ = wavelength of monochromatic light ($\mathrm{m}$)
  • $y$ = linear distance on screen measured from the central maximum ($y = 0$) ($\mathrm{m}$)
  • $m$ = fringe order integer ($0, \pm 1, \pm 2, \dots$)
  • $\Delta y$ = fringe separation (fringe width) between consecutive bright or dark fringes ($\mathrm{m}$)

Worked Example 8.2 — Determining Wavelength from Fringe Separation
Textbook Example 8.2 (Page 128):
In a double-slit experiment, the separation between the slits is $0.10\mathrm{\,mm}$ and the screen is located $1.2\mathrm{\,m}$ from the slits. The distance between consecutive bright fringes is measured to be $0.65\mathrm{\,cm}$. Calculate the wavelength of the light used.
Given Data:
  • Slit separation $d = 0.10\mathrm{\,mm} = 1.0 \times 10^{-4}\mathrm{\,m}$
  • Screen distance $D = 1.2\mathrm{\,m}$
  • Fringe separation $\Delta y = 0.65\mathrm{\,cm} = 6.5 \times 10^{-3}\mathrm{\,m}$
Calculation:
The fringe spacing formula is: $$\Delta y = \frac{\lambda D}{d}$$ Solving for wavelength $\lambda$: $$\lambda = \frac{d\,\Delta y}{D} = \frac{(1.0 \times 10^{-4}\mathrm{\,m}) \times (6.5 \times 10^{-3}\mathrm{\,m})}{1.2\mathrm{\,m}}$$ $$\lambda = \frac{6.5 \times 10^{-7}}{1.2} \approx 5.42 \times 10^{-7}\mathrm{\,m} = 542\mathrm{\,nm}$$ Conclusion:
The wavelength of the monochromatic light is $542\mathrm{\,nm}$ (in the green region of the visible spectrum).
Textbook Exercise — Distance Between Selected Fringe Orders
Chapter 8 Exercise 6 (Textbook Page 135):
A beam of $650\mathrm{\,nm}$ red light is directed upon two slits separated by a distance of $0.750\mathrm{\,mm}$. A screen is placed $1.50\mathrm{\,m}$ away to capture the interference pattern. What is the distance from the 1st order maximum to the 3rd order minimum?
Given Data:
  • $\lambda = 650\mathrm{\,nm} = 6.50 \times 10^{-7}\mathrm{\,m}$
  • $d = 0.750\mathrm{\,mm} = 7.50 \times 10^{-4}\mathrm{\,m}$
  • $D = 1.50\mathrm{\,m}$
Step 1: Calculate fringe spacing $\Delta y$: $$\Delta y = \frac{\lambda D}{d} = \frac{(6.50 \times 10^{-7}\mathrm{\,m})(1.50\mathrm{\,m})}{7.50 \times 10^{-4}\mathrm{\,m}} = 1.30 \times 10^{-3}\mathrm{\,m} = 1.30\mathrm{\,mm}$$ Step 2: Find the positions:
  • 1st order maximum ($m = 1$): $$y_{\text{max}, 1} = 1 \cdot \Delta y = 1.30\mathrm{\,mm}$$
  • 3rd order minimum corresponds to $m' = 2$ in $y = (m' + \frac{1}{2})\Delta y$, so: $$y_{\text{min}, 3} = \left(2 + \frac{1}{2}\right)\Delta y = 2.5\,\Delta y = 2.5 \times 1.30\mathrm{\,mm} = 3.25\mathrm{\,mm}$$
Step 3: Distance between them (on the same side): $$\Delta L = y_{\text{min}, 3} - y_{\text{max}, 1} = (2.5 - 1.0)\,\Delta y = 1.5\,\Delta y$$ $$\Delta L = 1.5 \times 1.30\mathrm{\,mm} = 1.95\mathrm{\,mm} = 1.95 \times 10^{-3}\mathrm{\,m}$$ (If on opposite sides: $y_{\text{min}, 3} + y_{\text{max}, 1} = 3.25 + 1.30 = 4.55\mathrm{\,mm}$).
Key Takeaways
  • Stable interference requires coherent sources (identical frequency and constant phase difference).
  • In Young's double-slit experiment, path difference is $\Delta r = d \sin\theta$.
  • Constructive condition: $d \sin\theta = m\lambda$ (bright lines). Destructive condition: $d \sin\theta = (m + \frac{1}{2})\lambda$ (dark lines).
  • Uniform fringe separation: $\Delta y = \frac{\lambda D}{d}$. Fringes are wider when slits are closer together ($d$ decreases) or wavelength is longer ($\lambda$ increases).
