Electrical Hazards and Safety Precautions

Concept — Physiological Impact of Shock and Domestic Hazard Prevention

While electrical energy is essential in daily life, misuse or failing infrastructure creates two severe life-threatening hazards: electrocution and electrical fire:

  • Major Causes of Electrical Accidents:
    1. Damaged Insulation: Physical abrasion, excessive flexing, rodent gnawing, or prolonged overheating degrades the protective PVC insulating sheath. Exposed live copper wires pose extreme shock risks on contact or ignite nearby wood and fabric via short-circuit sparks.
    2. Overheating of Cables (Circuit Overloading): Plugging too many high-power appliances into a single multi-way adapter forces the supply cable to carry current far exceeding its rated thermal limit ($I_{\text{total}} = \sum I_i > I_{\text{cable}}$). Extreme Joule dissipation ($I^2 R t$) rapidly melts insulation and causes structural fires.
    3. Damp and Wet Environments: Ordinary domestic tap water, ground moisture, and human perspiration contain dissolved mineral salts (ions) that dramatically reduce skin electrical resistance:
      • Dry Skin Resistance: Typically $10{,}000\text{--}100{,}000\,\Omega$. Current at $220\mathrm{\,V}$ is small ($I \approx 2\text{--}22\mathrm{\,mA}$).
      • Wet Skin Resistance: Drops precipitously to approximately $1{,}000\,\Omega$. By Ohm's law, contact with $220\mathrm{\,V}$ drives a current of: $$I = \frac{V}{R} = \frac{220\mathrm{\,V}}{1{,}000\,\Omega} = 0.22\mathrm{\,A} = 220\mathrm{\,mA}$$ This current is more than double the threshold for instantaneous fatal cardiac arrest. Operating electrical appliances with wet hands or in damp bathrooms is extremely hazardous.
  • Physiological Shock Thresholds: The severity of injury depends strictly on the magnitude of current passing through the body, its path through vital organs, and the duration of contact:
    Body Current ($I$) Physiological Impact on the Human Body
    $\approx 1\mathrm{\,mA}$ Threshold of sensation; slight tingling feeling
    $5\mathrm{\,mA}$ Maximum safe, harmless continuous current
    $10\text{--}20\mathrm{\,mA}$ "Cannot Let Go" Threshold: Involuntary sustained muscle contraction; victim cannot release grip on energized conductor
    $50\text{--}100\mathrm{\,mA}$ Ventricular Fibrillation: Uncoordinated heart muscle fluttering; blood circulation ceases; fatal within seconds
    $> 1\mathrm{\,A}$ Sustained cardiac arrest, respiratory paralysis, severe external and internal tissue burning
  • Why Standard Fuses Do Not Prevent Human Shock: Standard domestic fuses or circuit breakers are rated at $5\mathrm{\,A}, 10\mathrm{\,A}, 13\mathrm{\,A}$, or $30\mathrm{\,A}$ to safeguard structural cables and equipment against fire. Because lethal electrocution occurs at merely $0.05\text{--}0.1\mathrm{\,A}$ ($50\text{--}100\mathrm{\,mA}$), the current killing a human is far below the fuse trip threshold. Protection against personal electrocution requires a Residual Current Device (RCD / GFCI) that trips at $\le 30\mathrm{\,mA}$ within $30\mathrm{\,ms}$.
Core Formulas — Shock Current and Skin Resistance
$$I_{\text{body}} = \frac{V_{\text{contact}}}{R_{\text{skin}} + R_{\text{internal}}} \qquad\qquad I_{\text{lethal}} \ge 50\mathrm{\,mA} \quad (0.05\mathrm{\,A}) \qquad\qquad I_{\text{fuse}} \ge 5\mathrm{\,A} \gg I_{\text{lethal}}$$
  • $V_{\text{contact}} = 220\mathrm{\,V}$ = contact voltage from mains supply
  • $R_{\text{skin}}$ = skin contact resistance (dry $\sim 10^5\,\Omega$, wet $\sim 10^3\,\Omega$)
  • $I_{\text{body}}$ = resulting electric current passing through torso ($\mathrm{A}$ or $\mathrm{mA}$)
  • $I_{\text{lethal}} = 50\text{--}100\mathrm{\,mA}$ = ventricular fibrillation threshold

Conceptual Analysis — Fuses vs. Shock Hazards
Chapter 11 Exercise 8 (Textbook Page 193):
Why are ordinary building circuit breakers and wire fuses ineffective in preventing lethal electric shocks to humans?
Comparative Analysis:
  • Operating Threshold of Fuses & Breakers: Household branch fuses and Miniature Circuit Breakers (MCBs) have minimum trip ratings of $5\mathrm{\,A}, 10\mathrm{\,A}, 13\mathrm{\,A}$, or $30\mathrm{\,A}$. They are specifically engineered to protect copper building wiring against overheating and fire caused by gross short circuits or large overloads.
  • Lethal Biological Threshold: Ventricular fibrillation and fatal electrocution in the human cardiovascular system occur at currents of just $0.05\mathrm{\,A}\text{--}0.10\mathrm{\,A}$ ($50\text{--}100\mathrm{\,mA}$).
  • Conclusion: A lethal current of $100\mathrm{\,mA}$ passing through a human body represents only $\frac{1}{50}\text{th}$ of the current needed to melt a $5\mathrm{\,A}$ fuse. The fuse views this current as a negligible, normal load and will never trip. Specialized electrocution protection requires a Residual Current Device (RCD / GFCI) detecting earth leakage currents of $\le 30\mathrm{\,mA}$ within milliseconds.
Key Takeaways
  • Major electrical hazards: damaged insulation, cable overloading (fire hazard), and wet conditions.
  • Water and sweat drastically drop skin resistance from $\sim 100{,}000\,\Omega$ to $\sim 1{,}000\,\Omega$, multiplying shock current to lethal levels.
  • Lethal ventricular fibrillation occurs at $\ge 50\mathrm{\,mA}$. Standard fuses ($5\text{--}30\mathrm{\,A}$) protect cables from fire, not people from electrocution; RCDs ($\le 30\mathrm{\,mA}$) are required for shock protection.
