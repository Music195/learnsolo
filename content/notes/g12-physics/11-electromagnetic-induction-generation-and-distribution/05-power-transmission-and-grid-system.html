Power Transmission and the Grid System

Concept — Long-Distance Power Distribution and Joule Loss Mitigation

Electrical power generated at distant stations (hydroelectric dams, thermal stations, and gas turbines) must be transmitted over hundreds of kilometers to metropolitan and rural consumer centers through a national grid system:

  • Structure of the National Grid: A national grid is an interconnected network of power stations, substations, and high-voltage transmission lines:
    1. Generation Stage: Three-phase alternators typically generate electricity at intermediate voltages ($11\mathrm{\,kV}$ to $25\mathrm{\,kV}$).
    2. Step-Up Substation: Giant step-up transformers elevate voltage to ultra-high grid transmission levels ($132\mathrm{\,kV}, 220\mathrm{\,kV}, 400\mathrm{\,kV}$, or $500\mathrm{\,kV}$).
    3. Grid Transmission Lines: Strung along steel lattice pylons using aluminum-conductor steel-reinforced ($\mathrm{ACSR}$) cables with low electrical resistance and high tensile strength.
    4. Intermediate Step-Down Substations: Near cities, voltage is stepped down to $33\mathrm{\,kV}$ for heavy industrial plants, then to $11\mathrm{\,kV}$ for light manufacturing.
    5. Distribution Substations: Local pole-mounted or ground transformers step the voltage down to standard consumer levels: $220\text{--}240\mathrm{\,V}$ single-phase for homes, and $400\mathrm{\,V}$ three-phase for commercial facilities.
  • The High-Voltage Transmission Imperative ($P_{\text{loss}} \propto 1/V^2$): Let $P$ be the power to be delivered over transmission lines of total loop resistance $R_{\text{line}}$. The transmission current is: $$I = \frac{P}{V}$$ The electrical power wasted as thermal Joule heating in the cables is: $$P_{\text{loss}} = I^2 R_{\text{line}} = \left(\frac{P}{V}\right)^2 R_{\text{line}} = \frac{P^2 R_{\text{line}}}{V^2}$$
    • Square-Law Reduction: If transmission voltage $V$ is increased by a factor of $100$, the current drops by a factor of $100$, and the resistive transmission power loss $P_{\text{loss}}$ decreases by a factor of $100^2 = 10{,}000$!
    • Without high-voltage stepping, attempting to transmit bulk power at domestic $240\mathrm{\,V}$ would dissipate the entire generated energy as cable heat before reaching consumers.
Core Formulas — Transmission Power Loss
$$I = \frac{P}{V} \qquad\qquad P_{\text{loss}} = I^2 R_{\text{line}} = \frac{P^2 R_{\text{line}}}{V^2} \qquad\qquad \%\,\text{Loss} = \frac{P_{\text{loss}}}{P} \times 100\%$$
  • $P$ = total electrical power being transmitted ($\mathrm{W}$ or $\mathrm{kW}$)
  • $V$ = transmission line operating voltage ($\mathrm{V}$ or $\mathrm{kV}$)
  • $I$ = line transmission current ($\mathrm{A}$)
  • $R_{\text{line}}$ = total resistance of both supply and return conductors ($\Omega$)
  • $P_{\text{loss}}$ = power dissipated as Joule heat in transmission cables ($\mathrm{W}$)

Worked Problem — Power Loss Comparison in High-Voltage Lines
Chapter 11 Exercise 16 (Textbook Page 194):
$120\mathrm{\,kW}$ of electrical power is sent to a small town from a power plant $10\mathrm{\,km}$ away. The transmission lines have a resistance of $0.40\,\Omega\mathrm{\,km^{-1}}$. Calculate the power loss if the electricity is transmitted at:
(i) $240\mathrm{\,V}$
(ii) $24{,}000\mathrm{\,V}$
Given Data:
  • Transmitted power $P = 120\mathrm{\,kW} = 1.20 \times 10^5\mathrm{\,W}$
  • Distance $L = 10\mathrm{\,km}$
  • Total loop resistance for a two-wire line (outward and return conductors): $$R_{\text{line}} = 2 \times (10\mathrm{\,km} \times 0.40\,\Omega\mathrm{\,km^{-1}}) = 8.0\,\Omega$$
Case (i): Transmitted at $V_1 = 240\mathrm{\,V}$: $$I_1 = \frac{P}{V_1} = \frac{1.20 \times 10^5\mathrm{\,W}}{240\mathrm{\,V}} = 500\mathrm{\,A}$$ $$P_{\text{loss}, 1} = I_1^2 R_{\text{line}} = (500\mathrm{\,A})^2 \times 8.0\,\Omega = 250{,}000 \times 8.0 = 2{,}000{,}000\mathrm{\,W} = 2000\mathrm{\,kW}$$ Conclusion: The calculated loss ($2000\mathrm{\,kW}$) vastly exceeds the generated $120\mathrm{\,kW}$, meaning transmission at $240\mathrm{\,V}$ over $10\mathrm{\,km}$ is physically impossible without total power collapse. Case (ii): Transmitted at $V_2 = 24{,}000\mathrm{\,V}$ ($24\mathrm{\,kV}$): $$I_2 = \frac{P}{V_2} = \frac{1.20 \times 10^5\mathrm{\,W}}{24{,}000\mathrm{\,V}} = 5.0\mathrm{\,A}$$ $$P_{\text{loss}, 2} = I_2^2 R_{\text{line}} = (5.0\mathrm{\,A})^2 \times 8.0\,\Omega = 25 \times 8.0 = 200\mathrm{\,W} = 0.20\mathrm{\,kW}$$ $$\text{Percentage Loss} = \frac{0.20\mathrm{\,kW}}{120\mathrm{\,kW}} \times 100\% \approx 0.17\%$$ Conclusion: At $24\mathrm{\,kV}$, less than $0.2\%$ of total power is lost as cable heat!
Key Takeaways
  • The national grid uses transformers to transmit power at hundreds of kilovolts ($132\text{--}500\mathrm{\,kV}$).
  • Line Joule heating loss is $P_{\text{loss}} = I^2 R = \frac{P^2 R}{V^2}$.
  • Increasing transmission voltage by $100\times$ reduces power loss by a factor of $10{,}000$ ($100^2$).
