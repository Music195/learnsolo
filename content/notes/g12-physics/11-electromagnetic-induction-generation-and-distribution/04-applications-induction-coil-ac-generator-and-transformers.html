Applications: Induction Coil, AC Generator, and Transformers

Concept — Electromechanical and Mutual Induction Systems

Electromagnetic induction forms the technological backbone of power generation and electrical conversion via three primary engineering devices:

  • The Induction Coil (Ruhmkorff Coil, 1851): Converts low DC battery voltage ($6\text{--}12\mathrm{\,V}$) into high-voltage electrical pulses ($10\text{--}50\mathrm{\,kV}$) capable of jumping across spark gaps:
    • Primary Winding: Few turns of thick insulated copper wire wound around a laminated soft-iron rod core.
    • Secondary Winding: Many thousands of turns of hair-thin insulated copper wire wound coaxially over the primary.
    • Interrupter & Quenching Capacitor: A vibrating contact breaker repeatedly makes and breaks the primary DC current. A parallel capacitor suppresses destructive contact arcing and accelerates flux collapse, inducing tens of thousands of volts in the secondary. Widely applied in automobile gasoline spark-plug ignition systems.
  • The AC Generator (Alternator): Converts mechanical rotational energy into sinusoidal alternating electrical power:
    • A rectangular armature coil of $N$ turns and planar area $A$ rotates at angular velocity $\omega = 2\pi f$ in a uniform magnetic field $B$.
    • The instantaneous magnetic flux is $\Phi(t) = B A \cos(\omega t)$. By Faraday's Law: $$E(t) = -N \frac{d\Phi}{dt} = N B A \omega \sin(\omega t) = E_0 \sin(\omega t)$$
    • Peak Output Voltage: $$E_0 = N B A \omega = 2\pi f N B A$$
    • Methods to Increase Generator Output: (1) Increase rotational frequency $\omega$ (also increases electrical frequency), (2) Enlarge coil cross-sectional area $A$, (3) Add more armature winding turns $N$, (4) Use powerful electromagnets with higher field strength $B$.
    • Slip Rings and Brushes: Continuous copper slip rings rotating with the shaft maintain continuous sliding contact with static carbon brushes, tapping AC without tangled leads.
  • The Electrical Transformer: A static electromagnetic device that converts alternating voltage and current levels between circuits via mutual induction without altering cyclical frequency:
    • Ideal Transformer Relations: $$\frac{V_s}{V_p} = \frac{N_s}{N_p} = \frac{I_p}{I_s} \qquad (\text{under } 100\% \text{ efficiency, } P_{\text{in}} = P_{\text{out}})$$
    • Step-Up Transformer: $N_s > N_p \implies V_s > V_p$ and $I_s < I_p$. Used at generating stations.
    • Step-Down Transformer: $N_s < N_p \implies V_s < V_p$ and $I_s > I_p$. Used in substations and appliance power supplies.
  • Transformer Energy Losses & Engineering Remedies:
    1. Copper Losses ($I^2 R$): Joule heat dissipated in winding resistance; minimized by using thick, high-conductivity oxygen-free copper conductors.
    2. Eddy Current Losses: Alternating core flux induces internal circulating swirl currents in the iron block; minimized by constructing the core from thin laminated sheets of silicon steel insulated with varnish.
    3. Hysteresis Loss: Energy expended in repeatedly realigning magnetic domains each half-cycle; minimized using soft magnetic silicon steel with narrow B-H hysteresis loops.
    4. Flux Leakage: Incomplete magnetic coupling; minimized by winding secondary turns directly over primary turns on a closed core.
Core Formulas — Generator and Transformer Equations
$$E_0 = N B A \omega = 2\pi f N B A \qquad\qquad \frac{V_s}{V_p} = \frac{N_s}{N_p} = \frac{I_p}{I_s} \qquad\qquad \eta = \frac{V_s I_s}{V_p I_p} \times 100\%$$
  • $E_0$ = peak generator output voltage ($\mathrm{V}$)
  • $N$ or $N_p, N_s$ = number of turns in generator armature or transformer primary/secondary
  • $B$ = magnetic flux density ($\mathrm{T}$)
  • $A$ = coil area ($\mathrm{m^2}$)
  • $\omega = 2\pi f$ = rotational speed ($\mathrm{rad/s}$)
  • $V_p, V_s$ = primary and secondary terminal voltages ($\mathrm{V}$)
  • $I_p, I_s$ = primary and secondary currents ($\mathrm{A}$)
  • $\eta$ = percentage power conversion efficiency
Interactive Demo — AC Generator and Transformer

How does a spinning coil make AC, and how does a transformer change 240 V into 60 V?

Generator: the coil turns between the poles. The flux through it, $BA\cos\omega t$ (dashed), changes fastest when the coil is edge-on, so that is when the emf $E = NBA\omega \sin \omega t$ peaks. Change $N$, $B$, $A$ or $f$ and watch the peak $E_0 = NBA\omega$. Transformer: the alternating flux in the iron core links both coils, so every turn gets the same emf: $V_s/V_p = N_s/N_p$. Try a DC battery: the flux stops changing and the secondary gives nothing.

Worked Example 11.6 — Step-Down Transformer Currents and Turns
Textbook Example 11.6 (Page 185):
A $2\mathrm{\,kW}$ ideal transformer is used to step down an AC supply of $240\mathrm{\,V}$ to $60\mathrm{\,V}$.
(i) What is the turns ratio $\frac{N_s}{N_p}$ of the transformer?
(ii) What is the current in the primary coil?
(iii) What is the current in the secondary coil?
Given Data:
  • Rated power $P = 2\mathrm{\,kW} = 2000\mathrm{\,W}$
  • Primary voltage $V_p = 240\mathrm{\,V}$
  • Secondary voltage $V_s = 60\mathrm{\,V}$
Part (i): Turns Ratio $\frac{N_s}{N_p}$: $$\frac{N_s}{N_p} = \frac{V_s}{V_p} = \frac{60\mathrm{\,V}}{240\mathrm{\,V}} = \frac{1}{4} = 0.25$$ Part (ii): Primary Current $I_p$: $$P_{\text{in}} = V_p I_p \implies I_p = \frac{P}{V_p} = \frac{2000\mathrm{\,W}}{240\mathrm{\,V}} = \frac{25}{3}\mathrm{\,A} \approx 8.33\mathrm{\,A}$$ Part (iii): Secondary Current $I_s$: $$P_{\text{out}} = V_s I_s \implies I_s = \frac{P}{V_s} = \frac{2000\mathrm{\,W}}{60\mathrm{\,V}} = \frac{100}{3}\mathrm{\,A} \approx 33.33\mathrm{\,A}$$ Check: $\frac{I_s}{I_p} = \frac{33.33}{8.33} = 4 = \frac{N_p}{N_s}$. Stepping voltage down by $4\times$ steps current up by $4\times$, conserving power.
Key Takeaways
  • AC Generator: $E_0 = N B A \omega$; output scales with turns, field strength, area, and speed.
  • Transformer: $\frac{V_s}{V_p} = \frac{N_s}{N_p} = \frac{I_p}{I_s}$. Steps voltage up or down via mutual induction.
  • Core laminations minimize parasitic eddy currents by restricting circulating current paths.
