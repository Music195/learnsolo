Centripetal Acceleration

Concept — Why Acceleration Exists in Circular Motion

An object revolving in a circle is continuously accelerating, even when moving at constant speed:

  • Uniform Circular Motion: The object moves along a circular path at constant speed $v$. While the magnitude of velocity remains constant, its direction continuously changes at every point. This continuous change in velocity produces an acceleration directed radially inward toward the center, called centripetal acceleration (or radial acceleration, $\vec{a}_c$).
  • Perpendicular Vectors: In uniform circular motion, the velocity vector $\vec{v}$ is tangent to the circle, while the centripetal acceleration $\vec{a}_c$ points directly toward the center. They are strictly perpendicular ($\vec{a}_c \perp \vec{v}$) at all times.
  • Nonuniform Circular Motion: If the speed of the object changes along the circular path, it possesses two simultaneous components of acceleration:
    1. Centripetal acceleration ($a_c = \frac{v^2}{r} = r\omega^2$): Directed toward the center, responsible for changing the direction of motion.
    2. Tangential acceleration ($a_T = r\alpha$): Directed tangent to the path, responsible for changing the speed of motion.
    The total (resultant) acceleration vector is the vector sum: $\vec{a} = \vec{a}_c + \vec{a}_T$.
Core Formulas — Centripetal and Resultant Accelerations
$$a_c = \frac{v^2}{r} = r\omega^2 \qquad\qquad a = \sqrt{a_c^2 + a_T^2} \qquad\qquad \tan\phi = \frac{a_T}{a_c}$$
  • $a_c$ = centripetal (radial) acceleration ($\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$), directed radially inward toward the center
  • $a_T$ = tangential acceleration ($\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$), along the tangent ($a_T = r\alpha$)
  • $a$ = magnitude of the resultant linear acceleration ($\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$)
  • $\phi$ = angle of the resultant acceleration measured from the radial direction ($\vec{a}_c$)
  • $r$ = radius of the circular path ($\mathrm{m}$); $v$ = linear speed ($\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$); $\omega$ = angular velocity ($\mathrm{rad\,s^{-1}}$)
Geometric Derivation of $a_c = \frac{v^2}{r}$

Consider an object moving along a circle of radius $r$ at constant speed $v$. During a small time interval $\Delta t$, it moves from point $A$ to point $B$ along an arc of length $\Delta s$, sweeping an angle $\Delta\theta$.

The velocities at $A$ and $B$ are $\vec{v}_1$ and $\vec{v}_2$, both of magnitude $v$. Drawing $\vec{v}_1$ and $\vec{v}_2$ tail-to-tail forms a velocity triangle with change vector $\Delta\vec{v} = \vec{v}_2 - \vec{v}_1$.

Because $\vec{v}_1 \perp \vec{r}_A$ and $\vec{v}_2 \perp \vec{r}_B$, the isosceles velocity triangle is geometrically similar to the position triangle $CAB$:

$$\frac{|\Delta\vec{v}|}{v} \approx \frac{\Delta s}{r} \implies |\Delta\vec{v}| \approx \frac{v}{r}\Delta s$$

Dividing both sides by $\Delta t$ and taking the limit as $\Delta t \to 0$:

$$a_c = \lim_{\Delta t \to 0} \frac{|\Delta\vec{v}|}{\Delta t} = \frac{v}{r} \lim_{\Delta t \to 0} \frac{\Delta s}{\Delta t} = \frac{v}{r} \cdot v = \frac{v^2}{r}$$

Substituting $v = r\omega$ yields the equivalent rotational form:

$$a_c = \frac{(r\omega)^2}{r} = r\omega^2$$

As $\Delta t \to 0$, the vector $\Delta\vec{v}$ becomes exactly perpendicular to the velocity vectors, pointing straight toward the center of the circle.

Worked Examples — Textbook Problems

Example 1.4 (Textbook p. 9): The platter of the hard drive of a computer uniformly rotates at $7200\ \mathrm{rpm}$. If the reading head of the drive is located $3\ \mathrm{cm}$ from the rotational axis:

  1. What is the linear speed of the point on the platter just below the reading head?
  2. What is its centripetal acceleration?
  3. If a single bit requires $0.5\ \mu\mathrm{m}$ of length along the direction of motion, how many bits per second can the writing head write at $3\ \mathrm{cm}$ from the axis?
Show step-by-step solution

Given: $r = 3\ \mathrm{cm} = 0.03\ \mathrm{m}$. Convert rotation rate to angular velocity $\omega$:

$$\omega = 7200\ \mathrm{rpm} = 7200 \times \frac{2\pi}{60}\ \mathrm{rad\,s^{-1}} = 240\pi\ \mathrm{rad\,s^{-1}} \approx 753.98\ \mathrm{rad\,s^{-1}}$$

(1) Linear speed:

$$v = r\omega = 0.03 \times 240\pi = 7.2\pi \approx 22.62\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$$

(2) Centripetal acceleration:

$$a_c = \frac{v^2}{r} = \frac{(22.62)^2}{0.03} \approx 1.71 \times 10^4\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$$

(3) Bits written per second:

$$\text{Rate} = \frac{v}{\text{bit length}} = \frac{22.62\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}}{0.5 \times 10^{-6}\ \mathrm{m/bit}} = 4.52 \times 10^7\ \text{bits s}^{-1} \quad (45.2\ \text{Mbps})$$

Answer: $v \approx 22.6\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$, $a_c \approx 1.71 \times 10^4\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$, and $4.52 \times 10^7\ \text{bits/s}$.

Example 1.5 (Textbook p. 9–10): A centrifuge has a radius of $20\ \mathrm{cm}$ and decelerates from a maximum rotational rate of $7.2 \times 10^3\ \mathrm{rpm}$ to rest in $30\ \mathrm{s}$ under constant angular acceleration, rotating counter-clockwise. What is the magnitude and direction of the resultant acceleration of a point at the tip of the centrifuge at $t = 10\ \mathrm{s}$?

Show step-by-step solution

Given: $r = 20\ \mathrm{cm} = 0.2\ \mathrm{m}$, $\omega_0 = 7.2 \times 10^3\ \mathrm{rpm} = 7200 \times \frac{2\pi}{60} = 240\pi\ \mathrm{rad\,s^{-1}}$, $\omega(30) = 0$, $t_{\text{stop}} = 30\ \mathrm{s}$.

Calculate the angular acceleration $\alpha$:

$$\alpha = \frac{\omega - \omega_0}{t} = \frac{0 - 240\pi}{30} = -8\pi\ \mathrm{rad\,s^{-2}} \approx -25.13\ \mathrm{rad\,s^{-2}}$$

At $t = 10\ \mathrm{s}$, find the instantaneous angular velocity:

$$\omega = \omega_0 + \alpha t = 240\pi + (-8\pi)(10) = 160\pi\ \mathrm{rad\,s^{-1}}$$

Compute centripetal and tangential accelerations:

$$a_c = r\omega^2 = 0.2 \times (160\pi)^2 \approx 5.05 \times 10^4\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$$ $$a_T = r\alpha = 0.2 \times (-8\pi) \approx -5.03\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$$

Magnitude of resultant acceleration:

$$a = \sqrt{a_c^2 + a_T^2} = \sqrt{(5.05 \times 10^4)^2 + (-5.03)^2} \approx 5.05 \times 10^4\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$$

Since $a_c \gg |a_T|$, the resultant acceleration is almost completely dominated by the centripetal component.

Direction of resultant acceleration:

$$\tan\phi = \frac{|a_T|}{a_c} = \frac{5.03}{5.05 \times 10^4} \approx 9.96 \times 10^{-5} \implies \phi \approx 0.0057^\circ$$

Answer: $a \approx 5.05 \times 10^4\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$, pointing at an angle of $0.0057^\circ$ with the radial inward vector $\vec{a}_c$.

Reviewed Exercise 2 (Textbook p. 11): A stone tied to a string is moving in a circle of radius $1.5\ \mathrm{m}$ at a constant speed of $8\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$. Calculate the magnitude of the centripetal acceleration of the stone.

Show step-by-step solution

Given: $r = 1.5\ \mathrm{m}$, $v = 8\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$.

Using the centripetal acceleration formula:

$$a_c = \frac{v^2}{r} = \frac{8^2}{1.5} = \frac{64}{1.5} \approx 42.67\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$$

Answer: $a_c \approx 42.7\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$.

Common Mistakes to Avoid
  • Zero Acceleration in Uniform Circular Motion: Although speed $v$ is constant, the velocity vector is constantly turning. Acceleration is never zero in circular motion; it is always at least $a_c = v^2/r$.
  • Confusing the Roles of $a_c$ and $a_T$:
    • $a_c = v^2/r$ changes the direction of $\vec{v}$. It points radially inward toward the center.
    • $a_T = r\alpha$ changes the magnitude (speed) of $\vec{v}$. It points along the tangent.
  • Directly Adding $a_c$ and $a_T$ as Scalars: Because $\vec{a}_c$ and $\vec{a}_T$ are perpendicular, the magnitude of the total acceleration is the Pythagorean hypotenuse: $a = \sqrt{a_c^2 + a_T^2}$, not $a_c + a_T$.
Key Takeaways
  • Centripetal acceleration is given by $a_c = \dfrac{v^2}{r} = r\omega^2$, always directed toward the center of the circular path.
  • In uniform circular motion, $\vec{a}_c \perp \vec{v}$ at all times.
  • In nonuniform circular motion, the total acceleration is $\vec{a} = \vec{a}_c + \vec{a}_T$, with magnitude $a = \sqrt{a_c^2 + a_T^2}$ and angle $\tan\phi = a_T/a_c$.
