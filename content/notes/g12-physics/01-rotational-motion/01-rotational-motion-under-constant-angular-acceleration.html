Rotational Motion under Constant Angular Acceleration

Concept — Circular Motion vs Rotational Motion
  • Circular motion: a single object moves along a circle. Example: an artificial satellite going around the Earth at a constant height.
  • Rotational motion: a whole body turns about an axis. Examples: the Earth spinning about its own axis (day and night), the blades of a fan, a Ferris wheel.
  • Rigid body: a body that does not change shape. The distance between any two of its particles stays constant. When a rigid body rotates, every particle moves in a circle about the same axis.
  • Constant angular acceleration: when the angular velocity $\omega$ changes at a constant rate, the angular acceleration $\alpha$ is constant. This is the rotational version of motion with constant acceleration.
Core Formulas — Equations of Motion for Constant $\alpha$
$$\omega = \omega_0 + \alpha t \qquad \bar{\omega} = \frac{\omega + \omega_0}{2} \qquad \theta = \omega_0 t + \tfrac{1}{2}\alpha t^2 \qquad \omega^2 = \omega_0^2 + 2\alpha\theta$$
  • $\omega_0$ = initial angular velocity at $t = 0$ (rad s⁻¹); $\omega$ = angular velocity at time $t$
  • $\alpha$ = angular acceleration (rad s⁻²): positive when speeding up, negative when slowing down
  • $\theta$ = angular displacement (rad). Number of revolutions $= \dfrac{\theta}{2\pi}$

Where the Equations Come From

Start from the definition of constant angular acceleration:

$$\alpha = \frac{\omega - \omega_0}{t} \implies \omega = \omega_0 + \alpha t \quad (1.1)$$

Because $\omega$ grows steadily, the average angular velocity is the mean of the first and last values, $\bar{\omega} = \frac{\omega + \omega_0}{2}$ (1.2). The angular displacement is $\theta = \bar{\omega}\,t$, so:

$$\theta = \frac{\omega_0 + (\omega_0 + \alpha t)}{2}\,t = \omega_0 t + \tfrac{1}{2}\alpha t^2 \quad (1.3)$$

Eliminating $t$ with $t = \frac{\omega - \omega_0}{\alpha}$ gives $\theta = \frac{\omega + \omega_0}{2}\cdot\frac{\omega - \omega_0}{\alpha}$, that is:

$$\omega^2 = \omega_0^2 + 2\alpha\theta \quad (1.4)$$

These are the Grade 10 equations of linear motion with $s \to \theta$, $v \to \omega$, $a \to \alpha$.

Worked Examples

Example 1.1 (Textbook p. 3): A Ferris wheel starts from rest and reaches an angular velocity of $1.5\ \mathrm{rad\,s^{-1}}$ in $10\ \mathrm{s}$ with constant angular acceleration. (i) Find its angular acceleration. (ii) How many revolutions does it make in $10\ \mathrm{s}$?

Show step-by-step solution

Given: $\omega_0 = 0$, $\omega = 1.5\ \mathrm{rad\,s^{-1}}$, $t = 10\ \mathrm{s}$.

(i) $\omega = \omega_0 + \alpha t \implies 1.5 = 0 + \alpha(10) \implies \alpha = 0.15\ \mathrm{rad\,s^{-2}}$.

(ii) $\theta = \omega_0 t + \frac{1}{2}\alpha t^2 = 0 + \frac{1}{2}(0.15)(10)^2 = 7.5\ \mathrm{rad}$.

Since $1\ \text{rev} = 2\pi\ \mathrm{rad}$: $\ \dfrac{7.5}{2\pi} = 1.19\ \text{rev}$.

Answer: $\alpha = 0.15\ \mathrm{rad\,s^{-2}}$; about $1.19$ revolutions.

Reviewed Exercise 2 (Textbook p. 3): A ball is whirled with constant angular acceleration. Starting from rest, it reaches an angular velocity of $25\ \mathrm{rad\,s^{-1}}$ after an angular displacement of $41\ \mathrm{rad}$. Find its angular acceleration.

Show step-by-step solution

Time is not given, so use the equation without $t$:

$$\omega^2 = \omega_0^2 + 2\alpha\theta \implies 25^2 = 0 + 2\alpha(41) \implies \alpha = \frac{625}{82} = 7.62\ \mathrm{rad\,s^{-2}}$$

Answer: $\alpha \approx 7.6\ \mathrm{rad\,s^{-2}}$.

Exercise 5 (Textbook p. 11): The angular velocity of a rotating body increases from $500\ \mathrm{rpm}$ to $1500\ \mathrm{rpm}$ in $2\ \mathrm{min}$. (i) Find its angular acceleration. (ii) What angle does it turn through in these $2\ \mathrm{min}$?

Show step-by-step solution

Convert to SI units ($1\ \mathrm{rpm} = \frac{2\pi}{60}\ \mathrm{rad\,s^{-1}}$):

$$\omega_0 = 500 \times \tfrac{2\pi}{60} = 52.4\ \mathrm{rad\,s^{-1}}, \quad \omega = 1500 \times \tfrac{2\pi}{60} = 157.1\ \mathrm{rad\,s^{-1}}, \quad t = 120\ \mathrm{s}$$

(i) $\alpha = \dfrac{\omega - \omega_0}{t} = \dfrac{157.1 - 52.4}{120} = 0.873\ \mathrm{rad\,s^{-2}}$.

(ii) $\theta = \bar{\omega}\,t = \dfrac{157.1 + 52.4}{2} \times 120 = 1.26 \times 10^4\ \mathrm{rad}$ (that is $2000$ revolutions).

Answer: $\alpha \approx 0.87\ \mathrm{rad\,s^{-2}}$; $\theta \approx 1.26 \times 10^4\ \mathrm{rad} = 2000\ \text{rev}$.

Common Mistakes to Avoid
  • Using rpm in the equations: convert first. $\omega\ (\mathrm{rad\,s^{-1}}) = \text{rpm} \times \dfrac{2\pi}{60}$.
  • Giving radians when revolutions are asked: revolutions $= \theta / 2\pi$.
  • Sign of $\alpha$: when a body slows down, $\alpha$ is negative. A body coming to rest has $\omega = 0$ as its final value.
  • Units: $\omega$ is in $\mathrm{rad\,s^{-1}}$ but $\alpha$ is in $\mathrm{rad\,s^{-2}}$.
Key Takeaways
  • In rotational motion, every particle of a rigid body moves in a circle about a common axis.
  • For constant $\alpha$: $\omega = \omega_0 + \alpha t$, $\ \theta = \omega_0 t + \frac{1}{2}\alpha t^2$, $\ \omega^2 = \omega_0^2 + 2\alpha\theta$, $\ \bar{\omega} = \frac{\omega + \omega_0}{2}$.
  • They are the linear equations of motion with $s, v, a$ replaced by $\theta, \omega, \alpha$.
