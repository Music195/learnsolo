The Doppler Effect

Concept — Frequency Shifts Due to Relative Motion

First analyzed mathematically by Austrian physicist Christian Doppler in 1842, the Doppler effect is the apparent shift in observed wave frequency when there is relative motion between a wave source and a receiver:

  • Physical Origin:
    • When approaching: The source catches up to the sound waves it previously emitted. Wavefronts are crowded closer together (shorter apparent wavelength $\lambda'$), so more crests reach the observer per second, raising the observed pitch ($f_o > f_s$).
    • When receding: The source moves away from emitted wavefronts, stretching them further apart (longer apparent wavelength $\lambda'$), so fewer crests reach the observer per second, lowering the observed pitch ($f_o < f_s$).
  • Everyday Manifestation: The sudden dropping pitch of an ambulance or police siren as it speeds past a stationary pedestrian (from a high-pitched whine on approach to a low-pitched drone on departure).
  • Applications Across Science:
    • Astronomy (Cosmological Redshift): Light emitted by distant receding galaxies is shifted to longer, redder wavelengths, providing conclusive observational proof of the expanding universe.
    • Police Radar & Speed Traps: Radar guns bounce microwaves off approaching vehicles; the measured Doppler frequency shift reveals instantaneous vehicle velocity ($\Delta f \propto v_{\text{car}}$).
    • Cardiovascular Medicine (Doppler Ultrasound): Ultrasonic pulses reflected by flowing red blood cells exhibit frequency shifts that measure hemodynamic blood velocity, revealing arterial blockages, carotid stenosis, and cardiac valve insufficiency.
Core Formulas — The General Doppler Equation
$$f_o = f_s \left(\frac{v \pm v_o}{v \mp v_s}\right)$$
  • $f_o$ = apparent frequency heard by the observer ($\mathrm{Hz}$)
  • $f_s$ = intrinsic frequency emitted by the sound source ($\mathrm{Hz}$)
  • $v$ = speed of sound in the stationary medium ($\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$)
  • $v_o$ = speed of the observer relative to the medium ($\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$)
  • $v_s$ = speed of the source relative to the medium ($\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$)
  • Sign Convention Rule:
    • Numerator (Observer): Use $+v_o$ if the observer moves toward the source; use $-v_o$ if moving away.
    • Denominator (Source): Use $-v_s$ if the source moves toward the observer; use $+v_s$ if moving away.
    • Memory rule: Any approach motion increases $f_o$; any receding motion decreases $f_o$.

Textbook Worked Examples — Doppler Shift Calculations

Example 6.7: An ambulance traveling at $44\text{ m\,s}^{-1}$ approaches a car driving ahead in the same direction at $28\text{ m\,s}^{-1}$. The ambulance siren sounds at $550\text{ Hz}$. At what frequency does the driver of the car hear the siren? Take the speed of sound $v = 330\text{ m\,s}^{-1}$.

Solution:
Given:
Source (ambulance): $v_s = 44\text{ m\,s}^{-1}$ moving towards the car $\implies -v_s$ in denominator.
Observer (car): $v_o = 28\text{ m\,s}^{-1}$ moving away from the ambulance $\implies -v_o$ in numerator.
Frequency $f_s = 550\text{ Hz}$, $v = 330\text{ m\,s}^{-1}$.

Applying the general Doppler equation: $$f_o = f_s \left(\frac{v - v_o}{v - v_s}\right)$$ $$f_o = 550 \times \left(\frac{330 - 28}{330 - 44}\right) = 550 \times \left(\frac{302}{286}\right)$$ $$f_o = 550 \times 1.05594 \approx 580.77\text{ Hz} \approx 581\text{ Hz}$$

Reviewed Exercises:

1. In Example 6.7, what will be the frequency of the siren heard by the car driver after the ambulance overtakes and speeds away from the car?
Once the ambulance passes the car:
Source (ambulance) moves away from the car $\implies +v_s$ in denominator.
Observer (car) moves towards the ambulance ahead $\implies +v_o$ in numerator.
$$f_o = f_s \left(\frac{v + v_o}{v + v_s}\right) = 550 \times \left(\frac{330 + 28}{330 + 44}\right) = 550 \times \left(\frac{358}{374}\right) \approx 550 \times 0.95722 \approx 526.47\text{ Hz}$$ The heard pitch drops sharply from $581\text{ Hz}$ to $526.5\text{ Hz}$ as it overtakes.

2. A monorail approaches a passenger platform at a speed of $50\text{ m\,s}^{-1}$ while blowing its whistle. A person standing stationary on the platform ($v_o = 0$) hears the whistle at $261\text{ Hz}$. The temperature is $0^\circ\text{C}$ where the speed of sound is $332\text{ m\,s}^{-1}$. What is the true rest frequency emitted by the whistle?
Given: $v_s = 50\text{ m\,s}^{-1}$ (approaching), $v_o = 0$, $v = 332\text{ m\,s}^{-1}$, $f_o = 261\text{ Hz}$.
$$f_o = f_s \left(\frac{v}{v - v_s}\right) \implies 261 = f_s \left(\frac{332}{332 - 50}\right) = f_s \left(\frac{332}{282}\right)$$ $$f_s = 261 \times \left(\frac{282}{332}\right) \approx 261 \times 0.8494 \approx 221.7\text{ Hz}$$

Key Takeaways
  • The Doppler effect is the perceived frequency shift caused by relative motion between source and observer.
  • General formula: $f_o = f_s \left(\frac{v \pm v_o}{v \mp v_s}\right)$.
  • Approaching motion always raises the observed pitch ($f_o > f_s$); receding motion lowers the pitch ($f_o < f_s$).
  • Applications include astronomical galactic red shifts, traffic speed radars, and medical Doppler echocardiography of blood flow.
