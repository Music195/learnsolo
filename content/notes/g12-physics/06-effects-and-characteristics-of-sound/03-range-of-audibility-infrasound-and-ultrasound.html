Range of Audibility, Infrasound, and Ultrasound

Concept — The Acoustic Frequency Spectrum

Acoustic waves span a vast frequency spectrum, divided into distinct categories based on human auditory perception:

  • Range of Audibility (Human Hearing Range): For healthy young human ears, the audible frequency band spans from a lower limit of $20\mathrm{Hz}$ to an upper limit of approximately $20\,000\mathrm{Hz}$ ($20\mathrm{kHz}$). Frequencies outside this interval cannot be registered by the human cochlea.
  • Infrasound ($f < 20\mathrm{Hz}$): Sound waves with frequencies below the lower threshold of human audibility:
    • Physical Properties: Infrasound possesses extremely long wavelengths ($\lambda > 17\mathrm{m}$ in air). Long wavelengths suffer minimal atmospheric absorption, scattering, and attenuation, allowing infrasonic signals to travel vast distances (hundreds to thousands of kilometers) around the globe.
    • Biological and Natural Sources: Used for long-range herd communication by elephants ($15\mathrm{Hz}$ up to $4\mathrm{km}$), rhinoceroses ($10\mathrm{Hz}$), and whales; generated geologically by earthquakes, volcanic eruptions, tsunamis, and severe storm fronts.
  • Ultrasound ($f > 20\,000\mathrm{Hz}$): Sound waves with frequencies above the upper threshold of human audibility ($20\mathrm{kHz}$ to several hundred $\mathrm{MHz}$):
    • Physical Properties: Extremely short wavelengths ($\lambda$ in millimeters or micrometers) enable ultrasound to form sharp, narrow, highly directional beams that resolve fine microscopic details and detect tiny flaws.
    • Industrial and Medical Applications:
      • Non-Destructive Testing (NDT) & Quality Control: Probing concrete slabs, aircraft alloys, welded joints, and subterranean pipelines for hidden hairline cracks and internal voids.
      • Medical Diagnostic Sonography: Piezoelectric transducers transmit $1\text{ to }15\mathrm{MHz}$ pulses through biological soft tissue; differential acoustic reflections produce real-time cross-sectional images of internal organs and developing fetuses without ionizing radiation hazards.
      • Ultrasonic Scalers in Dentistry: High-frequency microscopic mechanical vibrations shatter stubborn dental calculus and plaque, flushed away by a continuous antiseptic water spray.
      • Ultrasonic Cleaning & Cavitation: Intense ultrasound creates millions of microscopic vacuum vapor bubbles during the rarefaction phase. These bubbles implode violently during subsequent compression cycles, producing localized micro-jets that scrub microscopic grime from delicate watch mechanisms, jewelry, surgical scalpels, and optical components.
      • SONAR (Sound Navigation and Ranging): Marine navigation systems emit high-frequency pulses through water to gauge seabed bathymetry ($d = \frac{v t}{2}$), detect shipwrecks, and track submarines.
Core Formulas — Wave Relations in Acoustic Diagnostics
$$v = f \lambda \qquad\iff\qquad \lambda = \frac{v}{f} \qquad\qquad d = \frac{v \cdot t}{2}$$
  • $v$ = acoustic velocity in biological tissue or medium ($\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$)
  • $f$ = wave frequency ($\mathrm{Hz}$ or $\mathrm{MHz}$, $1\mathrm{MHz} = 10^6\mathrm{Hz}$)
  • $\lambda$ = acoustic wavelength ($\mathrm{m}$ or $\mathrm{mm}$) — sets the fundamental limit of spatial image resolution
  • $d$ = depth of reflecting tissue interface or seabed target ($\mathrm{m}$)
  • $t$ = round-trip echo return time ($\mathrm{s}$)

Textbook Worked Examples — Frequency and Wavelength Calculations

Example 6.6: Elephants communicate over long distances (up to $4\text{ km}$) using infrasonic calls with fundamental frequencies as low as $15\text{ Hz}$. A rhinoceros utilizes frequencies down to $10\text{ Hz}$.
(i) Why are elephants able to communicate over such extensive distances?
(ii) Can a rhinoceros communicate over even greater distances than an elephant? Explain.

Solution:
(i) Infrasound waves ($f < 20\text{ Hz}$) have very long acoustic wavelengths ($\lambda = v/f \approx 340 / 15 \approx 22.7\text{ m}$). Long-wavelength waves bend smoothly around obstacles (trees, hills) via diffraction and experience negligible atmospheric absorption and viscous scattering compared to higher audible frequencies. Consequently, elephants communicate reliably over distances exceeding $4\text{ km}$.
(ii) Yes. The rhinoceros emits frequencies down to $10\text{ Hz}$, which corresponds to an even longer wavelength ($\lambda \approx 340 / 10 = 34\text{ m}$). Lower frequencies experience even less atmospheric attenuation, allowing rhinoceros signals to propagate over distances exceeding $4\text{ km}$.

Reviewed Exercises:

1. Why can infrasound not be heard by human ears?
Human auditory physiology requires sound frequencies of at least $20\mathrm{Hz}$ to induce resonance and mechanical stimulation of the basilar membrane and stereocilia hair cells inside the cochlea. Below $20\mathrm{Hz}$, the pressure oscillations occur too slowly to trigger auditory nerve action potentials.

2. A piezoelectric medical transducer produces ultrasound at a frequency of $10\text{ MHz}$. Calculate the wavelength of this ultrasound in blood if the speed of sound in blood is $1560\text{ m\,s}^{-1}$.
Given: $f = 10\text{ MHz} = 10 \times 10^6\text{ Hz} = 10^7\text{ Hz}$, $v = 1560\text{ m\,s}^{-1}$.
Using the wave equation $v = f \lambda$: $$\lambda = \frac{v}{f} = \frac{1560\text{ m\,s}^{-1}}{10^7\text{ s}^{-1}} = 1.56 \times 10^{-4}\text{ m} = 0.156\text{ mm} = 156\ \mu\text{m}$$ This sub-millimeter wavelength enables ultrasonic imaging to resolve tiny anatomical structures inside blood vessels.

Key Takeaways
  • The human audibility range extends from $20\mathrm{Hz}$ to $20\,000\mathrm{Hz}$ ($20\mathrm{kHz}$).
  • Infrasound ($f < 20\mathrm{Hz}$) has long wavelengths that travel kilometers with minimal attenuation (used by elephants and rhinos).
  • Ultrasound ($f > 20\mathrm{kHz}$) has millimeter-scale wavelengths that permit precise directional beaming.
  • Key applications of ultrasound: non-destructive flaw testing, prenatal imaging, dental scaling, cavitation cleaning, and SONAR depth sounding.
