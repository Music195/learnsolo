Characteristics of Musical Sound

Concept — The Three Objective and Subjective Dimensions of Sound

Every musical sound is characterized by three distinct perceptual properties that correspond directly to physical wave parameters:

  • 1. Pitch (related to Frequency): The subjective sensation of how "sharp", "shrill", or "deep" a sound is:
    • Pitch is determined strictly by the fundamental frequency of vibration ($f$).
    • High frequency produces high pitch (e.g. bird chirp, flute, mosquito buzz, piccolo); low frequency produces low pitch (e.g. bass drum, toad croak, tuba, bull roar).
  • 2. Loudness (related to Amplitude and Intensity): The subjective physiological perception of sound energy received by the human ear:
    • Loudness depends on physical sound intensity ($I$), which in turn is proportional to the square of wave amplitude ($I \propto A^2$).
    • Doubling the physical displacement amplitude of vibrating air quadruples the transmitted energy flux ($2^2 = 4$). Unlike objective intensity ($\mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}$), perceived loudness is subjective and varies across individuals and across frequencies according to equal-loudness hearing curves.
  • 3. Quality or Timbre (related to Waveform and Harmonics): The characteristic that enables a listener to distinguish between two notes of identical pitch (same fundamental frequency) and identical loudness played on two different musical instruments (e.g. a middle C played on a flute versus a violin):
    • Timbre is governed by the composite acoustic waveform, which depends on the number, relative intensities, and phases of higher harmonics and overtones superimposed on the fundamental frequency.
    • A tuning fork emits an almost pure sinusoidal wave with zero overtones; a flute produces few harmonics; while a violin or trumpet produces rich waveforms with prominent higher harmonics.
Core Formulas — Wave Intensity and Harmonic Equations
$$I = \frac{1}{2}\rho v \omega^2 A^2 = 2\pi^2 \rho v f^2 A^2 \qquad\qquad \lambda = \frac{v}{f}$$
  • $I$ = sound wave intensity ($\mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}$)
  • $\rho$ = density of medium ($1.29\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$ for air at STP)
  • $v$ = speed of sound in the medium ($\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$)
  • $\omega = 2\pi f$ = angular frequency of the acoustic wave ($\mathrm{rad\,s^{-1}}$)
  • $A$ = physical displacement amplitude of air molecules ($\mathrm{m}$)
  • $f$ = fundamental frequency ($\mathrm{Hz}$)

Textbook Worked Examples — Molecular Displacement and Acoustics

Example 6.8: Calculate the displacement amplitude $A$ of air molecules for a sound wave having a frequency of $1000\text{ Hz}$ at the threshold of human hearing ($I = 1 \times 10^{-12}\text{ W\,m}^{-2}$). The speed of sound in air is $343\text{ m\,s}^{-1}$ at $20^\circ\text{C}$ and the density of air is $1.29\text{ kg\,m}^{-3}$.

Solution:
Given:
$f = 1000\text{ Hz}$, $I = 10^{-12}\text{ W\,m}^{-2}$, $v = 343\text{ m\,s}^{-1}$, $\rho = 1.29\text{ kg\,m}^{-3}$.
Angular frequency: $$\omega = 2\pi f = 2 \times 3.142 \times 1000 \approx 6284\text{ rad\,s}^{-1}$$ Using the sound wave intensity equation: $$I = \frac{1}{2}\rho v \omega^2 A^2 \implies A^2 = \frac{2 I}{\rho v \omega^2}$$ $$A^2 = \frac{2 \times 10^{-12}}{(1.29)(343)(6284)^2} = \frac{2 \times 10^{-12}}{(442.47)(3.9488 \times 10^7)} = \frac{2 \times 10^{-12}}{1.747 \times 10^{10}} \approx 1.145 \times 10^{-22}\text{ m}^2$$ Taking the square root: $$A = \sqrt{1.145 \times 10^{-22}} \approx 1.07 \times 10^{-11}\text{ m}$$ Physical Insight: At the threshold of hearing, the human ear is capable of detecting mechanical vibrations of air molecules that are smaller than $0.02\text{ nm}$ — less than one tenth the diameter of an individual atom!

Chapter Exercises:

Exercise 1: Sound travels faster in moist (humid) air than in dry air. Why is this?
Water molecules ($\mathrm{H_2O}$) have a molar mass of $18\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}$, whereas the diatomic nitrogen ($\mathrm{N_2}$, $28\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}$) and oxygen ($\mathrm{O_2}$, $32\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}$) that dominate dry air have an average molar mass of $\approx 29\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}$. When water vapor replaces dry air molecules at identical pressure and temperature, the overall density $\rho$ of the air decreases. Because sound speed $v = \sqrt{\gamma p / \rho}$ is inversely proportional to $\sqrt{\rho}$, sound travels faster through lighter moist air.

Exercise 3: The speed of sound in a particular liquid is $1.6 \times 10^3\text{ m\,s}^{-1}$. If the bulk modulus of the liquid is $0.15 \times 10^{10}\text{ N\,m}^{-2}$, what is the density of the liquid?
Using $v = \sqrt{B / \rho} \implies \rho = \frac{B}{v^2}$:
$$\rho = \frac{0.15 \times 10^{10}\text{ N\,m}^{-2}}{(1.6 \times 10^3\text{ m\,s}^{-1})^2} = \frac{1.5 \times 10^9}{2.56 \times 10^6} \approx 585.9\text{ kg\,m}^{-3}$$
Reviewed Exercise: Compare the sounds of a mosquito flying near your ear and a croaking toad in terms of pitch and loudness.

  • Mosquito: High pitch (rapid wingbeats $\sim 600\mathrm{Hz}$) but low loudness (very small acoustic amplitude and intensity).
  • Toad: Low pitch (slow vocal sac resonance $\sim 100\mathrm{Hz}$) but high loudness (large acoustic amplitude and power).

Key Takeaways
  • Pitch is the subjective perception of fundamental wave frequency ($f$).
  • Loudness is the subjective perception of sound wave amplitude ($A$) and intensity ($I \propto A^2$).
  • Quality or timbre is the acoustic fingerprint that distinguishes instruments of identical pitch and loudness, governed by waveform harmonic overtones.
  • At the threshold of human hearing, molecular displacement amplitude is on the order of $10^{-11}\mathrm{m}$.
