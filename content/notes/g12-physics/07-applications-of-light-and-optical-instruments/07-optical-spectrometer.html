The Optical Spectrometer

Concept — Dispersion of Light and Spectral Analysis

An optical spectrometer is a precision instrument designed to disperse composite polychromatic light into its component wavelengths and quantitatively measure refractive indices and spectral emission lines:

  • The Three Core Subsystems of a Spectrometer:
    • 1. Collimator: Consists of an adjustable entrance slit positioned precisely at the focal plane of an achromatic convex lens. It converts diverging light rays from the light source into a parallel, collimated beam.
    • 2. Prism / Grating Table: A level, rotatable horizontal circular stage that holds the dispersing element (either a 60° triangular glass prism or a ruled diffraction grating). It features leveling screws and an angular circular vernier scale.
    • 3. Telescope: An astronomical telescope mounted on a rotating arm coaxial with the circular scale. It gathers the dispersed parallel rays and focuses them onto an eyepiece reticle (cross-hairs) for accurate angular measurements.
  • Types of Optical Spectra:
    • Continuous Emission Spectrum: A seamless, unbroken rainbow band of colors emitted by incandescent, dense hot matter (e.g., tungsten lamp filaments, molten lava, the solar photosphere).
    • Line Emission Spectrum: A series of isolated, sharply defined bright colored lines produced by low-density, excited gases (e.g., sodium vapor, hydrogen discharge tubes, neon lighting). Each chemical element emits a unique spectral barcode corresponding to transitions between quantized electronic energy levels.
    • Absorption Spectrum: A continuous rainbow interrupted by dark absorption lines (e.g., solar Fraunhofer lines) formed when continuous light traverses a cooler intervening vapor that selectively absorbs resonant frequencies.
  • Spectrophotometry: The quantitative scientific technique of measuring how much light a chemical substance absorbs or transmits across continuous wavelengths, widely used in biochemistry, environmental monitoring, pharmacology, and astrophysics to identify unknown materials and determine chemical concentrations.
Core Formulas — Prism Refraction and Grating Equations
$$n = \frac{\sin\left(\frac{A + \delta_m}{2}\right)}{\sin\left(\frac{A}{2}\right)} \qquad\qquad d \sin\theta = m \lambda$$
  • $n$ = refractive index of the prism material for a specific spectral wavelength
  • $A$ = refracting angle of the prism (typically $60^\circ$)
  • $\delta_m$ = angle of minimum deviation measured with the spectrometer
  • $d = \frac{1}{N}$ = grating spacing / slit separation ($\mathrm{m}$), where $N$ is lines per meter
  • $\theta$ = diffraction angle of the $m^{\text{th}}$ order spectral line
  • $m = 0, \pm 1, \pm 2, \dots$ = spectral diffraction order; $\lambda$ = wavelength ($\mathrm{m}$)

Reviewed Exercises — Spectrometers and Dispersion

Exercise 1: Name the dispersion elements used in an optical spectrometer.

Answer:

  • Triangular Glass/Quartz Prism: Disperses light via wavelength-dependent refraction (dispersion). Shorter wavelengths (violet) encounter a higher refractive index and suffer greater angular deviation than longer wavelengths (red).
  • Diffraction Grating: An optical plate with thousands of closely spaced parallel ruled lines (e.g. $600\text{ lines/mm}$) that disperses light through constructive interference. Unlike prisms, gratings deviate red light through a greater angle than violet light ($d \sin\theta = m\lambda$).

Exercise 2: Mention the different types of spectrometers.

Answer:

  1. Prism Spectrometer: Uses a glass prism for educational and optical dispersion studies.
  2. Grating Spectrometer: Uses a diffraction grating to obtain high spectral resolution and linear dispersion.
  3. UV-Visible Spectrophotometer: Measures absorption in the ultraviolet and visible spectrum ($200\text{ to }800\mathrm{nm}$) to quantify chemical concentrations in solutions.
  4. Infrared (FTIR) Spectrometer: Analyzes vibrational absorption bands in the infrared region to determine molecular organic functional groups.

Key Takeaways
  • An optical spectrometer consists of three essential parts: collimator, prism/grating table, and telescope.
  • Prisms disperse light because shorter wavelengths (violet) experience higher refractive index and refract more sharply than longer wavelengths (red).
  • Line emission spectra act as unique elemental fingerprints for chemical identification.
  • Prism refractive index is determined from minimum deviation: $n = \frac{\sin[(A+\delta_m)/2]}{\sin(A/2)}$.
