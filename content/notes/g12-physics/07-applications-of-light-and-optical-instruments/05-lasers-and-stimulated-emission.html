Lasers and Stimulated Emission

Concept — Fundamentals of Coherent Optical Amplification

The acronym LASER stands for Light Amplification by Stimulated Emission of Radiation. A laser generates an intensely concentrated, coherent, and monochromatic beam of electromagnetic radiation:

  • The Three Atomic Radiative Processes:
    • 1. Stimulated Absorption: An electron in the lower energy level $E_1$ absorbs an incident photon of resonant energy $h\nu = E_2 - E_1$ and transitions up to an excited energy state $E_2$.
    • 2. Spontaneous Emission: An excited atom at $E_2$ randomly drops to $E_1$ after a short radiative lifetime ($\approx 10^{-8}\mathrm{s}$), emitting a photon in an arbitrary direction with random phase (the mechanism behind incandescent lightbulbs and fluorescent tubes).
    • 3. Stimulated Emission: Predicted by Albert Einstein in 1917. An oncoming incident photon of energy $h\nu = E_2 - E_1$ interacts with an already excited atom, stimulating it to transition down to $E_1$ and release an identical clone photon. The stimulated photon has the exact same frequency, wavelength, phase, polarization, and propagation direction as the triggering photon.
  • Essential Conditions for Laser Action:
    • Metastable State: An excited atomic level possessing a prolonged lifetime ($\approx 10^{-3}\mathrm{s}$, thousands of times longer than ordinary levels), enabling excited atoms to accumulate.
    • Population Inversion: A non-thermal state where the population of atoms in the upper excited state exceeds the ground state population ($N_2 > N_1$), ensuring stimulated emission outpaces stimulated absorption.
    • Optical Pumping: Supplying external energy (via flash lamps, electric discharges, or semiconductor laser diodes) to raise atoms into the excited state.
    • Optical Resonant Cavity: A pair of precisely aligned parallel mirrors enclosing the active laser medium: one fully reflective ($100\%$) and the other partially transmitting ($\sim 98-99\%$). Photons reflected repeatedly between the mirrors stimulate an exponential cascade of coherent emissions before emerging as an intense output beam.
  • Unique Characteristics of Laser Light:
    • Monochromatic: Emits an exceptionally pure single wavelength.
    • Coherent: All wave crests and troughs march in exact spatial and temporal lockstep.
    • Collimated (Directional): Spreads with virtually zero beam divergence over kilometers.
    • High Intensity: Concentrates enormous optical energy flux into microscopic focal spots (used in eye surgery, metal cutting, barcode scanning, and fiber telecommunications).
Core Formulas — Photon Energy and Laser Beam Power
$$E_{\text{photon}} = h \nu = \frac{h c}{\lambda} \qquad\qquad P = n \cdot E_{\text{photon}} = n \left(\frac{h c}{\lambda}\right) \qquad\iff\qquad n = \frac{P \lambda}{h c}$$
  • $E_{\text{photon}}$ = energy of a single photon ($\mathrm{J}$)
  • $h = 6.63 \times 10^{-34}\mathrm{J\,s}$ = Planck's constant
  • $c = 3.0 \times 10^8\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ = speed of light in vacuum
  • $\lambda$ = laser emission wavelength ($\mathrm{m}$; $1\mathrm{\text{Å}} = 10^{-10}\mathrm{m}$; $1\mu\mathrm{m} = 10^{-6}\mathrm{m}$)
  • $P$ = continuous laser output beam power ($\mathrm{W} = \mathrm{J\,s^{-1}}$)
  • $n$ = photon emission rate ($\mathrm{photons\,s^{-1}}$)

Each circle is an atom: grey = ground state, purple = pumped up, orange = waiting in the long-lived metastable state. A photon passing an orange atom makes it emit an identical photon (stimulated emission); passing a grey atom it is absorbed. Textbook Worked Examples — Laser Photon Flux

Example 7.5: A Helium-Neon (He-Ne) gas laser emits a red laser beam of wavelength $\lambda = 6328\text{ \AA}$ ($632.8\text{ nm}$) with continuous power $5\text{ mW}$. How many photons are emitted per second?

Solution:
Given:
Wavelength $\lambda = 6328\text{ \AA} = 6328 \times 10^{-10}\text{ m} = 6.328 \times 10^{-7}\text{ m}$.
Power $P = 5\text{ mW} = 5 \times 10^{-3}\text{ W} = 5 \times 10^{-3}\text{ J\,s}^{-1}$.

1. Energy of a single emitted photon: $$E_{\text{photon}} = \frac{h c}{\lambda} = \frac{(6.63 \times 10^{-34}\text{ J\,s})(3.0 \times 10^8\text{ m\,s}^{-1})}{6.328 \times 10^{-7}\text{ m}} = \frac{1.989 \times 10^{-25}}{6.328 \times 10^{-7}} \approx 3.143 \times 10^{-19}\text{ J}$$ 2. Rate of photon emission ($n$): $$P = n \cdot E_{\text{photon}} \implies n = \frac{P}{E_{\text{photon}}} = \frac{5 \times 10^{-3}\text{ J\,s}^{-1}}{3.143 \times 10^{-19}\text{ J}} \approx 1.59 \times 10^{16}\text{ photons\,s}^{-1}$$

Reviewed Exercise:

How many photons are emitted in $1\text{ s}$ by a $7.5\text{ mW}$ infrared $\mathrm{CO_2}$ laser with wavelength $\lambda = 10.6\ \mu\text{m}$?
Given: $P = 7.5 \times 10^{-3}\text{ W}$, $\lambda = 10.6 \times 10^{-6}\text{ m}$.
Energy per photon: $$E_{\text{photon}} = \frac{h c}{\lambda} = \frac{(6.63 \times 10^{-34})(3.0 \times 10^8)}{10.6 \times 10^{-6}} = \frac{1.989 \times 10^{-25}}{1.06 \times 10^{-5}} \approx 1.876 \times 10^{-20}\text{ J}$$ Number of photons per second: $$n = \frac{P}{E_{\text{photon}}} = \frac{7.5 \times 10^{-3}\text{ J\,s}^{-1}}{1.876 \times 10^{-20}\text{ J}} \approx 4.0 \times 10^{17}\text{ photons\,s}^{-1}$$

Key Takeaways
  • Stimulated emission occurs when an incident photon induces an excited atom to emit an identical in-phase photon.
  • Laser action requires a metastable state, population inversion ($N_2 > N_1$), and an optical cavity resonator.
  • Laser beams are distinguished by extreme monochromaticity, spatial/temporal coherence, collimation, and power density.
  • Photon flux: $n = \frac{P \lambda}{h c}$.
