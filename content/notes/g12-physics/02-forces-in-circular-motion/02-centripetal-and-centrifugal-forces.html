Centripetal and Centrifugal Forces

Concept — Real Inward Force vs Apparent Outward Effect

In analyzing circular motion, students frequently encounter two terms: centripetal force and centrifugal force. Understanding their fundamental difference is crucial in mechanics:

  • Centripetal Force (Real Force): The net force acting toward the center of curvature in an inertial (ground) frame of reference. It is provided by real physical interactions (such as tension, friction, or gravity) that continuously accelerate the object into a circular path.
  • The Newton's Third Law Misconception: When you whirl a ball on a string, your hand pulls inward on the ball (centripetal force on the ball). By Newton's third law, the ball exerts an equal and opposite outward reaction force on your hand. This reaction force acts on your hand, not on the ball!
  • Centrifugal Force (Apparent / Fictitious Force): When observed from inside a rotating (accelerating, non-inertial) frame of reference—such as a passenger inside a car turning sharply—objects seem to be pushed radially outward. This apparent outward push is not caused by any physical agent; it is simply the manifestation of the object's own inertia tending to keep moving along a straight line.

Technological Applications of the Centrifugal Effect

Although centrifugal force is fictitious in an inertial frame, the principle of inertial separation in rotating systems is utilized in numerous important engineering devices:

  1. Centrifuge (Sedimentation Principle): In chemical and medical laboratories, sample test tubes are spun at thousands of rpm. Because denser particles possess greater inertia, they require larger inward forces than the surrounding fluid can provide. Consequently, denser particles migrate outward to the bottom of the tube, separating from lower-density fluids.
  2. Washing Machine (Spin Drying): When wet clothes are spun rapidly inside a perforated drum, the rigid perforated wall exerts the normal force needed to keep the clothes moving in a circle. Water droplets on the clothes lack this inward restraint and escape tangentially through the drum perforations by inertia.
  3. Centrifugal Pump: A hydraulic machine that converts mechanical energy into pressure energy. Rotating impeller blades impart high kinetic energy to fluid, driving it outward through the volute casing to discharge under elevated pressure.
  4. Centrifugal Governor: A mechanical engine regulator consisting of two revolving fly-balls on pivoted arms. As engine speed increases, the fly-balls move farther outward, lifting a sleeve that partially closes a throttle valve to automatically stabilize engine speed.

Worked Examples — Textbook Problems

Example 2.3 (Textbook p. 20): An athlete weighing $790\ \mathrm{N}$ is running around a curve of radius of curvature $r = 5\ \mathrm{m}$ at a speed of $6\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$. (i) Find the centripetal force acting on him. (ii) Which force provides this centripetal force? (iii) What will happen to the athlete if the radius of curvature is smaller ($g = 9.8\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$)?

Show step-by-step solution

Given: $w = 790\ \mathrm{N}$, $v = 6\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$, $r = 5\ \mathrm{m}$.

First find the mass of the athlete:

$$m = \frac{w}{g} = \frac{790}{9.8} \approx 80.61\ \mathrm{kg}$$

(i) Centripetal force:

$$F_c = \frac{m v^2}{r} = \frac{80.61 \times 6^2}{5} = \frac{80.61 \times 36}{5} \approx 580.4\ \mathrm{N}$$

(ii) Physical source: The frictional force between the athlete's shoes and the running track surface provides the necessary inward centripetal force.

(iii) Smaller radius: Since $F_c = \dfrac{mv^2}{r}$, if $r$ is smaller while speed $v$ is maintained, the required centripetal force increases proportionally ($F_c \propto 1/r$). If the required force exceeds the maximum available static friction ($\mu_s N$), the athlete will slip and fall outward.

Answer: $F_c \approx 580.4\ \mathrm{N}$; provided by static friction; the athlete will slip if the required force exceeds maximum friction.

Reviewed Exercise (Textbook p. 20): When a skater turns in a circle, why must he lean inward toward the center? Explain with reference to forces.

Show step-by-step solution

When skating along a curved path, the ice exerts two forces on the skate blades: an upward normal force $F_N$ equal to the skater's weight ($mg$), and a horizontal inward friction force $F_f$ acting as the centripetal force.

If the skater stood completely vertical, the inward frictional force at the ice would exert an unbalanced torque about his center of mass, tipping him outward. By leaning inward at an angle $\theta$ such that $\tan\theta = \dfrac{v^2}{rg}$, the vector sum of $F_N$ and $F_f$ passes directly through the skater's center of mass, producing zero net torque and maintaining rotational equilibrium.

Answer: Inward lean aligns the resultant ground reaction force through the skater's center of mass, providing centripetal force while preventing outward tipping.

Common Mistakes to Avoid
  • Claiming Centrifugal Force Is the Reaction to Centripetal Force: This is false. Third-law action-reaction pairs must act on different bodies. The reaction to the inward centripetal force on the object is the outward force exerted by the object on the constraining agent (e.g. on the string or track). Centrifugal force is an apparent force inside rotating frames.
  • Drawing Centrifugal Force in an Inertial Frame: In standard ground-based Newtonian analysis, only real forces (centripetal net force) exist. Never draw a centrifugal force arrow alongside real physical forces in an inertial free-body diagram.
Key Takeaways
  • Centripetal force is a real, inward-directed net force ($F_c = mv^2/r$) that keeps objects in curved paths.
  • Centrifugal force is an apparent (inertial) outward effect perceived only from within a rotating frame of reference.
  • Inertial separation in rotating devices enables essential technologies: centrifuges, spin dryers, centrifugal pumps, and engine governors.
