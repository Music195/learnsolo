Radioactive Substances

Concept — Unstable Nuclei and Their Radiation

A radionuclide is an atom with an unstable nucleus that has excess energy. Radioactive pollution comes from radionuclides released into the environment (radioactive material, radioisotopes and radioactive waste).

  • Radioactivity is the spontaneous decay of heavy nuclei, giving off alpha (α) particles, beta (β) particles and gamma (γ) rays and forming new elements. These and X-rays are ionising radiation.
  • Penetrating power: α is stopped by a few sheets of paper; β passes paper but is stopped by aluminium foil (low-energy β enters the surface of skin, high-energy β a centimetre or more); γ is the hardest to stop and needs concrete, lead or other heavy shielding. γ rays and X-rays can badly damage cells.
  • Background radiation is mostly natural: radioactive minerals in the ground, soil and water, cosmic radiation, and radionuclides in our own bodies (mainly potassium-40 from food and water). Traces of uranium and thorium are found on Earth. Radon gas collects in homes and buildings and in underground water; long exposure can cause lung cancer.
  • Man-made sources: fissile U-235, Am-241 and Pu-239 made in breeder reactors; H-3 (tritium), Co-60, I-129, Cs-137 and Ba-137. Ionisation smoke detectors use Am-241 as an α source (safe if undamaged and used as directed).
  • Uses of radioisotopes: radiotracers in medicine, agriculture, food preservation and sterilisation, industry, civil engineering and research. C-14 for radiocarbon dating of wooden and organic objects; U-235 as fuel for nuclear power and naval propulsion; Cs-137 in radiation therapy for cancer and in industrial gauges for liquid flow in pipes.
  • Nuclear power and weapons: reactors use fissile heavy atoms (U-233, U-235, Pu-239) instead of fossil fuels. A nuclear bomb releases heat, blast waves and radiation, destroying buildings, with effects on the environment for many years.
  • Impact: K-40, Ra-226 and Ra-228 leach from minerals into water; mines, power plants and industry can leak waste. Radionuclides in water and food can collect in blood and vital organs and cause tumours and cancer.
Penetration Ladder
$$\text{penetrating power: } \gamma > \beta > \alpha \qquad \text{stopped by: } \alpha \to \text{paper},\ \beta \to \text{Al foil},\ \gamma \to \text{thick lead or concrete}$$
  • α is a helium nucleus (+2 charge, heavy), β is a fast electron (−1), γ is high-energy electromagnetic radiation (no charge)
  • Fissile nuclides can keep up a fission chain reaction: U-233, U-235, Pu-239
Common Mistakes to Avoid
  • "α is weak, so harmless": it penetrates least, but inside the body (radon breathed in) it ionises strongly.
  • Background = man-made: most background radiation is natural.

Worked Examples — Review Questions (p. 120)

Review Q1: Which particles or rays come from a radioactive substance, and how penetrating is each?

Show answer

α particles: stopped by a few sheets of paper. β particles: pass through paper, stopped by aluminium foil. γ rays: most penetrating, need concrete or lead shielding.

Review Q2: Uses of carbon-14, caesium-137, uranium-235 and americium-241.

Show answer

C-14: radiocarbon dating of wooden and organic objects. Cs-137: radiation therapy for cancer, and industrial gauges for liquid flow in pipes. U-235: fuel for nuclear power plants and naval propulsion. Am-241: α source in ionisation smoke detectors.

Review Q3: Choose the fissile nuclides: Co-60, I-129, U-233, Pu-239, Ba-137.

Show answer

U-233 and Pu-239 (they can sustain a fission chain reaction). Co-60, I-129 and Ba-137 are radioactive but not fissile.

Key Takeaways
  • α (paper), β (Al foil), γ (lead or concrete): γ > β > α in penetration.
  • Most background radiation is natural (minerals, radon, K-40, cosmic rays).
  • Uses: C-14 dating, U-235 fuel, Cs-137 therapy and gauges, Am-241 smoke detectors.
