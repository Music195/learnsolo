Le Chatelier's Principle

Concept — An Equilibrium Pushes Back

In 1884 the French chemist Henri Louis Le Chatelier described how an equilibrium responds to a change (a "stress"):

If a system is in equilibrium and any factor affecting it, such as temperature, pressure or concentration, is changed, the equilibrium shifts so as to cancel out the effect of the change.

Add something and the system uses it up; remove something and the system replaces it. Then a new equilibrium is set up.

  • Concentration (for $\mathrm{A + B \rightleftharpoons C + D}$): adding a reactant or removing a product shifts the equilibrium to the right (more products). Removing a reactant or adding a product shifts it to the left. Example: in $\mathrm{Fe^{3+}(aq) + SCN^-(aq) \rightleftharpoons FeSCN^{2+}(aq)}$ (pale yellow + colourless ⇌ blood red), adding SCN⁻ makes the solution a deeper red.
  • Temperature depends on the sign of $\Delta H$ (given for the forward reaction). Raising the temperature favours the endothermic direction (which absorbs the heat); lowering it favours the exothermic direction. For $\mathrm{N_2O_4(g) \rightleftharpoons 2NO_2(g)}$, $\Delta H = +$ (colourless ⇌ reddish-brown): in ice water the mixture becomes almost colourless, in hot water deep brown.
  • Pressure (gases only; solids and liquids are ignored): raising the pressure (smaller volume) shifts the equilibrium to the side with fewer moles of gas; lowering it shifts to the side with more moles. If both sides have equal moles of gas, pressure has no effect (e.g. $\mathrm{H_2 + I_2 \rightleftharpoons 2HI}$).
  • Catalyst: speeds up the forward and reverse reactions equally, so equilibrium is reached sooner, but the position of equilibrium does not change.

In industry: for the Haber process, $\mathrm{N_2(g) + 3H_2(g) \rightleftharpoons 2NH_3(g)}$ ($\Delta H$ negative; 4 mol of gas → 2 mol), a high pressure and a fairly low temperature give the best yield, but too low a temperature is too slow, so an iron catalyst (iron(III) oxide with a little aluminium oxide) is used at a compromise temperature. For methanol, $\mathrm{CO(g) + 2H_2(g) \rightleftharpoons CH_3OH(g)}$ ($\Delta H$ negative), a high pressure and about 250 °C with a Cu–ZnO–Al2O3 catalyst are used.

Summary — Which Way Does It Shift?
ChangeEquilibrium shifts…
add reactant / remove productright (forward)
remove reactant / add productleft (reverse)
raise temperaturein the endothermic direction (ΔH +)
lower temperaturein the exothermic direction (ΔH −)
raise pressureto the side with fewer moles of gas
lower pressureto the side with more moles of gas
add a catalystno shift (equilibrium reached faster)
Common Mistakes to Avoid
  • Counting moles of solids: in $\mathrm{4CuO(s) \rightleftharpoons 2Cu_2O(s) + O_2(g)}$ only the 1 mol of $\mathrm{O_2}$ counts.
  • "The shift cancels the change completely": it only partly cancels it. Squeezing $\mathrm{NO_2}$ makes the colour darker at once; then it fades a little as the equilibrium shifts, but it stays darker than before.
  • The sign of ΔH: it belongs to the forward reaction; the reverse reaction has the opposite sign.

Worked Examples — Textbook Examples 1–5 (pp. 66–69)

Example 1: Effect of adding SCN⁻ to $\mathrm{Fe^{3+}(aq) + SCN^-(aq) \rightleftharpoons FeSCN^{2+}(aq)}$.

Show answer

Adding a reactant: the system uses it up, so the forward reaction is favoured and the equilibrium shifts to the right. More $\mathrm{FeSCN^{2+}}$ forms: the colour becomes a deeper blood red.

Example 2: Effect of increasing temperature on $\mathrm{N_2(g) + 3H_2(g) \rightleftharpoons 2NH_3(g)}$, $\Delta H$ negative.

Show answer

The forward reaction is exothermic, the reverse endothermic. A rise in temperature favours the endothermic (reverse) reaction: the equilibrium shifts left and less ammonia is present.

Example 3: Effect of increasing pressure on $\mathrm{2SO_2(g) + O_2(g) \rightleftharpoons 2SO_3(g)}$.

Show answer

Left side: 2 + 1 = 3 mol gas; right side: 2 mol. Higher pressure favours fewer moles: the forward reaction, so the equilibrium shifts right (more $\mathrm{SO_3}$).

Example 4: Effect of decreasing pressure on $\mathrm{4CuO(s) \rightleftharpoons 2Cu_2O(s) + O_2(g)}$.

Show answer

The solids do not count: 0 mol gas on the left, 1 mol $\mathrm{O_2}$ on the right. Lower pressure favours more moles of gas: the forward reaction, so the equilibrium shifts right.

Example 5: Effect of increasing pressure on $\mathrm{H_2(g) + I_2(g) \rightleftharpoons 2HI(g)}$.

Show answer

2 mol gas on each side, so a pressure change has no effect on the position of equilibrium.

Worked Examples — Review Questions (p. 70) and Exercises (p. 75)

Review Q1: For $\mathrm{H_2(g) + Br_2(g) \rightleftharpoons 2HBr(g)}$: (a) which favours more HBr: (i) adding H2, (ii) removing Br2, (iii) removing HBr as it forms? (b) Why does pressure not affect the yield?

Show answer

(a) (i) yes: adding a reactant shifts right; (ii) no: removing a reactant shifts left; (iii) yes: removing the product shifts right. (b) Both sides have 2 mol of gas.

Review Q2: Ammonia from nitrogen and hydrogen; the heat evolved is 29 kJ mol−1 (as printed). (a) Thermochemical equation. (b) Endothermic or exothermic? (c) Why does the yield (i) rise with pressure, (ii) fall with temperature?

Show answer

(a) $\mathrm{N_2(g) + 3H_2(g) \rightleftharpoons 2NH_3(g)}$, $\Delta H = -29$ kJ mol−1 (heat evolved, so negative; most data books give $-92$ kJ for this equation).

(b) Exothermic. (c) (i) 4 mol of gas become 2 mol, so higher pressure shifts right. (ii) Raising T favours the endothermic reverse reaction, so less NH3.

Review Q3: How would you set the temperature and pressure to get more HCl in $\mathrm{2Cl_2(g) + 2H_2O(g) \rightleftharpoons 4HCl(g) + O_2(g)}$, $\Delta H$ positive?

Show answer

High temperature (favours the endothermic forward reaction) and low pressure (4 mol of gas on the left, 5 mol on the right).

Exercises 4–7: (4) $\mathrm{CH_3COOH(l) + C_2H_5OH(l) \rightleftharpoons CH_3COOC_2H_5(l) + H_2O(l)}$: (i) some CH3COOH removed, (ii) more ethanol added. (5) Decreasing pressure on (a) $\mathrm{2H_2O(g) + N_2(g) \rightleftharpoons 2H_2(g) + 2NO(g)}$, (b) $\mathrm{CO(g) + 2H_2(g) \rightleftharpoons CH_3OH(g)}$. (6) Most CaCO3 from $\mathrm{CaO(s) + CO_2(g) \rightleftharpoons CaCO_3(s)}$, $\Delta H$ positive (as printed). (7) For $\mathrm{2SO_2 + O_2 \rightleftharpoons 2SO_3}$, $\Delta H$ negative: pressure, temperature, catalyst?

Show answer

(4) (i) Shifts left (to replace the acid). (ii) Shifts right (to use up the ethanol), more ester.

(5) (a) 3 mol of gas on the left, 4 on the right: lower pressure shifts right. (b) 3 mol on the left, 1 on the right: lower pressure shifts left (less methanol).

(6) With $\Delta H$ positive as printed: high temperature, and high pressure (1 mol of gas on the left, none on the right). (In reality, CaO + CO2 → CaCO3 is exothermic, so a lower temperature would favour it; follow the sign given in the question.)

(7) (i) High pressure (3 mol → 2 mol). (ii) Low temperature (exothermic forward); in practice about 450 °C as a compromise for speed. (iii) No change in yield; the catalyst only makes equilibrium come sooner.

Exercises 8–10: (8) Heating $\mathrm{N_2O_4 \rightleftharpoons 2NO_2}$ makes it browner: which direction is favoured, and what is the sign of ΔH? (9) Raising T for (a) $\mathrm{CO + H_2O \rightleftharpoons CO_2 + H_2}$, ΔH −; (b) $\mathrm{H_2 + I_2 \rightleftharpoons 2HI}$, ΔH −. (10) Why does lowering T raise the SO3 yield, and does V2O5 change it?

Show answer

(8) The forward reaction (more NO2); raising T favours the endothermic direction, so ΔH is positive.

(9) Both are exothermic forward, so heating shifts both to the left.

(10) The forward reaction is exothermic; lowering T makes the system release heat by shifting right. V2O5 does not change the percentage yield; it only speeds up reaching equilibrium.

Key Takeaways
  • An equilibrium shifts to oppose a change in concentration, temperature or pressure.
  • Heat favours the endothermic direction; pressure favours fewer gas moles.
  • A catalyst does not move the equilibrium; it only gets there faster.
  • Industry chooses compromise conditions: yield (equilibrium) against speed (kinetics) and cost.
