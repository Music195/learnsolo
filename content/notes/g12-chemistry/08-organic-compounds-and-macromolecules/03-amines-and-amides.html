Amines and Amides

Concept — Nitrogen Compounds from Ammonia and Acids
  • Amines: one or more H atoms of NH₃ replaced by alkyl groups. Primary (1°) R–NH₂, secondary (2°) R₂NH, tertiary (3°) R₃N, by the number of alkyl groups on N. Simplest: methylamine CH₃NH₂.
    • Names: common names list the alkyl groups alphabetically + "amine" (ethylmethylamine). IUPAC: replace -e of the chain by -amine; groups on N get the prefix N-: C₃H₇NH₂ propanamine (propylamine), C₃H₇NHCH₃ N-methylpropanamine, C₃H₇N(CH₃)₂ N,N-dimethylpropanamine.
    • Preparation: heat bromoethane with excess ammonia in ethanol in a sealed tube: C₂H₅Br + NH₃ → C₂H₅NH₂ + HBr (the excess stops 2° and 3° amines and ammonium salts forming). Bromoethane itself: C₂H₅OH + HBr → C₂H₅Br + H₂O (a little H₂SO₄).
    • Properties: methyl-, dimethyl-, trimethyl- and ethylamine are gases; most others are liquids, heavy ones solids. Gaseous amines smell like ammonia, liquid ones fishy. 1° amines form more hydrogen bonds, so they dissolve better than 2° and 3°. 1° and 2° amines boil higher than alkanes or ethers of similar mass (N–H hydrogen bonds) but lower than alcohols (O is more electronegative than N).
    • Carbylamine test for 1° amines: C₂H₅NH₂ + CHCl₃ + 3KOH → C₂H₅NC (ethyl isocyanide, foul smell) + 3KCl + 3H₂O.
    • Uses: azo dyes, nylon, medicines and drugs, crop protection, water purification.
  • Amides: the –OH of a carboxylic acid replaced by –NH₂, –NHR or –NR₂: R–CO–NH₂. Simplest common one: ethanamide CH₃CONH₂.
    • Names: -oic acid → -amide, N- for groups on nitrogen: C₂H₅CONH₂ propanamide, C₂H₅CONHCH₃ N-methylpropanamide, C₂H₅CON(CH₃)₂ N,N-dimethylpropanamide.
    • Preparation (condensation): acid + ammonia → amide + water. With excess ethanoic acid: CH₃COOH + NH₃ → CH₃COONH₄, then heating gives CH₃CONH₂ + H₂O (the excess acid stops the ammonium salt dissociating before it dehydrates).
    • Properties: methanamide is a liquid, the rest are solids with high melting and boiling points; up to C₅ they dissolve in water; solutions are neutral.
    • Hydrolysis (reflux): with HCl → carboxylic acid + the ammonium salt of the amine; with NaOH → sodium salt of the acid + the amine.
    • Uses: plastics, rubber, paper, colours in crayons, pencils and ink, water and sewage treatment; nylon (a polyamide) for textiles, ropes, bulletproof vests, tyre cords. Paracetamol is an amide.

Common Mistakes to Avoid
  • 1°/2°/3° amines vs alcohols: for amines it counts the alkyl groups on N; for alcohols it counts groups on the C bearing –OH.
  • Amine vs amide: an amide has C=O next to the N (–CONH–) and is neutral; amines are basic like ammonia.

CH₃CH₂CH₂COOH: carboxylic acid, butanoic acid. H₂NCH₂CH₃: amine, ethanamine. CH₃COOCH₂CH₂CH₃: ester, propyl ethanoate. CH₃CHO: aldehyde, ethanal.

Warm the aldehyde gently with Fehling's solution: the clear blue solution gives a reddish-brown precipitate (Cu₂O). Ketones give no precipitate.

Extra (from the text): Why is excess ammonia used to make ethanamine from bromoethane, and why does ethanamine boil lower than ethanol?

Show answer

Excess hot ethanolic ammonia stops the ethanamine reacting with more bromoethane to give secondary and tertiary amines and ammonium salts. Ethanamine has N–H hydrogen bonds, but O is more electronegative than N, so the O–H hydrogen bonds in ethanol are stronger and ethanol boils higher.

Key Takeaways
  • Amines: 1° RNH₂, 2° R₂NH, 3° R₃N; names with -amine and N-; made from haloalkane + excess NH₃.
  • Boiling points: alcohols > 1° amines > 2° amines > 3° amines ≈ ethers ≈ alkanes (similar mass).
  • Amides R–CONH₂: from acid + NH₃ (condensation); hydrolysed by acid or alkali.
