Identifying Functional Groups: Tests and IR Spectra

Concept — A Test-Tube Clue, or a Spectrum

A functional group is the group of atoms on the carbon skeleton that gives a compound its typical reactions: C=C, C≡C, C=O (carbonyl), –OH (hydroxyl), –COOH (carboxyl), –COOR (carboalkoxy), –NH₂ (amine), –CONH₂ (carboxamide). It can be identified by chemical tests or by spectroscopy (IR, UV/visible, NMR, mass spectrometry; only IR is described here).

Chemical tests (Table 8.1) are quick test-tube reactions with a visible clue (colour change, precipitate or gas):

  • alkane: 1 % KMnO₄ is not decolourised; alkene: KMnO₄ decolourised
  • alkyne (HC≡C–): ammoniacal Cu₂Cl₂ gives a red precipitate
  • alcohol: warm acidified K₂Cr₂O₇ turns from orange to green
  • ether: boil with HI, then AgNO₃ gives a yellow precipitate (AgI)
  • aldehyde: warm Fehling's solution (blue, alkaline Cu²⁺) gives a reddish-brown precipitate (Cu₂O); ketone: no precipitate
  • aldehyde or ketone: 2,4-DNP gives a deep orange precipitate (a 2,4-dinitrophenylhydrazone)
  • carboxylic acid: saturated NaHCO₃ gives CO₂ (turns limewater milky)
  • ester: heat with ethanol, phenolphthalein and dil. NaOH (pink): the pink is decolourised as the NaOH is used up
  • primary amine: CHCl₃ + alkali gives an unpleasant smell (carbylamine test)
  • primary amide: boil with 20 % NaOH gives NH₃ (white fumes with HCl)

Infrared spectroscopy is fast, uses tiny samples, is non-destructive and gives more structural detail.

  • Radiation: $E = h\nu = hc/\lambda$. IR uses the wavenumber $\bar\nu = 1/\lambda$ (cm⁻¹). High frequency = large wavenumber = short wavelength = high energy.
  • Vibrations: IR photons make bonds stretch (bond length changes) or bend (bond angle changes). Only vibrations that change the dipole moment absorb IR, so N₂ is IR inactive and CO is IR active. Light atoms and multiple bonds vibrate at higher frequencies; stretching needs more energy than bending.
  • The spectrum plots % transmittance against wavenumber (4000 → 500 cm⁻¹, falling to the right); each dip is an absorption. More polar bonds and more bonds give stronger bands; hydrogen bonding makes O–H bands broad. N–H appears in the same region as O–H but is narrower and weaker.
Table 8.2 — Characteristic IR Bands
$$E = h\nu = \frac{hc}{\lambda} = hc\,\bar\nu \qquad \bar\nu = \frac{1}{\lambda}$$
  • C–O (alcohols, ethers, esters, acids): 1050–1410 cm⁻¹, strong
  • C=C (alkenes, aromatics): 1620–1680, medium to weak
  • C=O (aldehydes, ketones, acids, esters, amides): 1650–1750, strong and sharp
  • C≡C (alkynes): 2100–2260, medium to weak
  • O–H (carboxylic acids): 2500–3300, strong and very broad
  • C–H (aldehydes): 2720–2820, medium to weak
  • C–H (alkanes, alkenes): 2850–3090, strong
  • O–H (alcohols): 3200–3600, strong and broad
  • N–H (amines, amides): 3300–3500, narrower and weaker than O–H
Common Mistakes to Avoid
  • 2,4-DNP alone: it shows a carbonyl group but does not tell an aldehyde from a ketone; Fehling's does.
  • Reading the axis: IR wavenumbers decrease from left to right.
  • Energy and wavelength: shorter wavelength means higher energy.

Worked Examples — Review Questions (p. 144)

Review Q1: Name one reagent that distinguishes propanal from propanone.

Show answer

Fehling's solution: warm with propanal gives a reddish-brown precipitate; propanone gives none.

Review Q2: Which is higher in energy? (i) Wavenumber 100 cm⁻¹ or 2000 cm⁻¹. (ii) Wavelength 950 nm or 850 nm.

Show answer

$E = hc\bar\nu$ grows with wavenumber; $E = hc/\lambda$ grows as wavelength shrinks.

(i) 2000 cm⁻¹ (20 times the energy of 100 cm⁻¹). (ii) 850 nm ($950/850 = 1.12$ times the energy of 950 nm).

Review Q3: A spectrum shows absorptions at 1700 cm⁻¹ and 1200 cm⁻¹. Which fits: CH₃COOCH₃, CH₃COOH, CH₃CH₂OH?

Show answer

1700 cm⁻¹ is C=O; 1200 cm⁻¹ is C–O. CH₃CH₂OH has no C=O. CH₃COOH would also show a very broad O–H band at 2500–3300 cm⁻¹, which is not listed.

CH₃COOCH₃ (methyl ethanoate, an ester).

Worked Examples — Exercises 1(c), 3(c), 4(d), 5(b), 13, 14 (pp. 152–154)

Short answers: (1c) TRUE or FALSE: the greater the polarity of a bond, the stronger its IR absorption. (3c) A broad band at 3200 cm⁻¹ and a sharp band at 1711 cm⁻¹: ester, alcohol or carboxylic acid? (4d) Stretching is matched with? (5b) The instrument used to obtain an IR spectrum?

Show answer

(1c) TRUE. (3c) carboxylic acid (broad H-bonded O–H plus C=O). (4d) an IR vibrational mode. (5b) an IR spectrometer.

Exercise 13: How could IR distinguish CH₃CH₂CH₂OH from CH₃CH₂OCH₃?

Show answer

Propan-1-ol shows a strong, broad O–H band at 3200–3600 cm⁻¹. Methoxyethane (an ether) has no O–H, so no band there; it shows only C–H (2850–3090) and C–O (1050–1410 cm⁻¹).

Exercise 14: Spectrum 1 has a strong, broad band near 3300 cm⁻¹ and no band near 1700 cm⁻¹; spectrum 2 has a strong, sharp band near 1700 cm⁻¹ and nothing broad above 3000 cm⁻¹. Which is butanone and which is butanol?

Show answer

Spectrum 1 is butanol (broad O–H stretch of a hydrogen-bonded alcohol). Spectrum 2 is butanone (strong C=O stretch at about 1700 cm⁻¹, no O–H).

Key Takeaways
  • Key tests: KMnO₄ (C=C), Fehling's (aldehyde), 2,4-DNP (C=O), NaHCO₃ (COOH), K₂Cr₂O₇ (alcohol).
  • IR: broad O–H 3200–3600 (alcohol) or 2500–3300 (acid); sharp C=O 1650–1750; C–O 1050–1410.
  • Higher wavenumber = higher energy; only dipole-changing vibrations absorb IR.
