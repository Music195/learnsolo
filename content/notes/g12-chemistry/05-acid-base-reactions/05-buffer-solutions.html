Buffer Solutions

Concept — A Solution That Resists pH Change

Blood stays at about pH 7.4 and gastric juice at about 1.5; enzymes only work at the right pH. A buffer solution resists changes in pH when it is diluted or when a small amount of acid or base is added. There are two kinds:

  • A weak acid and its salt (acidic buffers, e.g. CH3COOH / CH3COONa). The salt gives a large reserve of CH3COO⁻; the acid is a large reserve of H⁺.
    • Add H⁺: $\mathrm{H^+ + CH_3COO^- \to CH_3COOH}$ (the added H⁺ is used up)
    • Add OH⁻: $\mathrm{OH^- + CH_3COOH \to CH_3COO^- + H_2O}$ (the added OH⁻ is used up)
  • A weak base and its salt (pH about 7–11, e.g. NH4OH / NH4Cl).
    • Add OH⁻: $\mathrm{OH^- + NH_4^+ \to NH_4OH}$
    • Add H⁺: $\mathrm{H^+ + NH_4OH \to NH_4^+ + H_2O}$

A strong acid with its salt (HCl/NaCl) is not a buffer: Cl⁻ is too weak a base to take up added H⁺.

Core Formula
$$K_a = \frac{[\mathrm{H^+}][\mathrm{A^-}]}{[\mathrm{HA}]} \;\Rightarrow\; [\mathrm{H^+}] = K_a\,\frac{[\mathrm{HA}]}{[\mathrm{A^-}]} \qquad \mathrm{pH} = \mathrm{p}K_a + \log\frac{[\mathrm{A^-}]}{[\mathrm{HA}]}$$
  • $[\mathrm{HA}]$ ≈ the acid concentration put in; $[\mathrm{A^-}]$ ≈ the salt concentration put in
  • Equal amounts of acid and salt: $\mathrm{pH} = \mathrm{p}K_a$ (4.74 for ethanoic acid)
  • Only the ratio matters, so diluting a buffer hardly changes its pH
Common Mistakes to Avoid
  • Upside-down ratio: it is $\log([\text{salt}]/[\text{acid}])$; more salt raises the pH.
  • "A buffer never changes": it only resists small additions. Once the A⁻ (or HA) reserve is used up, the pH jumps.
  • Grams, not moles: convert masses to mol (and to mol dm⁻³ if the volumes differ) before the ratio.

Worked Examples — Examples 10–12 and Review Questions (pp. 92–93)

Example 10: Which are buffer solutions? (i) HCN/NaCN, (ii) HBr/KBr, (iii) HCOOH/HCOOK, (iv) HClO4/NaClO4.

Show answer

(i) Buffer: HCN is a weak acid and NaCN is its salt. (ii) Not a buffer: HBr is a strong acid. (iii) Buffer: HCOOH is a weak acid and HCOO⁻ is its conjugate base. (iv) Not a buffer: HClO4 is a strong acid.

Example 11: pH of a buffer with 0.01 mol ethanoic acid and 0.01 mol sodium ethanoate per dm³ ($K_a = 1.8 \times 10^{-5}$).

Show answer

$[\mathrm{CH_3COOH}] = [\mathrm{CH_3COO^-}] = 0.01$, so $[\mathrm{H^+}] = K_a \times \frac{0.01}{0.01} = 1.8 \times 10^{-5}$ mol dm⁻³.

$\mathrm{pH} = -\log(1.8 \times 10^{-5}) = \mathbf{4.74}$

Example 12: A sodium ethanoate–ethanoic acid buffer has pH 4.50. Find $[\mathrm{CH_3COO^-}]/[\mathrm{CH_3COOH}]$.

Show answer

$[\mathrm{H^+}] = 10^{-4.50} = 3.16 \times 10^{-5}$ mol dm⁻³.

$\dfrac{[\mathrm{CH_3COO^-}]}{[\mathrm{CH_3COOH}]} = \dfrac{K_a}{[\mathrm{H^+}]} = \dfrac{1.8 \times 10^{-5}}{3.16 \times 10^{-5}} = \mathbf{0.570}$ (less salt than acid, because pH 4.50 < pKa 4.74)

Review Q1: Which are buffers? (i) H2CO3/HCO3−, (ii) HCl/NaCl, (iii) NH3/NaOH, (iv) HF/NaF.

Show answer

(i) Yes: weak acid and its conjugate base (the buffer in blood). (ii) No: HCl is a strong acid. (iii) No: NaOH is a strong base, not the salt (conjugate acid) of NH3; it would need NH4Cl. (iv) Yes: HF is a weak acid and F⁻ its conjugate base.

Review Q2: A methanoic acid / sodium methanoate buffer has a little dilute HCl added. What happens to the extra H⁺?

Show answer

The methanoate ions take them up: $\mathrm{H^+ + HCOO^- \to HCOOH}$. The H⁺ becomes undissociated methanoic acid, so $[\mathrm{H^+}]$ and the pH hardly change.

Review Q3: 0.2 g of HF and 0.42 g of NaF in 0.2 dm³ of water. pH? (pKa of HF = 3.14; Na 23, H 1, F 19)

Show answer

HF: $0.2 / 20 = 0.010$ mol. NaF: $0.42 / 42 = 0.010$ mol. Same volume, so the ratio is $1$.

$\mathrm{pH} = 3.14 + \log 1 = \mathbf{3.14}$

Exercises 1(e), 2(e), 13 and 14: (1e) A buffer resists the change of ___. (2e) Which combination buffers at about pH 9: CH3COOH/CH3COONa, NH3/NH4Cl or HCl/NaCl? (13b) pH of 0.01 mol methanoic acid (pKa 3.8) and 0.1 mol sodium methanoate per dm³. (14) 2.2 g of sodium ethanoate added to 0.1 dm³ of 0.4 mol dm⁻³ ethanoic acid ($K_a = 1.8 \times 10^{-5}$). pH?

Show answer

(1e) pH. (2e) NH3/NH4Cl (a weak base and its salt; pH = 14 − 4.74 = 9.26 for equal amounts).

(13a) NH4+ removes added OH⁻ ($\mathrm{OH^- + NH_4^+ \to NH_4OH}$) and NH4OH removes added H⁺ ($\mathrm{H^+ + NH_4OH \to NH_4^+ + H_2O}$).

(13b) $\mathrm{pH} = 3.8 + \log\frac{0.1}{0.01} = 3.8 + 1 = \mathbf{4.8}$.

(14) CH3COONa: $M = 82$, $2.2 / 82 = 0.0268$ mol in 0.1 dm³ $= 0.268$ mol dm⁻³. $\mathrm{p}K_a = -\log(1.8 \times 10^{-5}) = 4.74$.

$\mathrm{pH} = 4.74 + \log\frac{0.268}{0.4} = 4.74 - 0.17 = \mathbf{4.57}$

Key Takeaways
  • Buffer = weak acid + its salt, or weak base + its salt.
  • The salt's ion removes added H⁺; the weak acid (or base) removes added OH⁻ (or H⁺).
  • $\mathrm{pH} = \mathrm{p}K_a + \log([\text{salt}]/[\text{acid}])$; equal amounts give pH = pKa.
