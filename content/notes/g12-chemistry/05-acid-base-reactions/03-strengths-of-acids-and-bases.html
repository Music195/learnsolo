Strengths of Acids and Bases: Ka and Kb

Concept — How Much Does It Ionise?

Strength means how completely an acid or base ionises in water, not how concentrated it is.

  • Strong acids (strong electrolytes, mostly inorganic) ionise completely: HCl, HNO3, HClO4, H2SO4 (first step: $\mathrm{H_2SO_4 \to H^+ + HSO_4^-}$; HSO4− can still lose its second H⁺).
  • Weak acids (weak electrolytes, most organic acids) ionise only partly: HF, HOCl, CH3COOH, HCOOH: $\mathrm{HF + H_2O \rightleftharpoons H_3O^+ + F^-}$.
  • Basicity of an acid = the number of H⁺ ions one molecule can give: monoprotic (monobasic) HCl, CH3COOH; diprotic (dibasic) H2SO4, H2CO3; triprotic (tribasic) H3PO4.
  • Strong bases: hydroxides of Li, Na, K and of Ca, Sr, Ba, which ionise completely: $\mathrm{Ba(OH)_2 \to Ba^{2+} + 2OH^-}$. Weak bases: ammonia, methylamine, aniline, pyridine: $\mathrm{NH_3 + H_2O \rightleftharpoons NH_4^+ + OH^-}$. An alkali is a base that dissolves in water.
  • Acidity of a base = the number of molecules of a monoprotic acid (like HCl) that one molecule of base neutralises: NaOH 1, Ca(OH)2 2.
  • Acid dissociation constant $K_a$ for $\mathrm{HA \rightleftharpoons H^+ + A^-}$: the larger $K_a$, the stronger the acid. $\mathrm{p}K_a = -\log K_a$: the smaller $\mathrm{p}K_a$, the stronger the acid. Example order: HSO4− ($1.3 \times 10^{-2}$) > HNO2 ($4.5 \times 10^{-4}$) > CH3COOH ($1.8 \times 10^{-5}$).
  • Base dissociation constant $K_b$ for $\mathrm{B + H_2O \rightleftharpoons HB^+ + OH^-}$ (water is left out: it is in huge excess). Example order: NH3 ($1.8 \times 10^{-5}$) > CH3COO− ($5.6 \times 10^{-10}$) > NO2− ($2.2 \times 10^{-11}$).
Core Formulas
$$K_a = \frac{[\mathrm{H^+}][\mathrm{A^-}]}{[\mathrm{HA}]} \qquad \mathrm{p}K_a = -\log K_a$$ $$K_b = \frac{[\mathrm{HB^+}][\mathrm{OH^-}]}{[\mathrm{B}]} \qquad \mathrm{p}K_b = -\log K_b$$ $$\text{weak acid of concentration } c:\quad [\mathrm{H^+}] \approx \sqrt{K_a\,c} \qquad \text{weak base:}\quad [\mathrm{OH^-}] \approx \sqrt{K_b\,c}$$
  • Strong monoprotic acid: $[\mathrm{H^+}] = c$. Strong base M(OH)n: $[\mathrm{OH^-}] = n\,c$
  • The square-root shortcut assumes so little ionises that $[\mathrm{HA}] \approx c$ (good when ionisation is under about 5%)
Common Mistakes to Avoid
  • Strong ≠ concentrated: 0.001 M HCl is dilute but strong; 1 M CH3COOH is concentrated but weak.
  • pKa direction: a larger pKa means a weaker acid.
  • Diprotic bases: 0.012 M Ca(OH)2 gives $[\mathrm{OH^-}] = 0.024$ M.

Worked Examples — Textbook Examples 5–8 (pp. 85–87)

Example 5: [H⁺], [OH⁻] and pH of 0.02 mol dm−3 HNO3.

Show solution

Strong acid: $[\mathrm{H^+}] = 0.02$ mol dm−3; $[\mathrm{OH^-}] = \dfrac{1.0 \times 10^{-14}}{0.02} = 5.0 \times 10^{-13}$ mol dm−3.

pH $= -\log 0.02 = 1.70$

Example 6: pH and pOH of 0.012 mol dm−3 Ca(OH)2 (slaked lime).

Show solution

$[\mathrm{OH^-}] = 2 \times 0.012 = 0.024$ mol dm−3; pOH $= -\log 0.024 = 1.62$.

pH $= 14 - 1.62 = 12.38$

Example 7: Nicotinic acid HA: $[\mathrm{HA}] = 0.049$, $[\mathrm{H_3O^+}] = [\mathrm{A^-}] = 8.4 \times 10^{-4}$ mol dm−3. Find $K_a$ and $\mathrm{p}K_a$.

Show solution

$K_a = \dfrac{(8.4 \times 10^{-4})^2}{0.049} = \dfrac{7.06 \times 10^{-7}}{0.049} = 1.4 \times 10^{-5}$ mol dm−3

$\mathrm{p}K_a = -\log(1.4 \times 10^{-5}) = 4.85$

Example 8: 0.055 mol dm−3 ammonia has $[\mathrm{OH^-}] = 1.0 \times 10^{-3}$ mol dm−3. Find $K_b$.

Show solution

$[\mathrm{NH_4^+}] = [\mathrm{OH^-}] = 1.0 \times 10^{-3}$; so little ionises that $[\mathrm{NH_3}] \approx 0.055$.

$K_b = \dfrac{(1.0 \times 10^{-3})^2}{0.055} = 1.8 \times 10^{-5}$ mol dm−3

Worked Examples — Review Questions (p. 88) and Exercises (p. 95)

Review Q1 and Exercise 11(a): pKa of ethanoic acid ($1.8 \times 10^{-5}$) and HF ($7.2 \times 10^{-4}$). Which is stronger?

Show solution

Ethanoic acid: $-\log(1.8 \times 10^{-5}) = 4.74$. HF: $-\log(7.2 \times 10^{-4}) = 3.14$. HF is stronger (larger $K_a$, smaller pKa).

Review Q2: pH of a solution with 7.3 g HCl per dm3 (H 1, Cl 35.5).

Show solution

$n = 7.3 / 36.5 = 0.20$ mol, so $[\mathrm{H^+}] = 0.20$ mol dm−3 and pH $= -\log 0.20 = 0.70$.

Review Q3: (a) Acid strength and $K_a$? (b) Base strength and pKb? (c) Order HOCl ($3.5 \times 10^{-8}$), HCN ($4.9 \times 10^{-10}$), HNO2 ($4.5 \times 10^{-4}$).

Show answer

(a) The larger $K_a$, the stronger the acid. (b) The smaller pKb, the stronger the base.

(c)(i) Increasing strength: HCN < HOCl < HNO2. (ii) Increasing pKa: HNO2 (3.35) < HOCl (7.46) < HCN (9.31).

Exercises 8 and 9: (8) $K_a$ and pKa of 0.1 mol dm−3 lactic acid of pH 2.4. (9) pOH = 1.88: concentration of (i) KOH, (ii) Ba(OH)2.

Show solution

(8) $[\mathrm{H^+}] = 10^{-2.4} = 3.98 \times 10^{-3}$. With $[\mathrm{HX}] \approx 0.1$: $K_a = (3.98 \times 10^{-3})^2/0.1 = 1.6 \times 10^{-4}$, pKa = 3.8. (Using $0.1 - 0.004 = 0.096$ gives $1.65 \times 10^{-4}$, pKa 3.78.)

(9) $[\mathrm{OH^-}] = 10^{-1.88} = 0.0132$ mol dm−3. (i) KOH: 0.0132 mol dm−3. (ii) Ba(OH)2 gives 2 OH⁻: 0.0066 mol dm−3.

Exercises 10 and 11: (10) pH of 0.24 mol dm−3 weak base with $K_b = 3.5 \times 10^{-6}$. (11b) Higher pH: 0.20 M NH3 ($K_b = 1.8 \times 10^{-5}$) or 0.20 M NaOH? (11c) A weak acid has pKa 5.35: [A⁻] in 0.10 M solution?

Show solution

(10) $\mathrm{B + H_2O \rightleftharpoons HB^+ + OH^-}$, $K_b = \dfrac{[\mathrm{HB^+}][\mathrm{OH^-}]}{[\mathrm{B}]}$. $[\mathrm{OH^-}] = \sqrt{3.5 \times 10^{-6} \times 0.24} = 9.2 \times 10^{-4}$; pOH = 3.04; pH = 10.96.

(11b) NaOH: pH $= 14 + \log 0.20 = 13.30$. NH3: $[\mathrm{OH^-}] = \sqrt{1.8 \times 10^{-5} \times 0.20} = 1.9 \times 10^{-3}$, pH = 11.28. NaOH has the higher pH.

(11c) $K_a = 10^{-5.35} = 4.5 \times 10^{-6}$; $[\mathrm{A^-}] = [\mathrm{H^+}] = \sqrt{4.5 \times 10^{-6} \times 0.10} = 6.7 \times 10^{-4}$ mol dm−3.

Exercise 1(d): [OH⁻] in 0.1 mol dm−3 Ba(OH)2?

Show answer

0.2 mol dm−3 (two OH⁻ per formula).

Key Takeaways
  • Strong acids and bases ionise completely; weak ones only partly.
  • $K_a$ (or $K_b$) measures strength: larger $K$, smaller p$K$ → stronger.
  • Weak acid: $[\mathrm{H^+}] \approx \sqrt{K_a c}$; weak base: $[\mathrm{OH^-}] \approx \sqrt{K_b c}$.
  • Basicity of an acid = H⁺ per molecule; acidity of a base = HCl molecules it neutralises.
