Theories of Acids and Bases

Concept — Three Ways to Say What an Acid Is

Acid–base reactions are everywhere: antacids such as aluminium and magnesium hydroxide soothe the stomach, quicklime (CaO) neutralises acidic soil, limewater absorbs SO2 from power stations. The definitions of "acid" and "base" grew wider as chemists learned more.

  • Arrhenius (1887): an acid produces hydrogen ions H⁺ in water; a base produces hydroxide ions OH⁻ in water.
    • Strong ones ionise completely: $\mathrm{HCl(aq) \to H^+(aq) + Cl^-(aq)}$, $\mathrm{NaOH(aq) \to Na^+(aq) + OH^-(aq)}$.
    • Weak ones ionise only partly: $\mathrm{CH_3COOH(aq) \rightleftharpoons H^+(aq) + CH_3COO^-(aq)}$, $\mathrm{NH_4OH(aq) \rightleftharpoons NH_4^+(aq) + OH^-(aq)}$.
    • Weak points: it only works in water, and cannot explain why $\mathrm{CO_2}$, $\mathrm{SO_2}$, $\mathrm{SO_3}$ (no H) are acidic or why CaO and MgO (no OH) are basic.
  • Brønsted–Lowry (1923): an acid is a proton (H⁺) donor; a base is a proton acceptor. It works in any solvent.
    • Conjugate pairs: when an acid loses a proton, what remains is its conjugate base; when a base gains a proton, it becomes its conjugate acid. The pair differ by exactly one H⁺.
    • $\mathrm{CH_3COOH + H_2O \rightleftharpoons CH_3COO^- + H_3O^+}$: acid 1 + base 2 ⇌ base 1 + acid 2. Water acts as a base here; $\mathrm{H_3O^+}$ is the hydronium ion.
    • $\mathrm{NH_3 + H_2O \rightleftharpoons NH_4^+ + OH^-}$: here water acts as an acid.
    • A strong acid has a weak conjugate base, and a weak acid has a strong(er) conjugate base.
    • Weak point: it cannot explain acid–base reactions without proton transfer (acidic oxides with basic oxides, or $\mathrm{BF_3}$ and $\mathrm{AlCl_3}$ behaving as acids).
  • Lewis (1923): a base is an electron-pair donor; an acid is an electron-pair acceptor. This is the most general definition. In $\mathrm{H_3N{:} + BF_3 \to H_3N{\to}BF_3}$, ammonia gives its lone pair to the electron-deficient boron: NH3 is the Lewis base, BF3 the Lewis acid, and the product is a "Lewis salt" (adduct) held by a coordinate bond.

Common Mistakes to Avoid
  • Conjugate pairs differ by one H⁺ only: the conjugate base of $\mathrm{H_2SO_4}$ is $\mathrm{HSO_4^-}$, not $\mathrm{SO_4^{2-}}$.
  • Pairing the wrong species: in $\mathrm{NH_3 + H_2O \rightleftharpoons NH_4^+ + OH^-}$, the pairs are NH3/NH4+ and H2O/OH−, never NH3/OH−.
  • Water is not always a base: it is a base towards acids and an acid towards bases (amphiprotic).

Review Q1: Which of BF3, H⁺, HF, AlCl3, HCOOH are Lewis acids and which are Brønsted–Lowry acids?

Show answer

Lewis acids (electron-pair acceptors): BF3 and AlCl3 (B and Al have only 6 valence electrons), and H⁺ (an empty orbital that accepts a lone pair, e.g. from H2O).

Brønsted–Lowry acids (proton donors): HF and HCOOH. (Every Brønsted–Lowry acid's H⁺ is itself a Lewis acid, but BF3 and AlCl3 have no proton to give.)

Review Q2: Identify the acid, base and conjugates in (a) $\mathrm{HNO_2 + H_2O \rightleftharpoons H_3O^+ + NO_2^-}$, (b) $\mathrm{CN^- + H_2O \rightleftharpoons HCN + OH^-}$, (c) $\mathrm{H_2SO_4 + Cl^- \rightleftharpoons HCl + HSO_4^-}$.

Show answer

(a) Acid HNO2 / conjugate base NO2−; base H2O / conjugate acid H3O+.

(b) Base CN− / conjugate acid HCN; acid H2O / conjugate base OH−.

(c) Acid H2SO4 / conjugate base HSO4−; base Cl− / conjugate acid HCl.

Review Q3: Why can HCN act as both an Arrhenius acid and a Brønsted–Lowry acid?

Show answer

In water it produces H⁺ ions ($\mathrm{HCN \rightleftharpoons H^+ + CN^-}$), the Arrhenius definition; and it does this by donating a proton (to water), the Brønsted–Lowry definition.

Key Takeaways
  • Arrhenius: H⁺ / OH⁻ in water. Brønsted–Lowry: proton donor / acceptor. Lewis: electron-pair acceptor / donor.
  • A conjugate acid–base pair differs by one H⁺.
  • Water can act as an acid or a base (amphiprotic).
  • Lewis acids such as BF3 and AlCl3 need no hydrogen at all.
