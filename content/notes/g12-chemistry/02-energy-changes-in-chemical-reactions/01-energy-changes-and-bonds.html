Exothermic and Endothermic Reactions

Concept — Where the Heat of a Reaction Comes From

Energy can change from one form to another but can neither be created nor destroyed (Law of Conservation of Energy). An electric motor turns electrical energy into kinetic energy, a battery turns chemical energy into electrical energy, a solar cell turns light into electrical energy. Chemical energy is the energy stored in the bonds of substances, and almost every reaction changes some of it into heat.

  • System and surroundings: the system is the reaction we study; the surroundings are everything else (the water, the beaker, the air).
  • Spontaneous and non-spontaneous: a spontaneous change happens on its own (acid + alkali, iron rusting, ice melting at room temperature). A non-spontaneous one needs energy to start it (magnesium or copper reacting with oxygen must first be heated; once started, it may carry on by itself).
  • Exothermic reactions release heat to the surroundings, so the temperature rises. The products have less energy than the reactants. Examples: a burning candle, burning fuel, neutralisation, respiration, rusting, sulphuric acid poured into water.
  • Endothermic reactions absorb heat from the surroundings, so the temperature falls. The products have more energy than the reactants. Examples: ammonium chloride dissolving in water, melting ice, cooking an egg, evaporating water, thermal decomposition of limestone. A cold pack works this way.
  • Bonds are the reason: first the bonds in the reactants must be broken, which absorbs energy (endothermic). Then new bonds in the products are made, which releases energy (exothermic). The balance of the two decides the overall change.
Core Rule — Energy In versus Energy Out
$$\text{energy absorbed (bonds broken)} \;<\; \text{energy released (bonds made)} \;\Rightarrow\; \textbf{exothermic}$$ $$\text{energy absorbed (bonds broken)} \;>\; \text{energy released (bonds made)} \;\Rightarrow\; \textbf{endothermic}$$ $$\text{net energy change} = \text{energy in} - \text{energy out}$$
  • Count every bond: "2 mol of H–Cl" means twice the H–Cl bond energy
  • A negative net change means energy was given out (exothermic); positive means taken in (endothermic)
  • Energy level diagram: exothermic → products drawn below reactants; endothermic → products above
Common Mistakes to Avoid
  • "Breaking bonds releases energy": it is the other way round. Breaking always absorbs energy; making always releases it.
  • Spontaneous does not mean exothermic: melting ice is spontaneous at room temperature but endothermic.
  • Forgetting the coefficients: in $\mathrm{H_2 + Cl_2 \to 2HCl}$ two H–Cl bonds form, so the energy out is $2 \times 431$, not 431.

Worked Examples — Textbook Example 1 and Review Questions (pp. 29–30)

Example 1: Predict whether $\mathrm{H_2(g) + Cl_2(g) \to 2HCl(g)}$ is exothermic or endothermic. Breaking 1 mol H–H needs 436 kJ, 1 mol Cl–Cl needs 242 kJ; making 1 mol H–Cl releases 431 kJ.

Show step-by-step solution

Energy absorbed for bond breaking $= 1(\text{H–H}) + 1(\text{Cl–Cl}) = 436 + 242 = 678\ \text{kJ}$.

Energy released for bond making $= 2(\text{H–Cl}) = 2 \times 431 = 862\ \text{kJ}$.

$678\ \text{kJ} < 862\ \text{kJ}$, so more energy is released than absorbed: the reaction is exothermic (net $678 - 862 = -184$ kJ).

Review Question 1: What energy transformation takes place in (i) electric bulbs, (ii) fuel cells, (iii) an electric generator, (iv) a steam boiler, (v) a gas turbine?

Show answer

(i) Electrical → light (and heat). (ii) Chemical → electrical. (iii) Mechanical (kinetic) → electrical. (iv) Chemical (burning fuel) → thermal energy of steam. (v) Chemical/thermal energy of hot gas → mechanical (kinetic) energy.

Review Question 2: Exothermic or endothermic? (i) burning a candle, (ii) digestion of food, (iii) cooking an egg, (iv) condensation, (v) cracking alkanes.

Show answer

(i) Exothermic. (ii) Exothermic (food is broken down to release energy). (iii) Endothermic (the egg must keep absorbing heat). (iv) Exothermic (a gas gives out heat as it turns to liquid; the reverse of evaporation). (v) Endothermic (C–C bonds are broken by heating).

Review Question 3: Is $\mathrm{2H_2(g) + O_2(g) \to 2H_2O(l)}$ exothermic or endothermic? O=O 496 kJ, H–H 436 kJ, O–H 465 kJ per mol.

Show step-by-step solution

Bonds broken: 2 H–H and 1 O=O: $2(436) + 496 = 872 + 496 = 1368\ \text{kJ}$.

Bonds made: each $\mathrm{H_2O}$ has two O–H bonds, so 4 O–H: $4(465) = 1860\ \text{kJ}$.

$1368 < 1860$, so the reaction is exothermic (net $1368 - 1860 = -492$ kJ for 2 mol of water).

Key Takeaways
  • Energy is conserved; reactions change chemical energy into heat (or take heat in).
  • Exothermic: heat out, temperature rises, products lower in energy. Endothermic: heat in, temperature falls, products higher.
  • Bond breaking absorbs energy; bond making releases it.
  • Compare total energy in (bonds broken) with total energy out (bonds made), remembering the coefficients.
