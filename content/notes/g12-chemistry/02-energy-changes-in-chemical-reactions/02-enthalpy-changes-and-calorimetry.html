Enthalpy Changes and Calorimetry

Concept — Enthalpy, ΔH and How to Measure It

The total chemical energy (heat content) of a substance is its enthalpy, $H$. We cannot measure $H$ itself, only how much it changes. The enthalpy change $\Delta H$ is the heat absorbed or released by a process at constant pressure, in kJ mol−1.

  • Sign: endothermic → products have more enthalpy → $\Delta H$ is positive (e.g. $\tfrac12\mathrm{N_2} + \tfrac12\mathrm{O_2} \to \mathrm{NO}$). Exothermic → $\Delta H$ is negative (e.g. NaOH + HCl).
  • Standard conditions (symbol $\Delta H^\circ$): 25 °C (298 K) and 1 atm (760 mmHg, 101 kPa).
  • Thermochemical equation: a balanced equation with the physical states (s), (l), (g), (aq) and $\Delta H$, e.g. $\mathrm{C(graphite) + O_2(g) \to CO_2(g)}\quad \Delta H^\circ = -394\ \text{kJ mol}^{-1}$. States matter because $\Delta H$ depends on them. For carbon we write "graphite", its most stable form.
  • Standard enthalpy changes with special names:
    • of reaction $\Delta H^\circ_r$: the molar amounts in the equation react.
    • of formation $\Delta H^\circ_f$: one mole of a compound forms from its elements, e.g. $\mathrm{C(graphite) + \tfrac12 O_2(g) \to CO(g)}$, $\Delta H^\circ_f = -110$ kJ mol−1. For an element in its standard state, $\Delta H^\circ_f = 0$.
    • of combustion $\Delta H^\circ_c$: one mole of a substance burns completely in oxygen. Always negative: $\mathrm{CH_4(g) + 2O_2(g) \to CO_2(g) + 2H_2O(l)}$, $-891$ kJ mol−1.
    • of neutralisation $\Delta H^\circ_n$: an acid and an alkali react to form one mole of water. Always negative: HCl + NaOH, $-57.1$ kJ mol−1.
    • of solution $\Delta H_{sol}$: one mole of a substance dissolves in water.
  • Calorimetry: a calorimeter (for example two nested styrofoam cups with a lid and a thermometer) traps the heat of a reaction in a known mass of water. The temperature change gives the heat, $q = mc\Delta T$. Very accurate enthalpies of combustion come from a bomb calorimeter: the sample is ignited in high-pressure oxygen inside a sealed vessel.
Core Formulas
$$\Delta H = H_{\text{products}} - H_{\text{reactants}}$$ $$q = m\,c\,\Delta T \qquad \Delta T = T_{\text{final}} - T_{\text{initial}}$$ $$\Delta H = \pm\frac{q}{n}\quad\text{(kJ mol}^{-1}\text{)}$$
  • $m$ = mass of water or solution in g (1 cm3 of dilute solution ≈ 1 g); $c = 4.18$ J g−1 °C−1
  • $n$ = moles of the substance dissolved, burnt, or of water formed (for neutralisation)
  • Temperature rises → heat given out → $\Delta H$ negative; temperature falls → heat taken in → $\Delta H$ positive
  • Equal volumes of acid and base: use the average of their starting temperatures as $T_{\text{initial}}$
Common Mistakes to Avoid
  • Sign of ΔH: $q$ from $mc\Delta T$ is the heat gained by the water. The reaction's $\Delta H$ has the opposite sign: a temperature rise means $\Delta H < 0$.
  • Joules and kilojoules: $mc\Delta T$ is in J; divide by 1000 before quoting kJ mol−1.
  • Wrong mass: use the mass of the water (or total solution, e.g. 50 + 50 = 100 g), not the mass of the solid.
  • "Per mole of what?" Neutralisation is per mole of water; formation is per mole of compound; combustion per mole of fuel.

How do we turn a thermometer reading into an enthalpy change in kJ mol−1?

Choose an experiment, set the amounts, and press Add and stir. Watch the thermometer and the temperature–time graph. The readout then works backwards from the measured temperature change: $q = mc\Delta T$, the number of moles, and $\Delta H$ with the right sign. Worked Examples — Textbook Examples and Review Questions (pp. 32–35)

Example 2: Write the thermochemical equations for (i) combustion of graphite ($-394$), (ii) combustion of methane ($-891$), (iii) combustion of ethanol ($-1367$), (iv) formation of ethanol ($-235$), (v) formation of $\mathrm{NaHCO_3(s)}$ ($-951$ kJ mol−1).

Show answer

(i) $\mathrm{C(graphite) + O_2(g) \to CO_2(g)}\quad \Delta H^\circ_c = -394\ \text{kJ mol}^{-1}$

(ii) $\mathrm{CH_4(g) + 2O_2(g) \to CO_2(g) + 2H_2O(l)}\quad \Delta H^\circ_c = -891\ \text{kJ mol}^{-1}$

(iii) $\mathrm{C_2H_5OH(l) + 3O_2(g) \to 2CO_2(g) + 3H_2O(l)}\quad \Delta H^\circ_c = -1367\ \text{kJ mol}^{-1}$

(iv) $\mathrm{2C(graphite) + 3H_2(g) + \tfrac12 O_2(g) \to C_2H_5OH(l)}\quad \Delta H^\circ_f = -235\ \text{kJ mol}^{-1}$

(v) $\mathrm{Na(s) + \tfrac12 H_2(g) + C(graphite) + \tfrac32 O_2(g) \to NaHCO_3(s)}\quad \Delta H^\circ_f = -951\ \text{kJ mol}^{-1}$

For formation, exactly one mole of product is made from elements, so fractions such as $\tfrac12\mathrm{O_2}$ are normal.

Example 3: 7.10 g of ammonium nitrate dissolves in 100 g of water; the temperature falls from 18.2 °C to 12.8 °C. Find the enthalpy change of solution. (N = 14, H = 1, O = 16)

Show step-by-step solution

$q = mc\Delta T = 100 \times 4.18 \times (12.8 - 18.2) = 100 \times 4.18 \times (-5.4) = -2257\ \text{J} = -2.257\ \text{kJ}$

The temperature fell, so heat was absorbed: endothermic, $\Delta H$ positive.

$M(\mathrm{NH_4NO_3}) = 14 + 4(1) + 14 + 3(16) = 80\ \text{g mol}^{-1}$, so $n = 7.10/80 = 0.08875$ mol.

$\Delta H_{sol} = +\dfrac{2.257}{0.08875} = +25.4\ \text{kJ mol}^{-1}$ (the textbook writes 25.43).

Example 4: 50 cm3 of 2.0 mol dm−3 HCl (17.5 °C) and 50 cm3 of 2.0 mol dm−3 NaOH (17.9 °C) are mixed; the final temperature is 31.0 °C. Find $\Delta H_n$.

Show step-by-step solution

Average starting temperature $= (17.5 + 17.9)/2 = 17.7$ °C, so $\Delta T = 31.0 - 17.7 = 13.3$ °C.

$q = (50 + 50) \times 4.18 \times 13.3 = 5559\ \text{J} = 5.559\ \text{kJ}$ released (temperature rose: exothermic).

$n(\mathrm{HCl}) = n(\mathrm{NaOH}) = \dfrac{50}{1000} \times 2.0 = 0.10$ mol, which forms 0.10 mol of water.

$\Delta H_n = -\dfrac{5.559}{0.10} = -55.6\ \text{kJ mol}^{-1}$

Review Question 1: Nitrogen and oxygen in car engines form $\mathrm{NO_2}$ with $\Delta H = +33.2$ kJ mol−1. (i) Write the equation. (ii) Exothermic or endothermic? (iii) What energy changes occur in bond breaking and forming?

Show answer

(i) $\mathrm{\tfrac12 N_2(g) + O_2(g) \to NO_2(g)}\quad \Delta H = +33.2\ \text{kJ mol}^{-1}$ (or doubled: $\mathrm{N_2 + 2O_2 \to 2NO_2}$, $+66.4$ kJ).

(ii) Endothermic ($\Delta H$ positive).

(iii) Breaking N≡N and O=O absorbs energy (endothermic); forming the N–O bonds releases energy (exothermic). Here more is absorbed than released.

Exercises 5 and 6 (p. 44): (5a) Burning 0.45 g of propane raises 100 cm3 of water by 20 °C. (5b) How many moles of ethanol ($\Delta H_c = -1367$) heat 500 g of water from 18 °C to 100 °C? (6a) 8.0 g NaCl in 40 cm3 of water: 22.0 → 20.5 °C. (6b) 75 cm3 of 2.0 M ethanoic acid (18.2 °C) + 75 cm3 of 2.0 M ammonium hydroxide (18.6 °C) → 31.0 °C.

Show step-by-step solutions

(5a) $q = 100 \times 4.18 \times 20 = 8360\ \text{J}$, so $\Delta H = -8.36$ kJ for 0.45 g. (Per mole: $n = 0.45/44 = 0.0102$ mol, $-8.36/0.0102 \approx -817$ kJ mol−1.)

(5b) $q = 500 \times 4.18 \times (100 - 18) = 171\,380\ \text{J} = 171.4\ \text{kJ}$; $n = 171.4 / 1367 = 0.125$ mol of ethanol (about 5.8 g).

(6a) $q = 40 \times 4.18 \times (22.0 - 20.5) = 40 \times 4.18 \times 1.5 = 251\ \text{J}$ absorbed (3 s.f.).

(6b) Average start $= (18.2 + 18.6)/2 = 18.4$ °C, $\Delta T = 12.6$ °C, $m = 150$ g. $q = 150 \times 4.18 \times 12.6 = 7900\ \text{J} = 7.90$ kJ. $n(\text{water}) = 0.075 \times 2.0 = 0.15$ mol.

$\Delta H_n = -7.90/0.15 = -52.7\ \text{kJ mol}^{-1}$ (a little less than −57 because ethanoic acid and ammonia are weak).

Key Takeaways
  • $\Delta H = H_{\text{products}} - H_{\text{reactants}}$: negative for exothermic, positive for endothermic.
  • Standard conditions: 298 K, 1 atm. Formation and combustion are per mole of compound or fuel; neutralisation per mole of water.
  • Calorimetry: $q = mc\Delta T$ with $c = 4.18$ J g−1 °C−1, then $\Delta H = \pm q/n$.
  • Temperature rise → $\Delta H < 0$; temperature fall → $\Delta H > 0$.
