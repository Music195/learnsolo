Factors Affecting the Rate of Reaction

Concept — Six Ways to Change the Speed

We often want reactions fast but controlled (burning fuel in an engine), or as slow as possible (food spoiling). Collision theory explains each factor:

  • Concentration: more reactant particles in the same volume → more collisions per second → more successful collisions. Zinc reacts slowly with 1.0 mol dm−3 HCl but much faster with 6.0 mol dm−3 HCl.
  • Particle size (surface area): for a solid, the reaction happens only at its surface. Smaller pieces → larger total surface → more collisions per second. Powdered aluminium reacts rapidly with cold NaOH solution while aluminium foil needs warming; small pieces of wood burn faster than a log.
  • Pressure (gases only): at constant temperature, a higher pressure squeezes the gas into a smaller volume, so the particles are closer and collide more often. Pressure has no effect on reactions of only solids and liquids. A pressure cooker cooks faster because the trapped steam is hotter.
  • Temperature: particles move faster, so they collide more often, and, much more importantly, a larger fraction of the collisions has energy ≥ $E_a$. The Boltzmann distribution of molecular energies flattens and spreads out at a higher temperature, and the area beyond $E_a$ grows. For many reactions a 10 °C rise roughly doubles the rate. Eggs boil faster at 100 °C than at 80 °C; a refrigerator slows food spoiling.
  • Catalyst: a substance that changes the rate of a reaction but is chemically unchanged at the end. A positive catalyst provides an alternative pathway with a lower activation energy, so more collisions succeed. Examples: iron (Haber process, NH3), V2O5 or platinised asbestos (Contact process, SO3), MnO2 (O2 from H2O2). A negative catalyst slows a reaction (alcohol slows the oxidation of sodium sulphite).
    • Homogeneous: the same phase as the reactants: NO gas in the lead chamber process, $\mathrm{2SO_2(g) + O_2(g) \xrightarrow{NO} 2SO_3(g)}$.
    • Heterogeneous: a different phase, usually a solid surface: Pt–Rh gauze in the Ostwald process, $\mathrm{4NH_3(g) + 5O_2(g) \xrightarrow{Pt-Rh} 4NO(g) + 6H_2O(g)}$.
    • Enzymes are protein biocatalysts (ptyalin in saliva breaks down starch, pepsin breaks down protein), often speeding reactions by $10^6$ to $10^{12}$ times.
  • Light: photochemical reactions get their activation energy from light; brighter light → faster. Examples: photosynthesis, ozone depletion, decomposition of silver bromide in photographic film, and $\mathrm{H_2 + Cl_2 \to 2HCl}$, which is very slow in the dark but explosive in bright sunlight.

Common Mistakes to Avoid
  • "A catalyst gives the particles more energy": no. It lowers $E_a$ by offering a different route; the energy distribution stays the same.
  • "A catalyst is used up": it is chemically unchanged at the end (it may change physically, e.g. become powdery).
  • "Higher concentration gives the particles more kinetic energy": only temperature changes the kinetic energy; concentration changes how often they collide.
  • Pressure and solids: pressure only matters when gases react.

Worked Examples — Review Questions (p. 57)

Review Q1: (a) List five factors that affect the rate. (b) Why do substances burn more rapidly in pure oxygen than in air?

Show answer

(a) Concentration, particle size (surface area), pressure, temperature, catalyst (and light).

(b) Air is only about 21% oxygen. Pure oxygen has a much higher concentration of $\mathrm{O_2}$ molecules, so there are more collisions per second with the burning substance.

Review Q2: Give reasons: (a) large sugar crystals burn slower than finely ground sugar; (b) powdered $\mathrm{CaCO_3}$ takes 2 min with 0.5 mol dm−3 HCl but 40 s with 1.0 mol dm−3; (c) $\mathrm{H_2 + Cl_2}$ is slow in the dark but explosive in sunlight; (d) food spoils faster at room temperature than in a refrigerator.

Show answer

(a) Ground sugar has a larger surface area, so more oxygen molecules hit it per second.

(b) The more concentrated acid has more $\mathrm{H^+}$ ions per unit volume, so collisions with the carbonate surface are more frequent (here 3 times faster: 120 s → 40 s).

(c) It is a photochemical reaction: light supplies the activation energy and starts a chain reaction.

(d) At a lower temperature, fewer molecules have energy ≥ $E_a$ and they collide less often, so the spoiling reactions (often enzyme or bacterial) are slower.

Review Q3: What is the difference between homogeneous and heterogeneous catalysts? Give two examples of each.

Show answer

Homogeneous: the same phase as the reactants, e.g. NO(g) in $\mathrm{2SO_2(g) + O_2(g) \to 2SO_3(g)}$ (lead chamber process); dissolved enzymes (ptyalin, pepsin) in aqueous digestive reactions.

Heterogeneous: a different phase (usually a solid with gases or liquids), e.g. Pt–Rh in the Ostwald process; iron in the Haber process; $\mathrm{V_2O_5}$ in the Contact process; $\mathrm{MnO_2}$ with $\mathrm{H_2O_2}$ solution.

Worked Examples — Exercises (pp. 58–60)

Exercise 1 (True/False): (a) The unit of rate depends only on the physical states of reactants. (b) A rate is always positive. (c) Increasing concentration increases kinetic energy. (d) Catalysts raise $E_a$. (e) Gas volumes are greatly affected by pressure.

Show answer

(a) FALSE: it depends on what is measured (mass, volume or concentration) and the time unit. (b) TRUE. (c) FALSE: concentration increases the collision frequency; temperature increases kinetic energy. (d) FALSE: catalysts lower the activation energy. (e) TRUE.

Exercises 8 and 10: (8) A glowing splint burns more vigorously in pure oxygen; would burning sawdust behave the same? (10) For $\mathrm{X + Y \to products}$ (gases): (i) pressure doubled, (ii) molecules of X doubled, (iii) temperature decreased at constant volume.

Show answer

(8) Higher oxygen concentration → more collisions → faster burning; the splint relights. Sawdust burns even faster: the same effect, plus a much larger surface area.

(10) (i) Faster: the same molecules in half the volume collide more often. (ii) Faster: a higher concentration of X. (iii) Slower: fewer molecules reach $E_a$, and collisions are less frequent.

Exercises 11 and 13: (11) Balance $\mathrm{NH_3 + O_2 \xrightarrow{Pt-Rh} NO + H_2O}$; which factors affect it; what type of catalyst is Pt–Rh? (13) Is $\mathrm{2SO_2(g) + O_2(g) \xrightarrow{V_2O_5} 2SO_3(g)}$ heterogeneous or homogeneous? How does a catalyst work?

Show answer

(11) $\mathrm{4NH_3(g) + 5O_2(g) \to 4NO(g) + 6H_2O(g)}$. Temperature, pressure (gases), concentration of reactants and the catalyst. Pt–Rh is a solid with gaseous reactants: a heterogeneous catalyst.

(13) Heterogeneous: solid $\mathrm{V_2O_5}$ with gaseous reactants. A catalyst provides an alternative pathway with a lower activation energy, so a larger fraction of collisions are successful and the rate increases.

Key Takeaways
  • Concentration, pressure and surface area increase the collision frequency.
  • Temperature mainly increases the fraction of molecules with energy ≥ $E_a$ (≈ doubling per 10 °C).
  • A catalyst lowers $E_a$ by an alternative route and is unchanged at the end; homogeneous = same phase, heterogeneous = different phase.
  • Photochemical reactions are driven by light; brighter light → faster.
