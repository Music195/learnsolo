Collision Theory and Activation Energy

Concept — Only Some Collisions Work

Collision theory is based on the kinetic theory: reactant particles (atoms, molecules or ions) move in random directions and keep colliding. But most collisions just bounce. A reaction only happens when particles collide

  1. with the correct orientation: the parts that must bond have to meet, and
  2. with at least a minimum energy, the activation energy $E_a$, which is enough to start breaking the old bonds.

Such a collision is a successful (effective) collision. The rate of reaction depends on how many successful collisions happen per second, so a reaction speeds up if (i) collisions become more frequent, or (ii) a larger fraction of particles has energy above $E_a$.

  • Activated complex: a short-lived, unstable, high-energy arrangement of atoms formed during a successful collision (the transition state). It can go on to products or break apart back into reactants. Example: $\mathrm{H_2(g) + I_2(g) \to [H_2I_2] \to 2HI(g)}$.
  • Energy profile: energy plotted against the reaction coordinate. $E_a$ is the height of the barrier from the reactants up to the activated complex; $\Delta H$ is the difference between the products and the reactants. For $\mathrm{H_2 + I_2}$ the products are lower, so the reaction is exothermic.
  • High $E_a$ → few collisions have enough energy → slow reaction. Low $E_a$ → many collisions succeed → fast reaction.
Core Ideas
$$\text{rate} \;\propto\; (\text{collisions per second}) \times (\text{fraction with } E \ge E_a) \times (\text{fraction correctly oriented})$$ $$\Delta H = E(\text{products}) - E(\text{reactants}) \qquad E_a = E(\text{activated complex}) - E(\text{reactants})$$
  • $E_a$ is always positive; $\Delta H$ can be negative (exothermic) or positive (endothermic)
  • For an exothermic reaction, the activation energy of the reverse reaction is $E_a - \Delta H$, larger than $E_a$
Common Mistakes to Avoid
  • "Every collision gives products": only a tiny fraction of collisions are successful.
  • Mixing up $E_a$ and $\Delta H$: $E_a$ is measured from the reactants up to the peak; $\Delta H$ from reactants to products. An exothermic reaction can still have a large $E_a$ (a match needs striking).
  • The activated complex is not a product: it cannot be collected; it exists only for an instant at the top of the barrier.

Worked Examples — Review Questions (p. 53) and Exercise 9 (p. 60)

Review Q1: (a) Which theory explains the rate of a reaction? (b) What are the main factors for a reaction to speed up?

Show answer

(a) Collision theory. (b) More frequent collisions, and a larger proportion of particles with energy greater than $E_a$ (both raised by higher concentration, pressure, temperature, smaller particles; a catalyst lowers $E_a$).

Review Q2: (a) What orientation is needed to make a product, and why? (b) Name the state the reactants must pass through.

Show answer

(a) The correct orientation: the atoms that must form the new bonds have to meet. With the wrong orientation, the molecules bounce off each other even with enough energy. (b) The transition state, where the activated complex forms.

Review Q3: (a) What is the activation energy? (b) Reactions A, B and C have $E_a$ = 8, 10 and 15 kcal mol−1. Which is fastest at 25 °C?

Show answer

(a) The minimum energy colliding particles must have for a successful collision (to form the activated complex).

(b) Reaction A: with the lowest barrier (8 kcal mol−1), the largest fraction of collisions at 25 °C has enough energy.

Exercise 9: For $\mathrm{CO(g) + NO_2(g) \to CO_2(g) + NO(g)}$, the energy diagram shows the products below the reactants. (ii) Exothermic or endothermic? (iii) What is $E_a$? (iv) What is ΔH?

Show answer

(ii) Exothermic: the products ($\mathrm{CO_2 + NO}$) have less energy than the reactants ($\mathrm{CO + NO_2}$), so energy is released.

(iii) $E_a$: the activation energy, the height of the peak above the reactants.

(iv) ΔH: the enthalpy change of the reaction, the energy difference between products and reactants (negative here).

Key Takeaways
  • A reaction needs collisions with the correct orientation and energy ≥ $E_a$.
  • The activated complex is the unstable, high-energy state at the top of the barrier.
  • $E_a$ = barrier height from reactants; ΔH = products − reactants.
  • Low $E_a$ → many successful collisions → fast reaction.
