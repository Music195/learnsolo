Ionic Bonding

Concept — Electron Transfer and Electrostatic Attraction

Ionic (electrovalent) bonding results from the transfer of electrons from an atom with a small ionisation energy (a reactive metal) to an atom with a large electron affinity (a reactive non-metal). The positive ions (cations) and negative ions (anions) then attract each other by strong electrostatic attraction: this attraction is the ionic bond.

  • When does it happen? It depends on the difference in electronegativity: an ionic bond mostly forms when the difference is greater than 1.8, generally between reactive metals and reactive non-metals.
  • Sodium chloride: EN(Na) = 0.9, EN(Cl) = 3.0, difference 2.1. One electron moves from Na (2.8.1) to Cl (2.8.7), giving $\mathrm{Na^+}$ (2.8, like Ne) and $\mathrm{Cl^-}$ (2.8.8, like Ar): $$\mathrm{Na\cdot} + \cdot\ddot{\underset{\cdot\cdot}{\mathrm{Cl}}}\!: \;\longrightarrow\; \mathrm{Na^+}\,\Big[:\!\ddot{\underset{\cdot\cdot}{\mathrm{Cl}}}\!:\Big]^-$$
  • Magnesium oxide: EN(Mg) = 1.2, EN(O) = 3.5, difference 2.3 > 1.8. Mg (2.8.2) gives two electrons to O (2.6), forming $\mathrm{Mg^{2+}}$ and $\mathrm{O^{2-}}$, both 2.8 like Ne.
  • Electrically neutral formula: electrons lost always equal electrons gained, so ions combine in the ratio that makes the compound neutral: $\mathrm{Na^+ : Cl^-} = 1:1$ gives NaCl; $\mathrm{Ca^{2+} : Cl^-} = 1:2$ gives $\mathrm{CaCl_2}$. The number of electrons lost or gained is the valency of the element.
  • Structure: in the solid, the ions are held in fixed positions in a regular three-dimensional crystal lattice, a giant structure built by repeating a unit cell. Cubic unit cells can be simple cubic (an ion at each corner), body-centred cubic (corners plus one at the centre) or face-centred cubic (corners plus one at the centre of each face).
  • Sodium chloride lattice: NaCl (rock salt) is built from face-centred cubic unit cells. Each $\mathrm{Na^+}$ has six neighbouring $\mathrm{Cl^-}$ ions and each $\mathrm{Cl^-}$ has six $\mathrm{Na^+}$: the coordination number is 6 and the coordination ratio is 6 : 6, which simplifies to the formula unit NaCl.
  • Properties: the greater the charges on the ions, the stronger the electrostatic attraction and the stronger the ionic bond, so the melting point tends to be higher. Solid ionic compounds do not conduct electricity because their ions cannot move.
Core Rules — Predicting an Ionic Compound
$$\Delta\mathrm{EN} = \mathrm{EN}_{\text{non-metal}} - \mathrm{EN}_{\text{metal}} > 1.8 \;\Rightarrow\; \text{ionic} \qquad\qquad \sum(\text{charges}) = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; \text{formula}$$
  • $\Delta\mathrm{EN}$ = difference in electronegativity of the two elements (no unit)
  • Group I, II, III metals form $\mathrm{M^{+}}$, $\mathrm{M^{2+}}$, $\mathrm{M^{3+}}$; Group V, VI, VII non-metals form $\mathrm{X^{3-}}$, $\mathrm{X^{2-}}$, $\mathrm{X^{-}}$
  • Coordination number = number of oppositely charged ions surrounding one ion in the lattice (6 in NaCl)
Common Mistakes to Avoid
  • "NaCl is a molecule": a NaCl crystal is a giant lattice of many $\mathrm{Na^+}$ and $\mathrm{Cl^-}$ ions. "NaCl" is only the simplest ratio (the formula unit).
  • Forgetting the brackets and charges in a Lewis structure: write $\mathrm{Mg^{2+}}\,[\,:\!\ddot{\mathrm{O}}\!:\,]^{2-}$, with all eight electrons drawn on the anion.
  • Saying solid NaCl conducts: it does not; its ions are fixed. Ions can move (and conduct) only when the compound is melted or dissolved.
  • Balancing charges wrongly: Ca gives away 2 electrons but each Cl takes only 1, so two Cl atoms are needed: $\mathrm{CaCl_2}$.

Worked Examples — Review Questions (Textbook pp. 6–7)

Review Question 1(c): Which of these pairs can form an ionic compound? (i) H and O (ii) Li and O (iii) C and O (iv) Ca and O. (EN: H 2.1, Li 1.0, C 2.5, Ca 1.0, O 3.5)

Show step-by-step answer

Find $\Delta\mathrm{EN}$ for each pair and compare it with 1.8:

  • (i) H and O: $3.5 - 2.1 = 1.4 \le 1.8$ → covalent (two non-metals), e.g. $\mathrm{H_2O}$.
  • (ii) Li and O: $3.5 - 1.0 = 2.5 > 1.8$ → ionic: $\mathrm{Li_2O}$ (two $\mathrm{Li^+}$ for one $\mathrm{O^{2-}}$).
  • (iii) C and O: $3.5 - 2.5 = 1.0$ → covalent, e.g. $\mathrm{CO_2}$.
  • (iv) Ca and O: $3.5 - 1.0 = 2.5 > 1.8$ → ionic: CaO ($\mathrm{Ca^{2+}}$ and $\mathrm{O^{2-}}$).

Ionic: Li and O, Ca and O (a reactive metal with a reactive non-metal).

Review Question 2: Compare NaCl and $\mathrm{CaCl_2}$ in terms of (i) electrostatic attraction, (ii) strength of the ionic bond, (iii) melting point.

Show answer

$\mathrm{Ca^{2+}}$ carries twice the charge of $\mathrm{Na^+}$ (and is a similar size), so by the textbook's rule:

  • (i) the electrostatic attraction between $\mathrm{Ca^{2+}}$ and $\mathrm{Cl^-}$ is greater;
  • (ii) the ionic bond in $\mathrm{CaCl_2}$ is stronger;
  • (iii) $\mathrm{CaCl_2}$ is expected to have the higher melting point.

Note: this is the answer the textbook expects. The measured melting points are in fact close (NaCl 801 °C, $\mathrm{CaCl_2}$ 772 °C), because the different crystal structures matter too. The charge rule works best for compounds with the same structure, e.g. NaCl (801 °C) vs MgO (2852 °C).

Review Question 3: (a) Can ionic bonding occur between $_{19}\mathrm{K}$ and $_{17}\mathrm{Cl}$? (EN: Cl 3.0, K 0.9 as given in the textbook) Show the formation using Lewis symbols. (b) In a NaCl unit cell, how many chloride ions surround a sodium ion? What are the coordination ratio and the formula?

Show step-by-step answer

(a) $\Delta\mathrm{EN} = 3.0 - 0.9 = 2.1 > 1.8$, so yes: K (2.8.8.1) loses one electron and Cl (2.8.7) gains it.

$$\mathrm{K\cdot} + \cdot\ddot{\underset{\cdot\cdot}{\mathrm{Cl}}}\!: \;\longrightarrow\; \mathrm{K^+}\,\Big[:\!\ddot{\underset{\cdot\cdot}{\mathrm{Cl}}}\!:\Big]^- \qquad (2.8.8 \text{ and } 2.8.8, \text{ both like Ar})$$

(b) Six $\mathrm{Cl^-}$ ions surround each $\mathrm{Na^+}$ (and six $\mathrm{Na^+}$ surround each $\mathrm{Cl^-}$). The coordination ratio is 6 : 6 = 1 : 1, so the formula is NaCl.

Chapter Exercise 5 (a), (b): Show the ionic bonding between (i) $_{3}\mathrm{Li}$ and $_{35}\mathrm{Br}$, (ii) $_{20}\mathrm{Ca}$ and $_{17}\mathrm{Cl}$; describe the formation of magnesium fluoride.

Show answer
  • (i) Li (2.1) gives one electron to Br (2.8.18.7): $\mathrm{Li^+}\,[\,:\!\ddot{\mathrm{Br}}\!:\,]^-$, formula LiBr.
  • (ii) Ca (2.8.8.2) gives one electron to each of two Cl atoms (2.8.7): $[\,:\!\ddot{\mathrm{Cl}}\!:\,]^-\,\mathrm{Ca^{2+}}\,[\,:\!\ddot{\mathrm{Cl}}\!:\,]^-$, formula $\mathrm{CaCl_2}$.
  • Mg (2.8.2) gives one electron to each of two F atoms (2.7): $\mathrm{Mg^{2+}}$ (2.8) and two $\mathrm{F^-}$ (2.8), formula $\mathrm{MgF_2}$.
Key Takeaways
  • An ionic bond is the electrostatic attraction between cations and anions formed by electron transfer, usually when $\Delta\mathrm{EN} > 1.8$.
  • Both ions reach noble-gas configurations; the formula is the ratio that makes the total charge zero.
  • Ionic solids are giant crystal lattices built from unit cells; NaCl is face-centred cubic with coordination number 6 (6 : 6).
  • Higher ionic charges → stronger attraction → higher melting points; solid ionic compounds do not conduct electricity.
