Polarity and Intermolecular Forces

Concept — Uneven Sharing and the Forces Between Molecules

When two different atoms share electrons, the more electronegative atom pulls the shared pair towards itself. This bond polarity separates charge and is the origin of most of the forces between molecules (intermolecular forces), which control melting points, boiling points and solubility.

  • Polar and non-polar bonds: in H–Cl (EN 2.1 and 3.0) the electrons are pulled towards Cl, giving $\mathrm{H^{\delta+}{-}Cl^{\delta-}}$: a polar covalent bond with a dipole. $\delta$ means a partial charge, smaller than the charge of a proton or electron. Between identical atoms (Cl–Cl) the pair is shared equally: a non-polar bond. The bigger the EN difference, the more polar the bond: H–F > H–Cl > H–Br > H–I.
  • Dipole moment: a measure of polarity, drawn as an arrow $\mathrm{H}\;{+}\!\!\longrightarrow\;\mathrm{Cl}$ pointing to the negative end. A non-polar bond has zero dipole moment.
  • Polar and non-polar molecules: a diatomic molecule with a polar bond is polar. For larger molecules, the dipole moment of the molecule is the vector sum of the bond dipoles, so the shape matters: bent $\mathrm{H_2O}$ is polar, but the two equal bond dipoles of linear $\mathrm{CO_2}$ cancel, so $\mathrm{CO_2}$ is non-polar.
  • van der Waals forces (weak):
    • Dipole–dipole: the $\delta-$ end of one polar molecule attracts the $\delta+$ end of another (HCl, HCN, $\mathrm{SO_2}$).
    • Ion–dipole: between an ion and a polar molecule, e.g. NaCl in water: $\mathrm{Na^+}$ attracts the $\mathrm{O^{\delta-}}$ ends and $\mathrm{Cl^-}$ attracts the $\mathrm{H^{\delta+}}$ ends of water molecules.
    • London dispersion forces: moving electrons create an instantaneous dipole in one atom, which induces a dipole in its neighbour (induced dipole–induced dipole). They are the weakest forces but exist between all atoms and molecules; they let non-polar substances such as $\mathrm{N_2}$, $\mathrm{CO_2}$ (dry ice), halogens and noble gases liquefy and freeze.
  • Hydrogen bonding: needs (1) H attached to a very electronegative atom (F, O or N) in one molecule and (2) a lone pair on a small electronegative atom (F, O or N) in a neighbouring molecule. The $\mathrm{H^{\delta+}}$ is attracted to that lone pair. Found between water molecules and in acetic acid dimers. It is a special, strong dipole–dipole interaction: weaker than a covalent bond, but the strongest intermolecular force.
Core Rules — Classifying Bonds and Forces
$$\Delta\mathrm{EN} = 0:\ \text{non-polar} \qquad 0 < \Delta\mathrm{EN} \le 1.8:\ \text{polar covalent} \qquad \Delta\mathrm{EN} > 1.8:\ \text{ionic}$$ $$\text{London dispersion} \;<\; \text{dipole–dipole} \;<\; \text{ion–dipole} \;<\; \text{hydrogen bonding} \;\ll\; \text{covalent bond}$$
  • $\vec{\mu}_{\text{molecule}} = \sum \vec{\mu}_{\text{bond}}$: the molecule is polar if the bond dipoles do not cancel
  • Symmetrical shapes with identical outer atoms (linear $\mathrm{CO_2}$, triangular $\mathrm{BF_3}$, tetrahedral $\mathrm{CH_4}$, $\mathrm{CCl_4}$) are non-polar even with polar bonds
  • Hydrogen bonding needs H–F, H–O or H–N in the molecule
Common Mistakes to Avoid
  • "Polar bonds mean a polar molecule": $\mathrm{CCl_4}$ has four polar C–Cl bonds but is non-polar, because the tetrahedral shape makes the dipoles cancel.
  • Confusing intermolecular and intramolecular forces: boiling water breaks hydrogen bonds between molecules, not the O–H covalent bonds inside them.
  • Hydrogen bonds with any H: the H in $\mathrm{CH_4}$ is bonded to C, which is not electronegative enough, so methane has no hydrogen bonding (only London forces).
  • Forgetting London forces in polar substances: London forces act in every substance; polar molecules have them as well as dipole–dipole forces.

Worked Examples — Review Questions (Textbook p. 20)

Review Question 1: For the bonds C–Br, N–H, N–N, O–H, C–O and C–C (EN as given in this question: H 1.0, C 2.5, N 3.0, O 3.5, Br 2.8): (i) which are polar, (ii) which are non-polar, (iii) arrange the polar bonds in increasing order of polarity.

Show step-by-step answer

$\Delta\mathrm{EN}$: C–Br $2.8-2.5 = 0.3$; N–H $3.0-1.0 = 2.0$; N–N $0$; O–H $3.5-1.0 = 2.5$; C–O $3.5-2.5 = 1.0$; C–C $0$.

(i) Polar: C–Br, N–H, O–H, C–O. (ii) Non-polar: N–N and C–C (identical atoms).

(iii) C–Br (0.3) < C–O (1.0) < N–H (2.0) < O–H (2.5).

Note: this question gives H = 1.0, but the textbook's main text (and most tables) use H = 2.1. With 2.1, N–H is 0.9 and O–H is 1.4, so the order becomes C–Br < N–H < C–O < O–H. Use the values given in the question you are answering.

Review Question 2: For $\mathrm{O_2}$, HBr and $\mathrm{SO_2}$ (EN: H 1.0, O 3.5, S 2.5, Br 2.8): which have a dipole moment, and which molecules are polar?

Show answer
  • $\mathrm{O_2}$: identical atoms, $\Delta\mathrm{EN} = 0$ → no dipole moment, non-polar.
  • HBr: $\Delta\mathrm{EN} = 2.8 - 1.0 = 1.8$ → a polar bond in a diatomic molecule → dipole moment, polar.
  • $\mathrm{SO_2}$: S–O bonds are polar ($\Delta\mathrm{EN} = 1.0$) and the molecule is bent (AX2E), so the dipoles do not cancel → dipole moment, polar.

Review Question 3: (a) Classify the intermolecular forces. (b) Arrange them in increasing order of strength. (c) Which force exists in (i) aqueous KI, (ii) $\mathrm{CH_3OH}$, (iii) $\mathrm{N_2}$, (iv) HI?

Show answer

(a) Hydrogen bonding, and van der Waals forces: ion–dipole, dipole–dipole and London dispersion forces.

(b) London dispersion < dipole–dipole < ion–dipole < hydrogen bonding (the textbook: hydrogen bonding is the strongest intermolecular force and London forces are the weakest).

(c) (i) Ion–dipole forces between $\mathrm{K^+}$ / $\mathrm{I^-}$ and water molecules. (ii) Hydrogen bonding (O–H group). (iii) London dispersion forces only (non-polar). (iv) Dipole–dipole interaction (H–I is slightly polar), plus London forces.

Key Takeaways
  • Unequal sharing makes a polar bond with $\delta+$ and $\delta-$ ends; equal sharing (identical atoms) is non-polar.
  • A molecule's dipole moment is the vector sum of its bond dipoles: shape decides whether they cancel.
  • van der Waals forces: dipole–dipole, ion–dipole and London dispersion (the weakest, present in all substances).
  • Hydrogen bonds form between H on F/O/N and a lone pair on F/O/N: the strongest intermolecular force.
