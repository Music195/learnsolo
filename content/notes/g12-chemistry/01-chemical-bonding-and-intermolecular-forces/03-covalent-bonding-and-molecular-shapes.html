Covalent Bonding, Lewis Structures and Molecular Shapes

Concept — Sharing Electron Pairs

A covalent bond is formed by sharing one or more pairs of valence electrons between atoms, usually non-metals. It is the electrostatic attraction between the nuclei and the shared electrons.

  • Formation of $\mathrm{H_2}$: as two H atoms approach, three forces act: (1) repulsion between the two electrons, (2) attraction between each nucleus and the other atom's electron, (3) repulsion between the two nuclei. Far apart, the attraction is too small to bond; too close, the nuclei repel strongly. At the bond length the net attraction is greatest and the molecule is most stable.
  • Orbital overlap: the outer orbitals overlap so that unpaired valence electrons can be shared, giving each atom an octet (H a duplet). Paired electrons that are not shared are lone pairs.
  • Single, double and triple bonds: one, two or three shared pairs (H–F; O=O, $\mathrm{CO_2}$, $\mathrm{C_2H_4}$; N≡N, CO). More shared electrons → stronger attraction → shorter and stronger bond: bond length single > double > triple; bond strength triple > double > single.
  • Coordinate (dative) bond: both shared electrons come from one atom that has a lone pair, shown by an arrow →. Examples: $\mathrm{NH_3 + H^+ \to NH_4^+}$, $\mathrm{H_2O + H^+ \to H_3O^+}$, $\mathrm{H_3N{\to}BF_3}$, and one of the three bonds in C≡O. Once formed, the four N–H bonds in $\mathrm{NH_4^+}$ are identical.
  • Exceptions to the octet rule: electron-deficient molecules have fewer than 8 electrons on the central atom (NO 7, $\mathrm{BeCl_2}$ 4, $\mathrm{BF_3}$ 6, $\mathrm{AlCl_3}$); expanded valence shell molecules have more than 8 ($\mathrm{PF_5}$ 10, $\mathrm{ICl_3}$ 10, $\mathrm{SF_6}$ 12).
  • VSEPR theory: the electron pairs around a central atom arrange themselves to be as far apart as possible. A multiple bond counts as one unit. Repulsion increases: bonding–bonding < lone–bonding < lone–lone, so lone pairs squeeze the bond angles below the ideal values.
  • Giant covalent structures: diamond (each C bonded to 4 others, tetrahedral network, no free electrons), graphite (layers of hexagonal rings, each C bonded to 3, free electrons, conducts electricity, weak forces between layers) and silica $\mathrm{SiO_2}$ (each Si bonded to 4 O, each O to 2 Si). Fullerene ($\mathrm{C_{60}}$), nanotubes and graphene are not giant structures.
Core Rules — Drawing Lewis Structures and Predicting Shapes
$$\text{remaining electrons} = \text{total valence electrons} - 2 \times (\text{number of bonds in the skeleton}) \qquad \text{lone pairs} = \frac{\text{remaining}}{2}$$
  • Step 1: add up the valence electrons (add 1 for each negative charge, subtract 1 for each positive charge)
  • Step 2: write the skeleton with the central atom joined to the others by single bonds
  • Step 3: subtract the shared electrons; place the rest as lone pairs, using double or triple bonds only if needed
  • Step 4: complete every octet (H needs only 2)
  • Shape: count electron pairs around the central atom → 2 linear (180°), 3 triangular planar (120°), 4 tetrahedral (109.5°); lone pairs then give bent or pyramidal shapes

Common Mistakes to Avoid
  • Counting a double bond as two electron pairs for the shape: in $\mathrm{CO_2}$ there are four bonding pairs, but they form two units, so the shape is linear.
  • Ignoring lone pairs: $\mathrm{NH_3}$ has four electron pairs (tetrahedral geometry) but only three atoms around N, so the molecule is trigonal pyramidal, not tetrahedral.
  • Forgetting the ion charge: $\mathrm{CO_3^{2-}}$ has $4 + 3 \times 6 + 2 = 24$ valence electrons; $\mathrm{NH_4^+}$ has $5 + 4 - 1 = 8$.
  • Thinking a coordinate bond is weaker or different once formed: in $\mathrm{NH_4^+}$ all four N–H bonds are identical.

Worked Examples — Textbook Examples 1–5 and Review Questions (pp. 10–16)

Example 1 (p. 11): Draw the Lewis structure of carbon tetrachloride, $\mathrm{CCl_4}$.

Show step-by-step solution

Total valence electrons $= 4\ (\mathrm{C}) + 7 \times 4\ (\mathrm{Cl}) = 32$. Skeleton: C in the centre with four C–Cl single bonds $= 8$ shared electrons.

Remaining $= 32 - 8 = 24$ electrons $= 12$ lone pairs, three on each Cl, so every atom has an octet.

C has four single bonds to Cl; each Cl carries 3 lone pairs.

Example 2 (p. 11): Draw the Lewis structure of the carbonate ion, $\mathrm{CO_3^{2-}}$.

Show step-by-step solution

Total $= 4\ (\mathrm{C}) + 6 \times 3\ (\mathrm{O}) + 2\ (\text{charge}) = 24$. Skeleton: three C–O bonds $= 6$ electrons. Remaining $= 24 - 6 = 18 = 9$ lone pairs.

Placing the 9 lone pairs on the three O atoms leaves C with only 6 electrons, so one lone pair from an O is moved in to make a C=O double bond.

$[\mathrm{O{=}C(O)O}]^{2-}$: one double and two single C–O bonds, the whole ion in brackets with charge 2−.

Example 3 (p. 11): Draw the Lewis structure of the ammonium ion, $\mathrm{NH_4^+}$.

Show step-by-step solution

Total $= 5\ (\mathrm{N}) + 1 \times 4\ (\mathrm{H}) - 1\ (\text{charge}) = 8$. Four N–H bonds use all 8 electrons: remaining $= 8 - 8 = 0$, no lone pairs.

N with four N–H bonds, in brackets with charge +.

Example 4 (p. 13): Predict the shape of $\mathrm{BF_3}$ and the F–B–F bond angle.

Show step-by-step solution

(i) Lewis structure: B with three B–F single bonds; each F has 3 lone pairs ($3 + 21 = 24$ electrons). (ii) There are 3 electron pairs around B and no lone pairs, so the geometry of electron pairs is triangular planar.

$\mathrm{BF_3}$ (type AX3) is triangular planar with F–B–F = 120°.

Example 5 (pp. 13–14): Predict the shape of $\mathrm{NH_3}$ and the H–N–H bond angle.

Show step-by-step solution

(i) Lewis structure: N with three N–H bonds and one lone pair ($5 + 3 = 8$ electrons). (ii) Four electron pairs around N → tetrahedral geometry of electron pairs. The lone pair repels the bonding pairs more strongly.

$\mathrm{NH_3}$ (AX3E) is trigonal pyramidal with H–N–H less than 109.5° (about 107°).

Review Question 2 (p. 15): Of $\mathrm{AlF_3}$ and $\mathrm{AlCl_3}$ (EN: F 4.0, Al 1.5, Cl 3.0), which is covalent and which is ionic? For the covalent one, give the Lewis structure, central atom, number of electron pairs, geometry of electron pairs and shape.

Show step-by-step answer

$\mathrm{AlF_3}$: $\Delta\mathrm{EN} = 4.0 - 1.5 = 2.5 > 1.8$ → ionic. $\mathrm{AlCl_3}$: $\Delta\mathrm{EN} = 3.0 - 1.5 = 1.5 \le 1.8$ → covalent.

$\mathrm{AlCl_3}$: (i) valence electrons $3 + 7 \times 3 = 24$; three Al–Cl bonds (6 electrons), remaining 18 = 9 lone pairs on the Cl atoms. (ii) Central atom: Al. (iii) 3 electron pairs around Al (only 6 electrons: electron-deficient). (iv) Triangular planar. (v) Triangular planar, 120°.

Review Question 3(a) (p. 16): For $\mathrm{NH_3}$ and $\mathrm{BF_3}$: which has a lone pair on the central atom, which is electron-deficient, and can they react?

Show answer

$\mathrm{NH_3}$ has a lone pair on N; $\mathrm{BF_3}$ is electron-deficient (6 electrons on B). Yes: N donates its lone pair to B, forming a coordinate bond:

$$\mathrm{H_3N\!: + BF_3 \longrightarrow H_3N{\to}BF_3}$$

(b) Giant covalent structures: diamond, graphite and silica (not fullerene or carbon nanotubes).

Chapter Exercise 6(c): Arrange single, double and triple bonds by bond length and bond strength.

Show answer

Bond length: triple < double < single. Bond strength: single < double < triple. More shared electrons pull the two nuclei together more strongly, so the bond becomes shorter and stronger.

Key Takeaways
  • A covalent bond is a shared electron pair; the bond length is where attraction and repulsion balance (lowest energy).
  • Lewis structures: count valence electrons (adjust for charge), make the skeleton, place lone pairs, add multiple bonds if needed.
  • A coordinate bond is a shared pair donated by one atom (→), e.g. $\mathrm{NH_4^+}$, $\mathrm{H_3O^+}$, $\mathrm{H_3N{\to}BF_3}$.
  • VSEPR: electron pairs spread out (2 linear, 3 triangular planar, 4 tetrahedral); lone pairs give bent and pyramidal shapes with smaller angles.
  • Diamond, graphite and silica are giant covalent structures; only graphite conducts (free electrons in its layers).
