Metallic Bonding

Concept — Positive Ions in a Sea of Electrons

Most metals are solids at room temperature, so something must hold their atoms together. In a metallic bond the atoms neither share electrons in pairs (covalent) nor hand them over to a partner (ionic).

  • The sea of electrons: in sodium, magnesium or aluminium, the valence electrons are held very loosely. They leave their atoms and form one common electron cloud that spreads through the whole metal. These mobile (delocalised) electrons are called "a sea of electrons".
  • The bond: the positive metal ions sit in a regular, closely packed lattice, and the sea of negative electrons moving between them holds them together by strong electrostatic attraction. This is why metals have low volatility (they are hard to turn into a gas).
  • Electrical conductivity: the mobile electrons can drift through the lattice when a voltage is applied, so metals conduct electricity in the solid state. Solid ionic compounds cannot: their ions are locked in place and they have no free electrons.
  • Strength of metallic bonding increases with:
    1. a larger positive charge on the ions in the lattice,
    2. a smaller size of the metal ions,
    3. more mobile (valence) electrons per atom.
    Metallic bonds are generally weaker than ionic bonds, so some metals melt at lower temperatures than ionic compounds (sodium melts at 98 °C, sodium chloride at 801 °C).

Common Mistakes to Avoid
  • "Metal atoms share electron pairs": no pairs are shared between two particular atoms. The valence electrons belong to the whole lattice.
  • "Ions carry the current in a metal": in a solid metal only the electrons move; the positive ions stay in their places in the lattice.
  • Forgetting the charge: Mg gives two electrons per atom to the sea and Al gives three, so their bonding is stronger than sodium's one-electron sea.

Worked Examples — Review Questions (Textbook pp. 21–22)

Review Question 1: Describe two everyday objects that contain (i) covalent bonds, (ii) ionic bonds, (iii) metallic bonds.

Show answer

(i) Covalent: water ($\mathrm{H_2O}$, O–H bonds), sugar or plastic bags (C–C and C–H bonds). (ii) Ionic: table salt (NaCl), chalk or limestone ($\mathrm{CaCO_3}$). (iii) Metallic: a copper electric wire, an aluminium cooking pot (any metal object).

Review Question 2(a): Identify the type of bond in Zn, $\mathrm{CCl_4}$, NaBr, Al and $\mathrm{MgCl_2}$. Which will conduct electricity most effectively?

Show answer

Zn: metallic. $\mathrm{CCl_4}$: covalent (two non-metals). NaBr: ionic. Al: metallic. $\mathrm{MgCl_2}$: ionic.

Aluminium conducts best: it is a metal with 3 mobile electrons per atom (zinc gives 2). Solid NaBr and $\mathrm{MgCl_2}$ have no free charges, and $\mathrm{CCl_4}$ has neither ions nor free electrons.

Review Question 2(b): Compare the strength of metallic bonding for (i) sodium and magnesium, (ii) magnesium and calcium.

Show answer

(i) $\mathrm{Mg^{2+}}$ has a larger charge than $\mathrm{Na^+}$, is smaller (72 pm vs 102 pm), and gives 2 electrons per atom to the sea instead of 1. So magnesium has the stronger metallic bonding (melting points: Mg 650 °C, Na 98 °C).

(ii) Both ions are 2+ and give 2 electrons, but $\mathrm{Mg^{2+}}$ (72 pm) is smaller than $\mathrm{Ca^{2+}}$ (100 pm), so by the textbook's rules magnesium has the stronger metallic bonding.

Note: the measured melting points (Mg 650 °C, Ca 842 °C) do not follow this rule, because the two metals pack their atoms in different crystal structures. For the exam, answer with the three rules: charge, size and number of mobile electrons.

Exercise 12 (c): Which have higher melting points, divalent or trivalent metals?

Show answer

Trivalent metals (such as Al). Their ions have a 3+ charge and each atom gives 3 electrons to the sea, so the attraction between the ions and the electron sea is stronger than for 2+ ions with 2 electrons each.

Exercise 13: Magnesium nitride, $\mathrm{Mg_3N_2}$, forms when nitrogen is passed over red-hot magnesium. (i) What type of compound is it? (ii) Describe its formation with Lewis symbols. (iii) High or low melting point?

Show step-by-step answer

(i) Ionic: magnesium is a metal (2.8.2) that loses electrons easily, nitrogen is a non-metal (2.5) that gains them. (With EN Mg 1.2 and N 3.0, $\Delta\mathrm{EN} = 1.8$, right at the boundary; a metal with a non-metal and highly charged ions makes it ionic.)

(ii) Each Mg loses 2 electrons and each N gains 3. To balance, the electrons lost must equal the electrons gained: $3 \times 2 = 6 = 2 \times 3$. So 3 Mg atoms give 6 electrons to 2 N atoms:

$$3\,\mathrm{Mg{:}} \;+\; 2\,\mathrm{\dot{\underset{\cdot}{N}}{:}} \;\longrightarrow\; 3\,\mathrm{Mg^{2+}} \;+\; 2\,[\mathrm{{:}\ddot{\underset{\cdot\cdot}{N}}{:}}]^{3-}$$

Each $\mathrm{N^{3-}}$ now has 8 electrons (2.8, like Ne) and each $\mathrm{Mg^{2+}}$ is 2.8 (like Ne).

(iii) A high melting point: the strong electrostatic attraction between $\mathrm{Mg^{2+}}$ and $\mathrm{N^{3-}}$ ions, which carry large charges, needs a lot of energy to overcome.

Exercise 14: Elements $_{13}$X, $_{9}$Y and $_{17}$Z (EN: X 1.5, Y 4.0, Z 3.0). (i) What type of compound does X form with Y, and X with Z? (iii) Give their formulas. (iv) Predict the shape of the covalent molecule.

Show step-by-step answer

X is 2.8.3 (aluminium, 3 valence electrons), Y is 2.7 (fluorine) and Z is 2.8.7 (chlorine); each of Y and Z needs one electron.

X and Y: $\Delta\mathrm{EN} = 4.0 - 1.5 = 2.5 > 1.8$ → ionic, formula $\mathrm{XY_3}$ ($\mathrm{AlF_3}$).

X and Z: $\Delta\mathrm{EN} = 3.0 - 1.5 = 1.5 < 1.8$ → polar covalent, formula $\mathrm{XZ_3}$ ($\mathrm{AlCl_3}$).

(iv) $\mathrm{XZ_3}$ has three bonding pairs and no lone pair on X (AX3), so it is triangular planar with 120° bond angles (like $\mathrm{BF_3}$; X has only 6 electrons, an electron-deficient molecule).

Key Takeaways
  • A metallic bond is the electrostatic attraction between positive metal ions in a lattice and a sea of delocalised valence electrons.
  • Mobile electrons make metals conduct electricity as solids; solid ionic compounds cannot.
  • Metallic bonding is stronger for higher ion charge, smaller ions and more mobile electrons per atom (Na < Mg < Al).
  • Metals bend because the electron sea moves with sliding layers; ionic crystals shatter because like charges meet.
