Basic Concepts to Understand Chemical Bonding

Concept — Electrons Decide How Atoms Join

Atoms are rarely found alone. They join by chemical bonds, the attractive forces that hold atoms together in molecules and compounds. Only the electrons in the outermost shell take part, so we first need to know where an atom's electrons are.

  • Shells, subshells and orbitals: the main shells K, L, M, N, … ($n = 1, 2, 3, 4, \dots$) are split into $s$, $p$, $d$ and $f$ subshells, which hold at most 2, 6, 10 and 14 electrons. An $s$ subshell has one spherical orbital, a $p$ subshell has three dumbbell-shaped orbitals ($p_x, p_y, p_z$), and a $d$ subshell has five double-dumbbell orbitals. Each orbital holds at most 2 electrons.
  • Aufbau principle: electrons fill the lower-energy orbitals before the higher ones, in the order $1s\ 2s\ 2p\ 3s\ 3p\ 4s\ 3d\ 4p \dots$ (note that $4s$ fills before $3d$).
  • Pauli's exclusion principle: no more than two electrons can occupy the same orbital, and two electrons in the same orbital must have opposite spins ($\uparrow\downarrow$).
  • Hund's rule: every orbital in a subshell is singly occupied before any orbital is doubly occupied, and all the single electrons have the same spin. (Electrons spread out because they repel each other.)
  • Valence electrons and the octet rule: the electrons in the outermost shell are the valence electrons; for main-group elements their number equals the group number (C, Group IV: 4; O, Group VI: 6). When bonds form, atoms lose, gain or share electrons to reach the stable arrangement of a noble gas, usually eight valence electrons (the octet rule). There are exceptions, and the rule does not apply to $d$-block elements.
  • Lewis symbols: the element symbol surrounded by one dot for each valence electron, e.g. $\cdot\ddot{\mathrm{O}}\!:$ for oxygen with 6 dots.
  • Electronegativity (EN) measures how strongly an atom attracts a shared pair of electrons. Electron affinity (EA) is the energy released when a gaseous atom gains an electron (high EA → forms anions easily). Ionisation energy (IE) is the energy needed to remove an electron from a gaseous atom (low IE → forms cations easily). Generally, the higher the first IE, the higher the electronegativity.
  • Types of bonding: the strong bonds are ionic, covalent and metallic. The weak forces between molecules (intermolecular forces) are hydrogen bonding and van der Waals forces (ion–dipole, dipole–dipole and London dispersion forces). Ionic bonds are generally stronger than covalent bonds; the hydrogen bond is the strongest intermolecular force and London forces are the weakest. Most real bonds are not 100 % ionic or 100 % covalent.
Core Rules — Filling Electrons into Orbitals
$$1s \to 2s \to 2p \to 3s \to 3p \to 4s \to 3d \to 4p \qquad\qquad s^{\le 2}\quad p^{\le 6}\quad d^{\le 10}\quad f^{\le 14} \qquad\qquad \text{maximum in shell } n = 2n^2$$
  • Superscripts give the number of electrons in a subshell: $_{8}\mathrm{O} = 1s^2\,2s^2\,2p^4$ (shell notation 2.6)
  • A noble-gas core can be written in brackets: $_{11}\mathrm{Na} = [\mathrm{Ne}]\,3s^1$
  • Valence electrons (main groups) = electrons in the highest shell $n$ = group number
  • The atomic number written at the lower left, $_{Z}\mathrm{X}$, is the number of electrons in the neutral atom
Common Mistakes to Avoid
  • Filling 3d before 4s: the $4s$ subshell has lower energy, so potassium is $[\mathrm{Ar}]\,4s^1$, not $[\mathrm{Ar}]\,3d^1$.
  • Pairing too early: nitrogen's three $2p$ electrons sit one in each $p$ orbital ($\uparrow\ \uparrow\ \uparrow$), not $\uparrow\downarrow\ \uparrow$ (Hund's rule).
  • Counting all electrons as valence electrons: bromine has 35 electrons but only 7 valence electrons ($4s^2\,4p^5$); the full $3d^{10}$ subshell is not counted.
  • Two famous exceptions (beyond this textbook's simple order): chromium is $[\mathrm{Ar}]\,3d^5\,4s^1$ and copper is $[\mathrm{Ar}]\,3d^{10}\,4s^1$, because half-full and full $d$ subshells are extra stable.

Worked Examples — Review Questions (Textbook p. 4)

Review Question 1: (a) What are the lowest-energy main shell and subshell that electrons occupy? (b) What are the directions of the electron spins in the same orbital? (c) How many orbitals are there in the $s$, $p$ and $d$ subshells, and what are their shapes?

Show answer

(a) The K shell ($n = 1$) and its $1s$ subshell.

(b) Opposite spins, $\uparrow\downarrow$ (Pauli's exclusion principle).

(c) $s$: 1 spherical orbital; $p$: 3 dumbbell-shaped orbitals ($p_x, p_y, p_z$); $d$: 5 orbitals with double-dumbbell shapes.

Review Question 3: (a) What is meant by electronegativity? (b) What does the type of chemical bonding depend on? (c) (i) Classify the main types of chemical bonding; which is the strongest? (ii) Mention the intermolecular forces.

Show answer

(a) Electronegativity is a measure of the ability of an atom in a bond to attract the shared electrons to itself.

(b) On the types of elements involved (metals or non-metals), that is, on how easily their atoms lose, gain or share valence electrons (their electronegativities).

(c) (i) Ionic, covalent and metallic bonding; in general the ionic bond is the strongest. (ii) Hydrogen bonding and van der Waals forces: ion–dipole, dipole–dipole and London dispersion forces.

Key Takeaways
  • Electrons fill orbitals in the order $1s\,2s\,2p\,3s\,3p\,4s\,3d\,4p$ (Aufbau), at most two per orbital with opposite spins (Pauli), singly before pairing (Hund).
  • Only valence electrons take part in bonding; atoms tend to reach a noble-gas octet.
  • Lewis symbols show valence electrons as dots around the element symbol.
  • High electron affinity → anions form easily; low ionisation energy → cations form easily; electronegativity controls how electrons are shared or transferred.
