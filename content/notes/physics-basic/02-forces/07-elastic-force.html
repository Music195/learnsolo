Elastic Force (Hooke's Law)

A spring always pulls or pushes back towards its natural length

A spring that is neither stretched nor squashed has its natural length (自然長). At the natural length it puts no force on anything.

Stretch it, and it pulls back; squash it, and it pushes back. Either way the force points towards the natural length: this is the elastic force (弾性力), a kind of restoring force (復元力). The interesting part: the direction flips between stretched and squashed.

Its size follows Hooke's law (フックの法則): it is proportional to how far the spring is stretched or squashed from its natural length.

Hooke's law

$$F = kx$$

$F$ the size of the elastic force ($\mathrm{N}$), $k$ the spring constant (ばね定数, $\mathrm{N/m}$), $x$ the stretch or squash from the natural length ($\mathrm{m}$). The direction is not in the formula: decide it by looking.

  1. Hang weights on a spring and measure the stretch. Double the weight and the stretch doubles; triple it and it triples. So force and stretch are proportional: $F = kx$. This is measured, not derived.
  2. The constant $k$ is the force per metre of stretch, so its unit is $\mathrm{N/m}$. A spring with $k = 50\ \mathrm{N/m}$ needs $50\ \mathrm{N}$ to stretch it by $1\ \mathrm{m}$.
  3. A big $k$ is a stiff (strong) spring; a small $k$ is a soft (weak) one.
  4. Squashing works the same way, as long as the spring isn't squashed solid or stretched out of shape.

Example 1

A spring with $k = 50\ \mathrm{N/m}$ is stretched by $0.20\ \mathrm{m}$. Find the size of its elastic force.

Show solution
  1. $F = kx = 50 \times 0.20$.

$F = 10\ \mathrm{N}$

Example 2

A $0.50\ \mathrm{kg}$ mass hangs at rest from a spring with $k = 49\ \mathrm{N/m}$. How far is the spring stretched?

Show solution
  1. Forces on the mass: gravity $mg$ down, elastic force $kx$ up.
  2. At rest: $kx = mg$, so $x = \dfrac{0.50 \times 9.8}{49} = \dfrac{4.9}{49}$.

$x = 0.10\ \mathrm{m}$ ($10\ \mathrm{cm}$)

Example 3

A spring's natural length is $20\ \mathrm{cm}$. Pulled with $2.0\ \mathrm{N}$, it is $25\ \mathrm{cm}$ long. Find $k$, and the spring's length when it is squashed with $3.0\ \mathrm{N}$.

Show solution
  1. The stretch is $25 - 20 = 5\ \mathrm{cm} = 0.050\ \mathrm{m}$, so $k = \dfrac{2.0}{0.050} = 40\ \mathrm{N/m}$.
  2. Squashed with $3.0\ \mathrm{N}$: $x = \dfrac{3.0}{40} = 0.075\ \mathrm{m} = 7.5\ \mathrm{cm}$.
  3. Length $= 20 - 7.5$.

$k = 40\ \mathrm{N/m}$; length $12.5\ \mathrm{cm}$

Example 4

Two people pull the two ends of a spring ($k = 100\ \mathrm{N/m}$) apart, each with $10\ \mathrm{N}$. How far does it stretch?

Show solution
  1. Look at one end: it is pulled out with $10\ \mathrm{N}$ and the spring pulls it back with $kx$.
  2. $kx = 10$, so $x = 0.10$. (Not $20\ \mathrm{N}$: this is the same as fixing one end to a wall and pulling the other with $10\ \mathrm{N}$.)

$x = 0.10\ \mathrm{m}$

$x$ is the change from the natural length, not the length of the spring. And $F = kx$ is a size, never negative: give the force its direction yourself, by asking "which way does the spring want to go back?"
$k$ is in $\mathrm{N/m}$, so turn centimetres into metres before using it: $5\ \mathrm{cm} = 0.05\ \mathrm{m}$.
  • The elastic force points back towards the natural length: it flips between stretched and squashed.
  • Size: $F = kx$, with $x$ measured from the natural length.
  • $k$ in $\mathrm{N/m}$: a bigger $k$ is a stiffer spring.
