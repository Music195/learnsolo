The Photoelectric Effect

Light comes in packets (photons) of energy $hf$; one photon frees one electron, giving $hf = W + K_{\max}$

Everything up to Magnetism was classical physics. Atomic physics is the first step into modern physics, where pictures from everyday life stop working. Don't try too hard to imagine it; follow the experiments and the equations.

Particles and waves. A particle can be counted (1, 2, 3, ...) and can collide; the electron is the classic example. A wave shows diffraction and interference; light was shown to be a wave by Young's double slit. The energy a wave carries depends on its frequency and amplitude; for light, a bigger amplitude means brighter (more intense) light.

The photoelectric effect: light shining on a metal knocks electrons out of it. (A negatively charged electroscope with a zinc plate collapses under ultraviolet light: its extra electrons fly off.) The experiments found:

  1. below a certain frequency $f_0$ (the threshold frequency), no electrons come out, however bright the light;
  2. above $f_0$, electrons come out at once, however dim the light;
  3. the largest kinetic energy of the electrons depends only on the frequency, not on the brightness.

If light were only a wave, a bright enough light (large amplitude) should always free electrons. It doesn't: only the frequency matters. Einstein solved this by saying light also behaves as particles, called photons:

  • each photon carries energy $E = hf$ ($h$ = Planck's constant); the amplitude does not appear;
  • brighter light means more photons, not stronger ones;
  • one photon hits one electron and gives it all its energy. The electron needs at least the work function $W$ to escape from the metal (it is held by the positive nuclei); whatever is left becomes kinetic energy.

Electrons that lose some energy on the way out (bumping into atoms) come out slower, so the formula gives the largest kinetic energy. Think of it as being given money for a train ticket: if the money is less than the fare $W$, you can't go at all, no matter how many people are each given that small amount. If it is more, you travel and keep the change; the one who wasted nothing on the way has the most left.

The phototube. In a phototube, light hits the cathode and the freed electrons cross to the anode, making a photocurrent. Raising the anode voltage collects more of them, until all are collected (the saturation current, set by the number of photons). Reversing the voltage pushes the electrons back; the reverse voltage $V_0$ that just stops even the fastest ones is the stopping voltage, with $eV_0 = K_{\max}$.

Photon energy and Einstein's photoelectric equation

$$E = hf = \frac{hc}{\lambda}, \qquad hf = W + K_{\max}, \qquad K_{\max} = eV_0, \qquad W = hf_0$$

$h = 6.6\times10^{-34}$ J·s (Planck's constant). $f$: frequency of the light (Hz). $W$: work function of the metal (J or eV). $K_{\max}$: largest kinetic energy of the freed electrons. $V_0$: stopping voltage. $f_0$: threshold frequency. $1\ \text{eV} = 1.6\times10^{-19}$ J.

  1. Energy is conserved when one photon frees one electron: photon energy = energy to escape + kinetic energy left, so $hf = W + K_{\max}$.
  2. Rearranged: $K_{\max} = hf - W$. This is a straight line $y = ax + b$ in $f$, with slope $a = h$ and intercept $b = -W$.
  3. So the graph of $K_{\max}$ (or $eV_0$) against $f$ has the same slope $h$ for every metal; only the intercept $-W$ changes. That is how $h$ is measured.
  4. The line meets the axis ($K_{\max} = 0$) at $f_0 = W/h$: below that frequency there is no photoelectric effect.

Light on a metal: change the frequency and the brightness. Right: $K_{\max}$ against $f$

Example 1

Find the energy of one photon of light of wavelength $5.0\times10^{-7}$ m, in joules and in electron volts. ($h = 6.6\times10^{-34}$ J·s, $c = 3.0\times10^8$ m/s, $e = 1.6\times10^{-19}$ C)

Show solution
  1. $E = \dfrac{hc}{\lambda} = \dfrac{6.6\times10^{-34} \times 3.0\times10^8}{5.0\times10^{-7}} = 4.0\times10^{-19}$ J.
  2. In eV: $\dfrac{3.96\times10^{-19}}{1.6\times10^{-19}} = 2.5$ eV.

$4.0\times10^{-19}$ J = 2.5 eV

Example 2

The work function of sodium is 2.3 eV. Find its threshold frequency and the longest wavelength that frees electrons.

Show solution
  1. $W = 2.3 \times 1.6\times10^{-19} = 3.68\times10^{-19}$ J.
  2. $f_0 = \dfrac{W}{h} = \dfrac{3.68\times10^{-19}}{6.6\times10^{-34}} = 5.6\times10^{14}$ Hz.
  3. $\lambda_0 = \dfrac{c}{f_0} = \dfrac{3.0\times10^8}{5.58\times10^{14}} = 5.4\times10^{-7}$ m (green light). Longer wavelengths have lower frequency, so they don't work.

$5.6\times10^{14}$ Hz; $5.4\times10^{-7}$ m

Example 3

Light of frequency $1.0\times10^{15}$ Hz falls on sodium ($W = 2.3$ eV). Find the largest kinetic energy of the electrons and the stopping voltage.

Show solution
  1. $hf = 6.6\times10^{-34} \times 1.0\times10^{15} = 6.6\times10^{-19}$ J $= 4.1$ eV.
  2. $K_{\max} = hf - W = 4.1 - 2.3 = 1.8$ eV.
  3. $eV_0 = K_{\max}$, so $V_0 = 1.8$ V.

$K_{\max} = 1.8$ eV ($2.9\times10^{-19}$ J); $V_0 = 1.8$ V

Example 4

In Example 3 the light is made twice as bright, keeping the same frequency. What happens to the stopping voltage and to the saturation current?

Show solution
  1. Brighter light = twice as many photons, each with the same energy $hf$.
  2. Each electron still gets the same energy, so $K_{\max}$ and $V_0$ do not change.
  3. Twice as many electrons are freed each second, so the saturation current doubles.

Stopping voltage unchanged (1.8 V); saturation current doubles

Example 5

For one metal, the stopping voltage is 1.0 V at $8.0\times10^{14}$ Hz and 2.0 V at $10.4\times10^{14}$ Hz. Find Planck's constant and the work function (in eV).

Show solution
  1. $eV_0 = hf - W$, so the slope of $eV_0$ against $f$ is $h$: $h = \dfrac{e\,\Delta V_0}{\Delta f} = \dfrac{1.6\times10^{-19} \times 1.0}{2.4\times10^{14}} = 6.7\times10^{-34}$ J·s.
  2. $W = hf - eV_0 = 6.67\times10^{-34} \times 8.0\times10^{14} - 1.6\times10^{-19} = 3.73\times10^{-19}$ J.
  3. In eV: $3.73\times10^{-19} / 1.6\times10^{-19} = 2.3$ eV.

$h = 6.7\times10^{-34}$ J·s; $W = 2.3$ eV

Brightness (intensity) changes the number of photons and so the number of electrons (the current). Only the frequency changes the energy of each electron. Never say "brighter light gives faster electrons".
Work in electron volts: an electron crossing a voltage $V$ gains $eV$ joules, which is simply $V$ electron volts. So $K_{\max}$ in eV is the stopping voltage in volts.
  • Photon energy $E = hf = hc/\lambda$; more intense light = more photons.
  • $hf = W + K_{\max}$; $K_{\max} = eV_0$; threshold $f_0 = W/h$.
  • $K_{\max}$–$f$ graph: slope $h$ for every metal, intercept $-W$.
