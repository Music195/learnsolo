The Compton Effect

An X-ray photon bounces off an electron like a ball: momentum and energy are conserved, and the photon's wavelength grows by $\frac{h}{mc}(1 - \cos\phi)$

In the Compton effect, X-rays fired at electrons come out scattered with a longer wavelength, and the electrons are knocked moving. A longer wavelength means less energy ($E = hc/\lambda$), so the photon has handed some energy to the electron, exactly as in a collision between two balls.

To treat it as a collision, the photon needs a momentum as well as an energy:

  • electron: energy $\tfrac12mv^2$, momentum $mv$ (as in mechanics);
  • photon: energy $hf = \dfrac{hc}{\lambda}$, momentum $\dfrac{hf}{c} = \dfrac{h}{\lambda}$.

A way to remember the photon momentum: energy and momentum differ by a speed in their units (for the electron, $\tfrac12mv^2 \to mv$ is "divide by a speed", apart from the ½). Divide the photon energy by its speed $c$: $\dfrac{hf}{c} = \dfrac{h}{\lambda}$.

The collision is elastic, so three equations hold: momentum along $x$, momentum along $y$, and energy. Solving them always follows the same three steps (below). This derivation is one to practise until you can write it on a blank sheet.

Photon momentum and the Compton shift

$$p = \frac{h}{\lambda}, \qquad \Delta\lambda = \lambda' - \lambda = \frac{h}{mc}(1 - \cos\phi)$$

$\lambda$, $\lambda'$: X-ray wavelength before and after. $\phi$: angle the photon is turned through. $m$: electron mass ($9.1\times10^{-31}$ kg). $\dfrac{h}{mc} = 2.4\times10^{-12}$ m.

  1. The photon (wavelength $\lambda$) comes in along $x$; afterwards it goes at angle $\phi$ above the axis with $\lambda'$, and the electron at angle $\theta$ below with speed $v$.
    ① $x$: $\dfrac{h}{\lambda} = \dfrac{h}{\lambda'}\cos\phi + mv\cos\theta$
    ② $y$: $0 = \dfrac{h}{\lambda'}\sin\phi - mv\sin\theta$
    ③ energy: $\dfrac{hc}{\lambda} = \dfrac{hc}{\lambda'} + \tfrac12mv^2$
  2. Step 1, remove $\theta$ using ① and ②. Write $mv\cos\theta = \dfrac{h}{\lambda} - \dfrac{h}{\lambda'}\cos\phi$ and $mv\sin\theta = \dfrac{h}{\lambda'}\sin\phi$, square both and add ($\cos^2 + \sin^2 = 1$): $(mv)^2 = \left(\dfrac{h}{\lambda}\right)^2 - \dfrac{2h^2}{\lambda\lambda'}\cos\phi + \left(\dfrac{h}{\lambda'}\right)^2$.
  3. Step 2, remove $v$ using ③. ③ says $\dfrac{(mv)^2}{2m} = \dfrac{hc}{\lambda} - \dfrac{hc}{\lambda'} = hc\,\dfrac{\lambda' - \lambda}{\lambda\lambda'}$. Put in $(mv)^2$ from step 1 and multiply by $\dfrac{\lambda\lambda'}{hc}$: $\lambda' - \lambda = \dfrac{h}{2mc}\left(\dfrac{\lambda'}{\lambda} + \dfrac{\lambda}{\lambda'} - 2\cos\phi\right)$.
  4. Step 3, approximate. $\lambda'$ is very close to $\lambda$, so $\dfrac{\lambda'}{\lambda} + \dfrac{\lambda}{\lambda'} \approx 2$. That gives $\Delta\lambda = \dfrac{h}{mc}(1 - \cos\phi)$.

Example 1

Find the momentum and the energy of an X-ray photon of wavelength $1.0\times10^{-10}$ m. ($h = 6.6\times10^{-34}$ J·s, $c = 3.0\times10^8$ m/s)

Show solution
  1. $p = \dfrac{h}{\lambda} = \dfrac{6.6\times10^{-34}}{1.0\times10^{-10}} = 6.6\times10^{-24}$ kg·m/s.
  2. $E = pc = 6.6\times10^{-24} \times 3.0\times10^8 = 2.0\times10^{-15}$ J.

$6.6\times10^{-24}$ kg·m/s; $2.0\times10^{-15}$ J

Example 2

Find $\dfrac{h}{mc}$ for an electron ($m = 9.1\times10^{-31}$ kg), and so the wavelength change when the photon is scattered through 90°.

Show solution
  1. $\dfrac{h}{mc} = \dfrac{6.6\times10^{-34}}{9.1\times10^{-31} \times 3.0\times10^8} = 2.4\times10^{-12}$ m.
  2. At 90°, $\cos\phi = 0$, so $\Delta\lambda = \dfrac{h}{mc} = 2.4\times10^{-12}$ m.

$2.4\times10^{-12}$ m for both

Example 3

At what angle is the change in wavelength largest, and what is it? What happens at $\phi = 0$?

Show solution
  1. $1 - \cos\phi$ is largest (= 2) at $\phi = 180°$: the photon bounces straight back. $\Delta\lambda = 2 \times 2.4\times10^{-12} = 4.8\times10^{-12}$ m.
  2. At $\phi = 0$, $\Delta\lambda = 0$: the photon misses and nothing changes.

At 180°: $4.8\times10^{-12}$ m; at 0°: no change

Example 4

X-rays of wavelength $5.0\times10^{-11}$ m are scattered through 90°. Find the new wavelength and the kinetic energy given to the electron.

Show solution
  1. $\lambda' = 5.0\times10^{-11} + 0.24\times10^{-11} = 5.24\times10^{-11}$ m.
  2. Energy lost by the photon: $hc\left(\dfrac1\lambda - \dfrac1{\lambda'}\right) = 1.98\times10^{-25} \times (2.000 - 1.908)\times10^{10} = 1.8\times10^{-16}$ J.
  3. That is the electron's kinetic energy (about 1.1 keV).

$5.2\times10^{-11}$ m; $1.8\times10^{-16}$ J

Example 5

A student writes the $x$-momentum of the scattered photon as $\dfrac{h}{\lambda'\cos\phi}$. What is wrong, and what is right?

Show solution
  1. The student split the wavelength into components. A wavelength is not a vector.
  2. Split the momentum vector instead: it has size $\dfrac{h}{\lambda'}$ along the angle $\phi$.
  3. Components: $\dfrac{h}{\lambda'}\cos\phi$ along $x$ and $\dfrac{h}{\lambda'}\sin\phi$ along $y$.

It should be $\dfrac{h}{\lambda'}\cos\phi$

Resolve the photon's momentum $h/\lambda'$ into components, never the wavelength. $\dfrac{h}{\lambda'\cos\phi}$ is a classic mistake.
The order never changes: ① and ② → remove the electron's angle (square and add); then ③ → remove $v$; then use $\dfrac{\lambda'}{\lambda} + \dfrac{\lambda}{\lambda'} \approx 2$.
  • Photon momentum $p = h/\lambda = hf/c$.
  • Elastic collision: momentum ($x$ and $y$) and energy are conserved.
  • $\Delta\lambda = \dfrac{h}{mc}(1 - \cos\phi)$; the scattered X-ray always has the longer wavelength.
