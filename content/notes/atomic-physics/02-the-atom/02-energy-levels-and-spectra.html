Energy Levels and Spectra

An electron jumping between levels gives out or takes in one photon with $hf = E_{n'} - E_n$, so hydrogen shows only certain wavelengths: a line spectrum

The Bohr model allows only the energies $E_n = -\dfrac{A}{n^2}$ (with $A = 13.6$ eV). Draw them as horizontal lines on an energy diagram:

  • $n = 1$: $-A$, the lowest, called the ground state;
  • $n = 2$: $-A/4$; $n = 3$: $-A/9$; $n = 4$: $-A/16$; ... crowding closer and closer together;
  • $n = \infty$: 0, the electron is free. All states with $n \ge 2$ are excited states.

Notice the spacing: $-A/4$ is already in the top quarter of the diagram, and the levels pile up just below 0.

The frequency condition. When the electron moves between two levels, it takes in or gives out exactly one photon, whose energy is the difference between the levels. Going up needs a photon of just the right energy (no more, no less); coming down gives one out. Since only certain differences exist, hot hydrogen glows at only certain wavelengths: a line spectrum.

The lines come in families, named by the level the electron falls to:

  • Lyman series: down to $n = 1$ (ultraviolet);
  • Balmer series: down to $n = 2$ (the visible lines);
  • Paschen series: down to $n = 3$ (infrared).

Within a series, the longest wavelength is the smallest drop (from the next level up) and the shortest wavelength is the biggest drop (from $n = \infty$).

The frequency condition (Bohr), with $n' > n$

$$hf = \frac{hc}{\lambda} = E_{n'} - E_n, \qquad \frac{1}{\lambda} = R\left(\frac{1}{n^2} - \frac{1}{n'^2}\right)$$

$E_n = -13.6/n^2$ eV. $R = \dfrac{13.6\ \text{eV}}{hc} = 1.10\times10^7$ /m (the Rydberg constant). $n$: the lower level ($n = 1$ Lyman, 2 Balmer, 3 Paschen).

  1. Energy is conserved: the photon carries away (or brings in) the difference $E_{n'} - E_n$.
  2. With $E_n = -\dfrac{A}{n^2}$: $E_{n'} - E_n = A\left(\dfrac1{n^2} - \dfrac1{n'^2}\right)$.
  3. Divide by $hc$: $\dfrac1\lambda = \dfrac{A}{hc}\left(\dfrac1{n^2} - \dfrac1{n'^2}\right)$, so $R = \dfrac{A}{hc} = \dfrac{13.6 \times 1.6\times10^{-19}}{6.6\times10^{-34} \times 3.0\times10^8} = 1.10\times10^7$ /m.

Example 1

A hydrogen electron drops from $n = 3$ to $n = 2$. Find the photon energy and wavelength. ($h = 6.6\times10^{-34}$ J·s, $c = 3.0\times10^8$ m/s)

Show solution
  1. $\Delta E = -1.51 - (-3.40) = 1.89$ eV $= 1.89 \times 1.6\times10^{-19} = 3.02\times10^{-19}$ J.
  2. $\lambda = \dfrac{hc}{\Delta E} = \dfrac{1.98\times10^{-25}}{3.02\times10^{-19}} = 6.6\times10^{-7}$ m: red light, the first Balmer line.

1.9 eV; $6.6\times10^{-7}$ m

Example 2

Find the longest wavelength in the Lyman series.

Show solution
  1. Longest wavelength = smallest drop down to $n = 1$: from $n = 2$. $\Delta E = 13.6 - 3.4 = 10.2$ eV.
  2. $\lambda = \dfrac{1.98\times10^{-25}}{10.2 \times 1.6\times10^{-19}} = 1.2\times10^{-7}$ m (ultraviolet).

$1.2\times10^{-7}$ m

Example 3

Find the shortest wavelength in the Balmer series.

Show solution
  1. Shortest = biggest drop down to $n = 2$: from $n = \infty$ ($E = 0$). $\Delta E = 3.4$ eV.
  2. $\lambda = \dfrac{1.98\times10^{-25}}{3.4 \times 1.6\times10^{-19}} = 3.6\times10^{-7}$ m.

$3.6\times10^{-7}$ m

Example 4

Hydrogen atoms in the ground state are hit by photons of 12.1 eV and by photons of 11.0 eV. Which are absorbed, and to which level?

Show solution
  1. From $n = 1$: to $n = 2$ needs 10.2 eV, to $n = 3$ needs $13.6 - 1.51 = 12.1$ eV, to $n = 4$ needs 12.75 eV.
  2. 12.1 eV matches $n = 1 \to 3$ exactly, so it is absorbed.
  3. 11.0 eV matches no gap, and a photon can't be partly absorbed, so it passes straight through.

12.1 eV is absorbed (to $n = 3$); 11.0 eV is not

Example 5

Electrons are in the $n = 4$ level. Counting every route down to $n = 1$, how many different spectral lines can appear?

Show solution
  1. Any pair of the levels 1, 2, 3, 4 gives one line: 4→3, 4→2, 4→1, 3→2, 3→1, 2→1.
  2. That is $\dfrac{4 \times 3}{2} = 6$.

6 lines

A series is named by the level the electron ends on (Lyman → 1, Balmer → 2), not where it starts. And "shortest wavelength" means the largest energy jump, from $n = \infty$.
Keep energies in eV until the end and use $hc = 1.98\times10^{-25}$ J·m, or the shortcut $\lambda\,(\text{m}) = \dfrac{1.24\times10^{-6}}{\Delta E\,(\text{eV})}$.
  • One photon per jump: $hf = E_{n'} - E_n$.
  • Ground state $n = 1$; excited states $n \ge 2$; free electron at $E = 0$.
  • Lyman → $n = 1$, Balmer → $n = 2$, Paschen → $n = 3$.
