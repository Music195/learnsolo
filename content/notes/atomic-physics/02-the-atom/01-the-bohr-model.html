The Bohr Model

The electron circles the nucleus as a particle and fits a whole number of its waves round the orbit; together these fix $r_n \propto n^2$ and $E_n \propto -1/n^2$

How the picture of the atom was found.

  • Thomson discovered the electron. Atoms are neutral, so he pictured a cloud of positive charge with electrons dotted through it.
  • Nagaoka pictured a heavy positive centre with electrons going round it.
  • Rutherford fired alpha particles (positive) at thin gold foil. If Thomson were right they would all pass almost straight through. Most did, but a few were deflected sharply and some bounced back. So the positive charge must be packed into a tiny, heavy nucleus, with the electrons round it.

Bohr then worked out the hydrogen atom (one proton, one electron) by treating the electron both ways at once:

  1. as a particle: it moves in a circle of radius $r$ at steady speed, and the Coulomb pull of the proton supplies the centripetal force;
  2. as a wave: its de Broglie wave must join up with itself round the orbit, making a standing wave. So the circumference must be a whole number $n$ of wavelengths. $n$ is called the quantum number.

Putting the two together, only certain radii and energies are allowed, set by $n = 1, 2, 3, \dots$. The radius goes as $n^2$ (1, 4, 9, ... times the smallest), and nothing in between is possible. The energy is negative (the electron is bound) and rises towards 0 as $n$ grows.

Like the Compton effect, this derivation is one to practise until you can do it on a blank sheet.

Bohr's two conditions and what they give

$$m\frac{v^2}{r} = k\frac{e^2}{r^2}, \quad 2\pi r = n\frac{h}{mv} \ \Rightarrow \ r_n = \frac{h^2}{4\pi^2kme^2}\,n^2, \quad E_n = -\frac{2\pi^2k^2me^4}{h^2}\cdot\frac{1}{n^2} = -\frac{13.6}{n^2}\ \text{eV}$$

$m$, $e$: electron mass and charge. $k = 9.0\times10^9$ N·m²/C². $n = 1, 2, 3, \dots$ (quantum number). $r_1 = 5.3\times10^{-11}$ m (the Bohr radius). $E_1 = -13.6$ eV $= -2.2\times10^{-18}$ J.

  1. Particle: $m\dfrac{v^2}{r} = k\dfrac{e^2}{r^2}$, so $mv^2 = \dfrac{ke^2}{r}$.
  2. Wave: $2\pi r = n\dfrac{h}{mv}$, so $v = \dfrac{nh}{2\pi mr}$ and $mv^2 = \dfrac{n^2h^2}{4\pi^2mr^2}$.
  3. Set the two expressions for $mv^2$ equal: $\dfrac{n^2h^2}{4\pi^2mr^2} = \dfrac{ke^2}{r}$, so $r = \dfrac{h^2}{4\pi^2kme^2}\,n^2$.
  4. Energy = kinetic + Coulomb potential: $E = \tfrac12mv^2 - \dfrac{ke^2}{r}$. Using step 1, $\tfrac12mv^2 = \dfrac{ke^2}{2r}$, so $E = -\dfrac{ke^2}{2r}$.
  5. Put in $r_n$: $E_n = -\dfrac{ke^2}{2}\cdot\dfrac{4\pi^2kme^2}{h^2n^2} = -\dfrac{2\pi^2k^2me^4}{h^2}\cdot\dfrac1{n^2}$. Everything in front is a constant; only $n$ changes.

Example 1

Find $r_1$ from the constants: $h = 6.6\times10^{-34}$ J·s, $k = 9.0\times10^9$ N·m²/C², $m = 9.1\times10^{-31}$ kg, $e = 1.6\times10^{-19}$ C.

Show solution
  1. Top: $h^2 = 4.36\times10^{-67}$.
  2. Bottom: $4\pi^2kme^2 = 39.5 \times 9.0\times10^9 \times 9.1\times10^{-31} \times 2.56\times10^{-38} = 8.28\times10^{-57}$.
  3. $r_1 = \dfrac{4.36\times10^{-67}}{8.28\times10^{-57}}$.

$5.3\times10^{-11}$ m

Example 2

Find the orbit radii for $n = 2$ and $n = 3$.

Show solution
  1. $r_n = r_1n^2$: $r_2 = 4 \times 5.3\times10^{-11} = 2.1\times10^{-10}$ m.
  2. $r_3 = 9 \times 5.3\times10^{-11} = 4.8\times10^{-10}$ m.

$2.1\times10^{-10}$ m and $4.8\times10^{-10}$ m

Example 3

In the $n = 1$ orbit the electron's speed is $2.2\times10^6$ m/s. Find its de Broglie wavelength and compare it with the circumference $2\pi r_1$.

Show solution
  1. $\lambda = \dfrac{h}{mv} = \dfrac{6.6\times10^{-34}}{9.1\times10^{-31} \times 2.2\times10^6} = 3.3\times10^{-10}$ m.
  2. $2\pi r_1 = 2\pi \times 5.3\times10^{-11} = 3.3\times10^{-10}$ m.
  3. They are equal: exactly one wavelength fits round the $n = 1$ orbit.

$3.3\times10^{-10}$ m, the same as the circumference

Example 4

Find $E_2$ and $E_3$ for hydrogen, and the energy needed to lift the electron from $n = 1$ to $n = 2$.

Show solution
  1. $E_2 = -13.6/4 = -3.4$ eV; $E_3 = -13.6/9 = -1.5$ eV.
  2. From $n = 1$ to $n = 2$: $E_2 - E_1 = -3.4 - (-13.6) = 10.2$ eV.

$-3.4$ eV, $-1.5$ eV; 10.2 eV

Example 5

How much energy, in joules, frees the electron completely from a hydrogen atom in its lowest state?

Show solution
  1. Free means $n \to \infty$, where $E = 0$. From $E_1 = -13.6$ eV the electron needs 13.6 eV.
  2. $13.6 \times 1.6\times10^{-19} = 2.2\times10^{-18}$ J.

13.6 eV = $2.2\times10^{-18}$ J

The energies are negative, and $n = 1$ is the lowest, not the highest. Bigger $n$ means a bigger orbit and an energy closer to 0.
The derivation is short if you aim at $mv^2$: get it once from the circular motion and once from the standing-wave condition, set them equal for $r$, then use $\tfrac12mv^2 = \dfrac{ke^2}{2r}$ for the energy.
  • Particle: $mv^2/r = ke^2/r^2$. Wave: $2\pi r = nh/(mv)$.
  • $r_n = r_1n^2$ with $r_1 = 5.3\times10^{-11}$ m.
  • $E_n = -13.6/n^2$ eV; both depend only on the quantum number $n$.
