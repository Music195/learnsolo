Half-Life

Every half-life, half of the nuclei that are still left decay: $N = N_0(1/2)^{t/T}$

You can't say when one particular nucleus will decay; it is a matter of chance. But with huge numbers of nuclei the pattern is very regular: in any fixed time, the same fraction of the remaining nuclei decays.

The half-life $T$ is the time for the number of remaining (undecayed) radioactive nuclei to fall to half. It depends on the kind of nucleus: seconds, days, or billions of years.

  • after $T$: $\tfrac12$ left;
  • after $2T$: half of that, $\tfrac14$ left;
  • after $3T$: $\tfrac18$ left; and so on, never quite reaching zero.

The graph of $N$ against $t$ is a curve that halves every $T$ (not a straight line).

Always ask: is the question about the nuclei left or the nuclei that have decayed? If $\tfrac14$ has decayed, $\tfrac34$ is left, and that $\tfrac34$ is what goes into the formula.

Number of nuclei remaining after time $t$

$$N = N_0\left(\frac12\right)^{t/T}$$

$N_0$: number of radioactive nuclei at the start. $N$: number still undecayed after time $t$. $T$: half-life (same time unit as $t$).

  1. Each half-life multiplies the number left by $\tfrac12$. After $t$, there have been $t/T$ half-lives, so $N = N_0 \times \left(\tfrac12\right)^{t/T}$.
  2. This also works when $t/T$ is not a whole number. To find $t$, take logs: $\log\dfrac{N}{N_0} = \dfrac{t}{T}\log\dfrac12$, so $t = T\,\dfrac{\log(N_0/N)}{\log 2}$.

Example 1

A substance has a half-life of 5 days. What fraction is left after 20 days?

Show solution
  1. $t/T = 20/5 = 4$ half-lives.
  2. $\left(\tfrac12\right)^4 = \tfrac1{16}$.

$\tfrac1{16}$

Example 2

After 15 years, $\tfrac18$ of a sample is left. Find the half-life.

Show solution
  1. $\tfrac18 = \left(\tfrac12\right)^3$, so 15 years is 3 half-lives.
  2. $T = 15/3$.

5 years

Example 3

The half-life of radium is $1.6\times10^3$ years. How long until $\tfrac14$ of a sample has decayed? ($\log 2 = 0.301$, $\log 3 = 0.477$)

Show solution
  1. The trap: $\tfrac14$ decayed means $\tfrac34$ is left, not $\tfrac14$ left (that would be 2 half-lives).
  2. $\tfrac34 = \left(\tfrac12\right)^{t/1600}$. Take logs: $\log 3 - 2\log 2 = -\dfrac{t}{1600}\log 2$.
  3. $t = 1600 \times \dfrac{2\log2 - \log3}{\log2} = 1600 \times \dfrac{0.602 - 0.477}{0.301} = 1600 \times 0.415$.
  4. (With the rougher values $\log 2 = 0.30$, $\log 3 = 0.48$ you get $1600 \times 0.40 = 640$ years.)

About $6.6\times10^2$ years

Example 4

Carbon-14 has a half-life of 5730 years. Old wood has $\tfrac14$ of the carbon-14 that living wood has. How old is it?

Show solution
  1. Here $\tfrac14$ is what is left: $\left(\tfrac12\right)^2$, so 2 half-lives.
  2. $2 \times 5730 = 11460$ years.

About $1.1\times10^4$ years

Example 5

A sample starts with $8.0\times10^6$ radioactive nuclei; the half-life is 10 minutes. How many nuclei have decayed after 30 minutes?

Show solution
  1. 3 half-lives: left $= 8.0\times10^6 \times \tfrac18 = 1.0\times10^6$.
  2. Decayed $= 8.0\times10^6 - 1.0\times10^6$.

$7.0\times10^6$

The formula gives the number left. If a question says "$\tfrac14$ has decayed", put $\tfrac34$ into it, not $\tfrac14$.
If the fraction left is $\tfrac12$, $\tfrac14$, $\tfrac18$, ..., just count half-lives. Use logs only when it isn't a power of $\tfrac12$.
  • $N = N_0(1/2)^{t/T}$, where $N$ is the number remaining.
  • Each half-life halves what is left: $\tfrac12$, $\tfrac14$, $\tfrac18$, ...
  • Decayed = $N_0 - N$.
