Mass Defect and Binding Energy

A nucleus weighs less than its separate protons and neutrons; the missing mass $\Delta m$ is the binding energy, $\Delta E = \Delta mc^2$

Weigh a workbook (100 g) and its answer booklet (50 g) separately, then slip the booklet inside and weigh them together: 150 g, of course. In the nucleus this fails. A helium nucleus ${}^4_2\mathrm{He}$ (2 protons + 2 neutrons) is lighter than 2 free protons plus 2 free neutrons. The difference is the mass defect $\Delta m$.

Why? Mass is energy. Einstein's $E = mc^2$ says mass and energy are two forms of the same thing: mass is a store of energy. With $c = 3.0\times10^8$ m/s, $c^2$ is enormous, so a tiny mass holds a huge energy (a pencil's worth of mass could flatten a city).

The nuclear force. Protons repel each other by the Coulomb force, so why does a nucleus hold together? A much stronger attraction, the nuclear force, acts between protons and neutrons at very short range. (Gravity is far weaker than the Coulomb force; the nuclear force is stronger still.)

A bound nucleus is in a low-energy, stable state. Pulling it apart into separate protons and neutrons takes a lot of energy, the binding energy $\Delta E$. The separate particles have that much more energy, and so, by $E = mc^2$, more mass: $\Delta E = \Delta m\,c^2$. Dividing by $c^2$ makes $\Delta m$ tiny (under 1%), but in the nucleus it can't be ignored.

Binding energy per nucleon ($\Delta E / A$) shows how firmly each particle is held. It rises to a peak near iron (about 8.8 MeV) and falls slowly for heavy nuclei. Joining light nuclei (fusion) or splitting heavy ones (fission) both move towards iron and release energy.

For a nucleus ${}^A_Z\mathrm{X}$ of mass $M$

$$\Delta m = Zm_p + (A - Z)m_n - M, \qquad \Delta E = \Delta m\,c^2, \qquad 1\ \text{u} \to 931\ \text{MeV}$$

$m_p$, $m_n$: proton and neutron masses ($m_p = 1.0073$ u, $m_n = 1.0087$ u). $Z$ protons and $A - Z$ neutrons. 1 u (atomic mass unit) $= 1.66\times10^{-27}$ kg, 1/12 of a carbon-12 atom. 1 MeV $= 10^6$ eV.

  1. Mass of 1 u in kg: $1.66\times10^{-27}$ kg.
  2. Its energy: $E = mc^2 = 1.66\times10^{-27} \times (3.0\times10^8)^2 = 1.49\times10^{-10}$ J.
  3. In eV: divide by $1.6\times10^{-19}$: $9.3\times10^8$ eV $= 9.3\times10^2$ MeV.
  4. With more exact constants this is 931 MeV. So $\Delta E$ (MeV) $\approx 931 \times \Delta m$ (u): a big shortcut in multiple-choice questions.

Example 1

Find the mass defect of ${}^4_2\mathrm{He}$. ($m_p = 1.0073$ u, $m_n = 1.0087$ u, He nucleus 4.0015 u)

Show solution
  1. Separate: $2 \times 1.0073 + 2 \times 1.0087 = 4.0320$ u.
  2. $\Delta m = 4.0320 - 4.0015 = 0.0305$ u.

0.0305 u

Example 2

Find the binding energy of ${}^4_2\mathrm{He}$ in joules and in MeV, working through kg. (1 u $= 1.66\times10^{-27}$ kg)

Show solution
  1. $\Delta m = 0.0305 \times 1.66\times10^{-27} = 5.06\times10^{-29}$ kg.
  2. $\Delta E = \Delta mc^2 = 5.06\times10^{-29} \times 9.0\times10^{16} = 4.6\times10^{-12}$ J.
  3. In MeV: $\dfrac{4.56\times10^{-12}}{1.6\times10^{-19}} \times 10^{-6} = 28.5$ MeV. (The shortcut $931 \times 0.0305 = 28.4$ MeV agrees.)

$4.6\times10^{-12}$ J, about 28.5 MeV

Example 3

A carbon-12 nucleus has mass 11.9967 u. Find its mass defect, binding energy and binding energy per nucleon.

Show solution
  1. 6 protons + 6 neutrons: $6 \times 1.0073 + 6 \times 1.0087 = 12.0960$ u.
  2. $\Delta m = 12.0960 - 11.9967 = 0.0993$ u.
  3. $\Delta E = 931 \times 0.0993 = 92$ MeV; per nucleon $92/12 = 7.7$ MeV.

0.0993 u; about 92 MeV; 7.7 MeV per nucleon

Example 4

How much energy is 1.0 g of mass equivalent to?

Show solution
  1. $E = mc^2 = 1.0\times10^{-3} \times (3.0\times10^8)^2$.

$9.0\times10^{13}$ J

Example 5

${}^{235}\mathrm{U}$ (7.6 MeV per nucleon) splits into two nuclei near $A = 117$ (8.5 MeV per nucleon). Roughly how much energy is released?

Show solution
  1. The 235 nucleons end up more tightly held: each by about $8.5 - 7.6 = 0.9$ MeV more.
  2. Energy released $\approx 235 \times 0.9 = 2\times10^2$ MeV.

About 200 MeV per fission

The separate particles are heavier; the nucleus is lighter. Binding energy is the energy needed to pull the nucleus apart (it is not stored inside the nucleus waiting to come out).
Remember "1 u ≈ 931 MeV". For a multiple-choice answer, multiply the mass defect in u by 931 and skip the kg and joule steps.
  • $\Delta m = Zm_p + (A - Z)m_n - M$.
  • Binding energy $\Delta E = \Delta mc^2$; 1 u ≈ 931 MeV.
  • Binding energy per nucleon peaks near iron.
