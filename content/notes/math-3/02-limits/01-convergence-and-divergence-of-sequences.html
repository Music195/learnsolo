Convergence and Divergence of Sequences

Where does the sequence end up?

Look at the terms $a_1, a_2, a_3, \dots$ as $n$ gets larger and larger. If they get as close as you like to one number $\alpha$, the sequence converges to $\alpha$, and $\alpha$ is its limit.

If not, it diverges. This happens in three ways: it grows without bound ($+\infty$), falls without bound ($-\infty$), or oscillates (vibrates and never settles).

The four possible outcomes

$$\text{converges: } \lim_{n \to \infty} a_n = \alpha$$
$$\text{diverges: } \lim_{n \to \infty} a_n = \infty, \qquad \lim_{n \to \infty} a_n = -\infty, \qquad \text{or oscillates}$$

Basic facts: $\displaystyle\lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{1}{n} = 0$, $\;\displaystyle\lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{1}{n^k} = 0$ ($k > 0$), $\;\displaystyle\lim_{n \to \infty} n^k = \infty$ ($k > 0$).

  1. For a fraction like $\dfrac{3n + 1}{2n - 1}$, both top and bottom go to $\infty$, so you can't read off the limit yet.
  2. Divide top and bottom by the highest power in the denominator ($n$): $\dfrac{3 + \frac{1}{n}}{2 - \frac{1}{n}}$.
  3. Now every $\dfrac{1}{n}$ goes to $0$, leaving $\dfrac{3}{2}$.

Plot the terms $(n, a_n)$

Example 1

Find $\displaystyle\lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{3n + 1}{2n - 1}$.

Show solution
  1. Divide top and bottom by $n$: $\dfrac{3 + \frac{1}{n}}{2 - \frac{1}{n}} \to \dfrac{3 + 0}{2 - 0}$.

$\dfrac{3}{2}$

Example 2

Find $\displaystyle\lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{2n^2 - 1}{n + 3}$.

Show solution
  1. Divide top and bottom by $n$: $\dfrac{2n - \frac{1}{n}}{1 + \frac{3}{n}}$.
  2. The top goes to $\infty$ and the bottom to $1$.

$\infty$ (diverges to positive infinity)

Example 3

Does $a_n = \dfrac{(-1)^n}{\sqrt{n}}$ converge?

Show solution
  1. The signs alternate, but the size is $|a_n| = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{n}}$, which goes to $0$.
  2. So the terms crowd in on $0$ from both sides (the odd terms from below, the even terms from above).

Yes: $\displaystyle\lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{(-1)^n}{\sqrt{n}} = 0$

Example 4

Find $\displaystyle\lim_{n \to \infty} \left(\sqrt{n + 1} - \sqrt{n}\right)$.

Show solution
  1. It is "$\infty - \infty$", so rewrite it. Multiply top and bottom by $\sqrt{n + 1} + \sqrt{n}$:
  2. $\dfrac{(n + 1) - n}{\sqrt{n + 1} + \sqrt{n}} = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{n + 1} + \sqrt{n}}$, and the bottom goes to $\infty$.

$0$

$\dfrac{\infty}{\infty}$ and $\infty - \infty$ have no fixed value. For example, $n^2 - n \to \infty$ but $(n + 1) - n \to 1$. Rewrite first (divide, or rationalise), then take the limit.
A changing sign does not mean divergence. $(-1)^n$ oscillates, but $\dfrac{(-1)^n}{n}$ converges to $0$, because the size of the terms shrinks to $0$.
  • Converge to $\alpha$, diverge to $\pm\infty$, or oscillate.
  • Fractions: divide top and bottom by the highest power in the denominator.
  • Differences of roots: multiply by the conjugate $\sqrt{A} + \sqrt{B}$.
