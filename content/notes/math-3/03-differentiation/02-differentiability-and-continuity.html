Differentiability and Continuity

No break, and no sharp corner

Continuous at $a$: the graph has no break there, so $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = f(a)$.

Differentiable at $a$: the graph has a single tangent there, so $f'(a)$ exists as a finite number. The slopes from the right and from the left must agree.

Differentiable is the stronger condition. A corner like $|x|$ at $0$ is continuous but has two different one-sided slopes, so it is not differentiable.

The one-way implication

$$f \text{ differentiable at } a \implies f \text{ continuous at } a$$

The converse is false. To test differentiability, compare $\displaystyle\lim_{h \to +0} \frac{f(a + h) - f(a)}{h}$ with $\displaystyle\lim_{h \to -0} \frac{f(a + h) - f(a)}{h}$.

  1. For $h \ne 0$: $f(a + h) - f(a) = \dfrac{f(a + h) - f(a)}{h} \cdot h$.
  2. As $h \to 0$ the fraction tends to $f'(a)$ (a finite number) and $h \to 0$, so the product tends to $f'(a) \cdot 0 = 0$.
  3. So $\displaystyle\lim_{h \to 0} f(a + h) = f(a)$, which means $f$ is continuous at $a$.

Slopes from the right and from the left at $x = 0$

Example 1

Is $f(x) = |x - 1|$ continuous at $x = 1$? Differentiable?

Show solution
  1. $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to 1} |x - 1| = 0 = f(1)$, so it is continuous.
  2. Right: $\dfrac{|h|}{h} = 1$ for $h > 0$. Left: $\dfrac{|h|}{h} = -1$ for $h < 0$. They differ.

Continuous but not differentiable at $x = 1$

Example 2

Is $f(x) = x|x|$ differentiable at $x = 0$?

Show solution
  1. $\dfrac{f(h) - f(0)}{h} = \dfrac{h|h|}{h} = |h| \to 0$ from both sides.

Yes, $f'(0) = 0$

Example 3

$f(x) = x^2$ for $x \le 1$ and $f(x) = ax + b$ for $x > 1$. Find $a$, $b$ so that $f$ is differentiable at $x = 1$.

Show solution
  1. Differentiable needs continuous first: the right-hand limit $a + b$ must equal $f(1) = 1$.
  2. The slopes must match: $x^2$ has slope $2$ at $x = 1$ and the line has slope $a$, so $a = 2$.
  3. Then $b = 1 - a = -1$.

$a = 2$, $b = -1$

$\sqrt[3]{x}$ at $0$ has a tangent line, but it is vertical: the difference quotient $\dfrac{\sqrt[3]{h}}{h} = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt[3]{h^2}} \to \infty$. An infinite slope is not a derivative, so it is not differentiable there.
For a function glued together from two pieces, check in order: (1) the pieces meet (continuity), (2) their slopes at the join agree (differentiability).
  • Differentiable $\Rightarrow$ continuous, but not the other way round.
  • Not differentiable at: jumps, corners ($|x|$), vertical tangents ($\sqrt[3]{x}$).
  • Differentiable at $a$ $\iff$ right and left slope limits exist and are equal.
