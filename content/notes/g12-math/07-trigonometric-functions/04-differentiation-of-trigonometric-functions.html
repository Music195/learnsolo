Differentiation of Trigonometric Functions

Concept — The Fundamental Trigonometric Limit & Derivatives

The entire calculus of trigonometric functions rests on one fundamental geometric limit (with $x$ measured in radians):

$$\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\sin x}{x} = 1$$

Using this limit and the definition of the derivative $\frac{dy}{dx} = \lim_{\delta x \to 0}\frac{\delta y}{\delta x}$, we find that the instantaneous rate of change (tangent slope) of $\sin x$ at any angle $x$ is $\cos x$, and the tangent slope of $\cos x$ is $-\sin x$.

Core Formulas — Derivatives of the Six Trigonometric Functions & Chain Rule

For any differentiable function $u = u(x)$ (with angles in radians):

  • 1. Sine: $\dfrac{d}{dx}(\sin x) = \cos x \qquad \Longrightarrow \qquad \dfrac{d}{dx}(\sin u) = \cos u \cdot \dfrac{du}{dx}$
  • 2. Cosine: $\dfrac{d}{dx}(\cos x) = -\sin x \qquad \Longrightarrow \qquad \dfrac{d}{dx}(\cos u) = -\sin u \cdot \dfrac{du}{dx}$
  • 3. Tangent: $\dfrac{d}{dx}(\tan x) = \sec^2 x \qquad \Longrightarrow \qquad \dfrac{d}{dx}(\tan u) = \sec^2 u \cdot \dfrac{du}{dx}$
  • 4. Cotangent: $\dfrac{d}{dx}(\cot x) = -\csc^2 x \qquad \Longrightarrow \qquad \dfrac{d}{dx}(\cot u) = -\csc^2 u \cdot \dfrac{du}{dx}$
  • 5. Secant: $\dfrac{d}{dx}(\sec x) = \sec x\tan x \qquad \Longrightarrow \qquad \dfrac{d}{dx}(\sec u) = \sec u\tan u \cdot \dfrac{du}{dx}$
  • 6. Cosecant: $\dfrac{d}{dx}(\csc x) = -\csc x\cot x \qquad \Longrightarrow \qquad \dfrac{d}{dx}(\csc u) = -\csc u\cot u \cdot \dfrac{du}{dx}$
Proof — Geometric Proof of $\lim_{x \to 0}\frac{\sin x}{x} = 1$ & First-Principles Derivative of $\sin x$

1. Unit-Circle Squeeze Proof of $\lim_{x \to 0}\frac{\sin x}{x} = 1$: In a unit circle ($OA = OB = 1$) with central angle $\angle AOB = x$ radians ($0 < x < \frac{\pi}{2}$), drop perpendicular $BD \perp OA$ and erect tangent $AC \perp OA$. Comparing the vertical lengths with circular arc $\widehat{AB}$ gives:

$$BD < \text{arc } AB < AC \implies \sin x < x < \tan x$$

Dividing through by $\sin x > 0$ yields $1 < \dfrac{x}{\sin x} < \dfrac{1}{\cos x}$, and taking reciprocals reverses the inequalities:

$$\cos x < \frac{\sin x}{x} < 1$$

Since $\lim_{x \to 0}\cos x = 1$ and $\lim_{x \to 0} 1 = 1$, the Squeeze Theorem forces $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to 0}\frac{\sin x}{x} = 1$.

2. First-Principles Proof of $\frac{d}{dx}(\sin x) = \cos x$: Let $y = \sin x$ and $y + \delta y = \sin(x + \delta x)$. Using the sum-to-product identity $\sin\alpha - \sin\beta = 2\cos\frac{\alpha + \beta}{2}\sin\frac{\alpha - \beta}{2}$:

$$\delta y = \sin(x + \delta x) - \sin x = 2\cos\!\left(x + \frac{\delta x}{2}\right)\sin\!\left(\frac{\delta x}{2}\right)$$ $$\frac{dy}{dx} = \lim_{\delta x \to 0}\frac{\delta y}{\delta x} = \lim_{\delta x \to 0}\cos\!\left(x + \frac{\delta x}{2}\right) \cdot \lim_{\frac{\delta x}{2} \to 0}\frac{\sin(\delta x / 2)}{\delta x / 2} = \cos x \times 1 = \cos x \quad \blacksquare$$

3. Quotient-Rule Proof of $\frac{d}{dx}(\tan x) = \sec^2 x$:

$$\frac{d}{dx}\left(\frac{\sin x}{\cos x}\right) = \frac{\cos x(\cos x) - \sin x(-\sin x)}{\cos^2 x} = \frac{\cos^2 x + \sin^2 x}{\cos^2 x} = \frac{1}{\cos^2 x} = \sec^2 x \quad \blacksquare$$

Worked Examples — Textbook Examples 9, 10, 11 & 12

Example 9 & 10 (Chain Rule, Product Rule & Quotient Rule with Trig Functions): Differentiate with respect to $x$:
(a) $y = \cos(7x^2 - 2)$   (b) $y = 5\sec(3x + 1)$   (c) $y = \tan^2(x^2)$   (d) $y = \dfrac{\tan x}{x}$.

Show step-by-step solution

(a) $\dfrac{d}{dx}\cos(7x^2 - 2) = -\sin(7x^2 - 2) \cdot \dfrac{d}{dx}(7x^2 - 2) = -14x\sin(7x^2 - 2)$.

(b) $\dfrac{d}{dx}[5\sec(3x + 1)] = 5\sec(3x + 1)\tan(3x + 1) \cdot \dfrac{d}{dx}(3x + 1) = 15\sec(3x + 1)\tan(3x + 1)$.

(c) Rewrite $y = [\tan(x^2)]^2$ and apply the chain rule from outside in (power $\to$ trig $\to$ inner $x^2$):

$$\frac{dy}{dx} = 2\tan(x^2) \cdot \frac{d}{dx}[\tan(x^2)] = 2\tan(x^2)\sec^2(x^2) \cdot (2x) = 4x\tan(x^2)\sec^2(x^2)$$

(d) By the quotient rule:

$$\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{x\frac{d}{dx}(\tan x) - \tan x\frac{d}{dx}(x)}{x^2} = \frac{x\sec^2 x - \tan x}{x^2}$$

Answer: (a) $-14x\sin(7x^2 - 2)$, (b) $15\sec(3x + 1)\tan(3x + 1)$, (c) $4x\tan(x^2)\sec^2(x^2)$, (d) $\dfrac{x\sec^2 x - \tan x}{x^2}$.

Example 11 (Implicit Differentiation with Trigonometric Functions): Given that $x + \sin y = \cos(xy)$, find $\dfrac{dy}{dx}$.

Show step-by-step solution

Differentiate both sides with respect to $x$, using the chain rule on $\sin y$ and $\cos(xy)$ and the product rule on $xy$:

$$1 + \cos y \cdot \frac{dy}{dx} = -\sin(xy) \cdot \frac{d}{dx}(xy) = -\sin(xy)\left(x\frac{dy}{dx} + y\right)$$

Expand and collect all terms containing $\dfrac{dy}{dx}$ on the left side:

$$1 + \cos y\frac{dy}{dx} = -x\sin(xy)\frac{dy}{dx} - y\sin(xy)$$ $$\big(\cos y + x\sin(xy)\big)\frac{dy}{dx} = -\big(1 + y\sin(xy)\big) \implies \frac{dy}{dx} = -\frac{1 + y\sin(xy)}{\cos y + x\sin(xy)}$$

Answer: $\dfrac{dy}{dx} = -\dfrac{1 + y\sin(xy)}{\cos y + x\sin(xy)}$.

Example 12 (Second-Order Derivative): Given that $y = x\sin x$, find $\dfrac{d^2y}{dx^2}$.

Show step-by-step solution

Apply the product rule for the first derivative:

$$\frac{dy}{dx} = x\cos x + \sin x$$

Differentiate a second time with respect to $x$:

$$\frac{d^2y}{dx^2} = \big(x(-\sin x) + \cos x\big) + \cos x = 2\cos x - x\sin x$$

Answer: $\dfrac{d^2y}{dx^2} = 2\cos x - x\sin x$.

Common Mistakes to Avoid
  • Sign Rule for "Co-" Functions: Every trigonometric function starting with "co-" ($\cos u, \cot u, \csc u$) has a negative sign in its derivative ($-\sin u, -\csc^2 u, -\csc u\cot u$), while $\sin u, \tan u, \sec u$ have positive signs!
  • Distinguishing $\tan^2 x$ from $\tan(x^2)$: $\tan^2 x = (\tan x)^2$ has derivative $2\tan x\sec^2 x$, whereas $\tan(x^2)$ has derivative $2x\sec^2(x^2)$.
Exam Tip — Remembering $\lim_{x \to 0}\frac{\sin kx}{x} = k$

Whenever evaluating limits of the form $\lim_{x \to 0}\frac{\sin(kx)}{x}$, multiply and divide by $k$ so that $\lim_{x \to 0} k \cdot \frac{\sin(kx)}{kx} = k \times 1 = k$.

Key Takeaways (မှတ်သားရန် အချက်များ)
  • Fundamental Limit: $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to 0}\frac{\sin x}{x} = 1$ ($x$ in radians).
  • Primary Derivatives: $\dfrac{d}{dx}\sin u = \cos u\dfrac{du}{dx}$, $\dfrac{d}{dx}\cos u = -\sin u\dfrac{du}{dx}$, and $\dfrac{d}{dx}\tan u = \sec^2 u\dfrac{du}{dx}$.
  • Reciprocal Derivatives: $\dfrac{d}{dx}\cot u = -\csc^2 u\dfrac{du}{dx}$, $\dfrac{d}{dx}\sec u = \sec u\tan u\dfrac{du}{dx}$, and $\dfrac{d}{dx}\csc u = -\csc u\cot u\dfrac{du}{dx}$.
