Chapter 9: Application of Differentiation

9.3 Concavity, Points of Inflection, Second Derivative Test & Optimization

The second derivative $f''(x)$ measures how the slope $f'(x)$ itself changes, determining the concavity of a curve, locating points of inflection, and solving real-world optimization problems.

1. Concavity and the Second Derivative

Even when two curves are both increasing on an interval, one may bend upward while the other bends downward. This bending property is called concavity.

Definition — Concave Up and Concave Down

Let $f$ be differentiable on an open interval $I$:

  • The graph of $f$ is concave up on $I$ if $f'$ is increasing on $I$ (the curve lies above its tangent lines on $I$).
  • The graph of $f$ is concave down on $I$ if $f'$ is decreasing on $I$ (the curve lies below its tangent lines on $I$).
Test for Concavity

Let $f$ be a function whose second derivative $f''$ exists on an open interval $I$:

  • If $f''(x) > 0$ for all $x \in I$, then the graph of $f$ is concave up on $I$.
  • If $f''(x) < 0$ for all $x \in I$, then the graph of $f$ is concave down on $I$.

2. Points of Inflection

Definition — Point of Inflection

A point $P(c, f(c))$ on a curve $y = f(x)$ is called a point of inflection (or inflection point) if $f$ is continuous at $c$ and the curve changes concavity from concave up to concave down (or from concave down to concave up) at $P$.

At any point of inflection $(c, f(c))$, either: $$f''(c) = 0 \qquad \text{or} \qquad f''(c) \text{ does not exist.}$$

Caution (Example 13): The condition $f''(c) = 0$ is necessary (when $f''$ exists) but not sufficient! For $f(x) = x^6$, $f''(x) = 30x^4$ satisfies $f''(0) = 0$, yet $f''(x) > 0$ for all $x \ne 0$. Since concavity does not change at $x = 0$, $(0, 0)$ is not an inflection point.

3. The Second Derivative Test for Local Extrema

Second Derivative Test

Suppose $f''$ is continuous on an open interval containing $x = c$, with $f'(c) = 0$:

  1. If $f''(c) < 0$, then $f$ has a local maximum at $x = c$ (graph is concave down).
  2. If $f''(c) > 0$, then $f$ has a local minimum at $x = c$ (graph is concave up).
  3. If $f''(c) = 0$, the test fails (is inconclusive); $f$ may have a local maximum, a local minimum, or neither — use the First Derivative Test instead.

5. Worked Textbook Examples

Example 11 (Textbook p. 158) — Concavity of $f(x) = \dfrac{1}{x^2 + 1}$

Determine the intervals on which the graph of $f(x) = \dfrac{1}{x^2 + 1}$ is concave up or concave down, and find its points of inflection.

Solution:

Differentiating twice: $$f'(x) = -\frac{2x}{(x^2 + 1)^2}$$ $$f''(x) = -\frac{(x^2 + 1)^2(2) - 2x \cdot 2(x^2 + 1)(2x)}{(x^2 + 1)^4} = \frac{2(3x^2 - 1)}{(x^2 + 1)^3}$$ Setting $f''(x) = 0$ gives $3x^2 - 1 = 0 \implies x = -\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{3}}$ or $x = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{3}}$.

  • On $\left(-\infty, -\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\right)$ and $\left(\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{3}}, \infty\right)$: $f''(x) > 0 \implies$ concave up.
  • On $\left(-\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{3}}, \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\right)$: $f''(x) < 0 \implies$ concave down.
  • The points of inflection are $\left(-\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{3}}, \dfrac{3}{4}\right)$ and $\left(\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{3}}, \dfrac{3}{4}\right)$.
Example 14 (Textbook p. 160) — $f(x) = x^4 - 4x^3 + 5$

Find the critical points, inflection points, and local extrema of $f(x) = x^4 - 4x^3 + 5$.

Solution:

We compute $f'(x) = 4x^3 - 12x^2 = 4x^2(x - 3)$ and $f''(x) = 12x^2 - 24x = 12x(x - 2)$.

  • Critical points: $f'(x) = 0 \implies x = 0, 3$, giving $(0, 5)$ and $(3, -22)$.
  • Second Derivative Test: At $x = 3$, $f''(3) = 36 > 0 \implies f(3) = -22$ is a local minimum value. At $x = 0$, $f''(0) = 0$ (test fails); since $f'(x) < 0$ on both $(-1, 0)$ and $(0, 3)$, there is no local extremum at $x = 0$.
  • Inflection points: $f''(x) = 0 \implies x = 0, 2$. Since $f''(x)$ changes sign at both $x = 0$ and $x = 2$, $(0, 5)$ and $(2, -11)$ are points of inflection.
Example 15 & Example 16 (Textbook pp. 161–162) — Range and Applied Optimization

(a) Example 15: Find the range of the function $f(x) = \ln x - x$.
Solution: The domain is $(0, \infty)$. Differentiating gives $f'(x) = \dfrac{1}{x} - 1 = 0 \implies x = 1$. Since $f''(x) = -\dfrac{1}{x^2} < 0$ for all $x > 0$, $f''(1) = -1 < 0$, so $f(1) = \ln 1 - 1 = -1$ is the global maximum value on $(0, \infty)$. Thus the range is $\{y \in \mathbb{R} : y \le -1\}$ or $(-\infty, -1]$.

(b) Example 16: An open cylindrical vessel is to be made of thin sheet metal to hold $400\pi\text{ cm}^3$ of water. Find the radius $r$ and minimum surface area $A$ of sheet metal required.
Solution: The volume is $V = \pi r^2 h = 400\pi \implies h = \dfrac{400}{r^2}$. The surface area of the open cylinder (circular base + curved side) is: $$A(r) = \pi r^2 + 2\pi r h = \pi r^2 + 2\pi r\left(\frac{400}{r^2}\right) = \pi r^2 + \frac{800\pi}{r} \qquad (r > 0)$$ Differentiating with respect to $r$: $$A'(r) = 2\pi r - \frac{800\pi}{r^2} = 0 \implies 2\pi r^3 = 800\pi \implies r^3 = 400 \implies r = \sqrt[3]{400}\text{ cm}$$ Since $A''(r) = 2\pi + \dfrac{1600\pi}{r^3} > 0$ for all $r > 0$, $r = \sqrt[3]{400}\text{ cm}$ gives the absolute minimum surface area: $$A_{\min} = \pi(400)^{2/3} + \frac{800\pi}{(400)^{1/3}} = \pi(400)^{2/3} + 2\pi(400)^{2/3} = 3\pi(400)^{2/3}\text{ cm}^2$$
