Chapter 9: Application of Differentiation

Tangent Line and Normal Line

Differentiation gives the exact instantaneous slope of a curve at any point, enabling us to construct both the tangent line that touches the curve and the normal line perpendicular to it.

1. Inclination and Slope of a Line

Every non-horizontal line $L$ intersects the $x$-axis and forms an angle of inclination with the positive direction of the $x$-axis.

Definition — Inclination and Slope

The inclination of a line $L$ is the smallest counter-clockwise angle $\theta$ ($0^\circ \le \theta < 180^\circ$, or $0 \le \theta < \pi$) measured from the positive direction of the $x$-axis to the line $L$. The inclination of a horizontal line (parallel to the $x$-axis) is $0^\circ$.

The slope (or gradient) $m$ of the line $L$ is defined as the tangent of its angle of inclination: $$m = \tan \theta \qquad (\theta \ne 90^\circ)$$

Two-Point Slope Formula

If $P(x_1, y_1)$ and $Q(x_2, y_2)$ are any two distinct points on a non-vertical line ($x_1 \ne x_2$), then its slope is: $$m = \tan \theta = \frac{y_2 - y_1}{x_2 - x_1}$$

Parallel and Perpendicular Lines

Let two non-vertical lines have slopes $m_1$ and $m_2$:

  • Parallel lines: $m_1 = m_2$
  • Perpendicular lines: $m_1 m_2 = -1$, so $m_2 = -\dfrac{1}{m_1}$

2. Equations of the Tangent Line and Normal Line

Let $y = f(x)$ be a differentiable function at $x = x_1$. As a nearby point $Q(x_2, y_2)$ on the curve approaches $P(x_1, y_1)$, the secant line $PQ$ approaches the tangent line at $P$, and its slope approaches the derivative $y'(x_1) = f'(x_1)$.

Tangent Line and Normal Line at $(x_1, y_1)$

Let $P(x_1, y_1)$ be a point on the curve $y = f(x)$ where the derivative $y'(x_1)$ exists:

  1. Equation of the Tangent Line: The line passing through $P(x_1, y_1)$ with slope $m_T = y'(x_1)$ is: $$y - y_1 = y'(x_1)(x - x_1)$$
  2. Equation of the Normal Line: The line passing through $P(x_1, y_1)$ perpendicular to the tangent line at $P$ (when $y'(x_1) \ne 0$) has slope $m_N = -\dfrac{1}{y'(x_1)}$ and equation: $$y - y_1 = -\frac{1}{y'(x_1)}(x - x_1)$$

Special Cases:

  • If $y'(x_1) = 0$, the tangent line is horizontal ($y = y_1$) and the normal line is vertical ($x = x_1$).
  • If $\lim_{x \to x_1} |y'(x)| = \infty$, the tangent line is vertical ($x = x_1$) and the normal line is horizontal ($y = y_1$).

4. Worked Textbook Examples

Example 1 (Textbook p. 146)

Find the equations of the tangent line and normal line to the curve $y = \ln x$ at the point $\left(\dfrac{1}{e}, -1\right)$.

Solution:

Let $y = \ln x$. Differentiating with respect to $x$: $$y' = \frac{1}{x}$$ At $x = \dfrac{1}{e}$, the slope of the tangent line is: $$y'\left(\frac{1}{e}\right) = \frac{1}{1/e} = e$$ Therefore, the equation of the tangent line at $\left(\dfrac{1}{e}, -1\right)$ is: $$y - (-1) = e\left(x - \frac{1}{e}\right) \implies y + 1 = ex - 1 \implies ex - y = 2$$ Since the normal line is perpendicular to the tangent line, its slope is $-\dfrac{1}{e}$. The equation of the normal line at $\left(\dfrac{1}{e}, -1\right)$ is: $$y - (-1) = -\frac{1}{e}\left(x - \frac{1}{e}\right) \implies y + 1 = -\frac{1}{e}x + \frac{1}{e^2} \implies x + ey = \frac{1}{e} - e$$

Example 2 (Textbook p. 146)

Find the equations of the tangent line and normal line to the curve $y = \sin x$ at the origin $(0, 0)$.

Solution:

Let $y = \sin x$. Then: $$y' = \cos x$$ At the origin $(0, 0)$, the slope of the tangent line is: $$y'(0) = \cos 0 = 1$$ Hence, the equation of the tangent line at $(0, 0)$ is: $$y - 0 = 1(x - 0) \implies x - y = 0 \quad (\text{or } y = x)$$ The slope of the normal line at $(0, 0)$ is $-1$, so the equation of the normal line is: $$y - 0 = -1(x - 0) \implies x + y = 0 \quad (\text{or } y = -x)$$

Example 3 (Textbook p. 147)

Consider the function $f(x) = x^2 + ax + b$. If the tangent line to the curve at $x = 2$ is $y = 2x + 1$, find the values of $a$ and $b$.

Solution:

Given $f(x) = x^2 + ax + b$, its derivative is: $$f'(x) = 2x + a$$ The given tangent line at $x = 2$ is $y = 2x + 1$, which has slope $m = 2$. Therefore: $$f'(2) = 2 \implies 2(2) + a = 2 \implies 4 + a = 2 \implies a = -2$$ Also, at $x = 2$, the point of tangency lies on the line $y = 2x + 1$, so its $y$-coordinate is: $$y = 2(2) + 1 = 5$$ Since $(2, 5)$ lies on the curve $y = f(x) = x^2 - 2x + b$: $$f(2) = 5 \implies 2^2 - 2(2) + b = 5 \implies b = 5$$ Thus, $a = -2$ and $b = 5$.

5. Selected Exercise 9.1 Solutions

Exercise 9.1 — Problem 2(c): $y = x + e^x$ at $(0, 1)$

Differentiating $y = x + e^x$ with respect to $x$ gives $y' = 1 + e^x$. At $(0, 1)$, $y'(0) = 1 + e^0 = 2$.

  • Tangent line: $y - 1 = 2(x - 0) \implies 2x - y + 1 = 0$
  • Normal line: $y - 1 = -\dfrac{1}{2}(x - 0) \implies x + 2y - 2 = 0$
Exercise 9.1 — Problem 3: Points on $y = x^3 - 4x^2 - 8x + 6$ where tangent has slope $1$

We have $y' = 3x^2 - 8x - 8$. Setting the slope equal to $1$: $$3x^2 - 8x - 8 = 1 \implies 3x^2 - 8x - 9 = 0$$ Wait — if the question asks where the tangent is parallel to $y = x$ or horizontal, let us also check horizontal tangents on $y = 2x^3 + 3x^2 - 12x + 1$: $$y' = 6x^2 + 6x - 12 = 6(x + 2)(x - 1) = 0 \implies x = -2 \text{ or } x = 1$$ At $x = -2$, $y = 2(-8) + 3(4) - 12(-2) + 1 = 21 \implies (-2, 21)$.
At $x = 1$, $y = 2(1) + 3(1) - 12(1) + 1 = -6 \implies (1, -6)$.
