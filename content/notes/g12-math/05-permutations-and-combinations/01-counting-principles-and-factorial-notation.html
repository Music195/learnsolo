Concept — Fundamental Counting Principles & Factorial Notation

Combinatorics (ရေတွက်ခြင်းဆိုင်ရာ သင်္ချာ) begins with two fundamental rules for counting possibilities without listing every outcome individually:

  • The Multiplication Principle (မြှောက်လဒ်နိယာမ — "AND / In Succession"): Suppose a first task can be performed in $m$ ways, and no matter how it has been performed, a second task can then be performed in $n$ ways. Then the number of ways to perform both tasks in succession is $m \times n$. (Extends to any finite sequence of tasks: $n_1 \times n_2 \times \cdots \times n_k$.)
  • The Addition Principle (ပေါင်းလဒ်နိယာမ — "OR / Disjoint Cases"): Suppose the outcomes of a counting problem split into $k$ pairwise disjoint cases (no arrangement belongs to two cases at once) with $n_1, n_2, \dots, n_k$ elements respectively. Then the total number of elements is $n_1 + n_2 + \cdots + n_k$.
Core Formulas — Factorial Notation ($n!$)
  • Definition of $n$ Factorial ($n!$): For any positive integer $n$, $n!$ is the product of the first $n$ positive integers: $$n! = n(n - 1)(n - 2)\cdots 3 \cdot 2 \cdot 1$$
  • Zero Factorial Convention: By definition, $$0! = 1$$
  • Recursive Reduction Identity: Any factorial can be factored by peeling off its leading terms: $$n! = n \cdot (n - 1)! = n(n - 1) \cdot (n - 2)!$$ For example, $6! = 6 \cdot 5 \cdot 4!$ and $\dfrac{13 \cdot 12 \cdot 11}{3 \cdot 2} = \dfrac{13 \cdot 12 \cdot 11 \cdot 10!}{3! \cdot 10!} = \dfrac{13!}{3!\,10!}$.
Justification — Why $n$ Distinct Pictures on $n$ Nails Gives $n!$ Ways

Suppose there are $n$ picture nails in a row and $n$ distinct pictures. By the Multiplication Principle:

  • The $1^{\text{st}}$ nail can hold any of the $n$ pictures ($n$ choices).
  • The $2^{\text{nd}}$ nail can hold any of the remaining $n - 1$ pictures ($n - 1$ choices).
  • Continuing down to the $n^{\text{th}}$ nail, only $1$ picture remains ($1$ choice).

Multiplying the choices for all $n$ successive tasks gives $n \times (n - 1) \times (n - 2) \times \cdots \times 2 \times 1 = n!$ arrangements.

Worked Examples — Textbook Examples 2, 4, 5, 6 & 7

Example 2 (Three-Stage Multiplication Principle): There are four blood types ($A, B, AB, O$). Blood can also be $\text{Rh}(+)$ or $\text{Rh}(-)$, and a donor can be classified as male or female. In how many different ways can a donor have his or her blood labeled?

Show step-by-step solution

There are $4$ possibilities for blood type, $2$ possibilities for the Rh factor, and $2$ possibilities for gender. By the Multiplication Principle:

$$\text{Total ways} = 4 \times 2 \times 2 = 16$$

Answer: $16$ ways.

Example 4 (Combining Multiplication and Addition Principles): How many different numbers can be formed using the digits $2, 4, 6, 8,$ and $9$ such that the numbers contain two or three digits without any repetition?

Show step-by-step solution

Split the problem into two disjoint cases:

  • Case 1 (Two-digit numbers): $5 \times 4 = 20$ ways.
  • Case 2 (Three-digit numbers): $5 \times 4 \times 3 = 60$ ways.

By the Addition Principle, total numbers $= 20 + 60 = 80$.

Answer: $80$ different numbers.

Example 5 (Careful Case Breakdown with Digit Restrictions): How many integers between $0$ and $100$ have the digit $5$ only once?

Show step-by-step solution

Partition into three pairwise disjoint cases:

  • Case 1 (One-digit integer): Only the integer $5$ itself $\implies 1$ integer.
  • Case 2 (Two-digit integer with $5$ in the tens place): Tens digit is $5$ ($1$ choice); ones digit can be any digit except $5$ ($0,1,2,3,4,6,7,8,9 \implies 9$ choices) $\implies 1 \times 9 = 9$ integers.
  • Case 3 (Two-digit integer with $5$ in the ones place): Tens digit can be any digit except $0$ and $5$ ($1,2,3,4,6,7,8,9 \implies 8$ choices); ones digit is $5$ ($1$ choice) $\implies 8 \times 1 = 8$ integers.

By the Addition Principle, total integers $= 1 + 9 + 8 = 18$.

Answer: $18$ integers.

Example 6 & 7 (Manipulating Factorials):
(a) Evaluate $\dfrac{10!}{8!\,3!}$.   (b) Evaluate $\dfrac{6! + 5! - 4!}{4!}$.   (c) Express $\dfrac{13 \cdot 12 \cdot 11}{3 \cdot 2}$ in factorial form.

Show step-by-step solution

(a) Expand $10! = 10 \cdot 9 \cdot 8!$ and cancel $8!$:

$$\frac{10!}{8!\,3!} = \frac{10 \cdot 9 \cdot 8!}{8! \cdot (3 \cdot 2 \cdot 1)} = \frac{90}{6} = 15$$

(b) Factor out the smallest factorial $4!$ from the numerator:

$$\frac{6! + 5! - 4!}{4!} = \frac{6 \cdot 5 \cdot 4! + 5 \cdot 4! - 4!}{4!} = \frac{(30 + 5 - 1) \cdot 4!}{4!} = 34$$

(c) Multiply numerator and denominator by $10! \cdot 1$ to complete both factorials:

$$\frac{13 \cdot 12 \cdot 11}{3 \cdot 2} = \frac{13 \cdot 12 \cdot 11 \cdot 10!}{(3 \cdot 2 \cdot 1) \cdot 10!} = \frac{13!}{3!\,10!}$$

Answer: (a) $15$, (b) $34$, (c) $\dfrac{13!}{3!\,10!}$.

Common Mistakes to Avoid
  • Forbidden Cancelation Inside Factorials: Never distribute or cancel across addition before factoring out the common factorial! For example, $\frac{6! + 5!}{4!} \neq 6! + 5$; always factor out $4!$ first: $\frac{4!(6\cdot 5 + 5)}{4!} = 35$.
  • Leading Zero in Multi-Digit Numbers: A two-digit or three-digit integer cannot begin with $0$ in its highest place value (unlike a keypad PIN code, which can start with $0$).
Exam Tip — When to Multiply vs. When to Add

Ask yourself whether a single complete arrangement requires performing both steps in sequence ("Step 1 AND then Step 2" $\implies$ Multiply) or choosing between mutually exclusive alternatives ("Case 1 OR Case 2" $\implies$ Add).

Key Takeaways (မှတ်သားရန် အချက်များ)
  • Multiplication Principle (မြှောက်လဒ်နိယာမ): Successive tasks with $m$ and $n$ choices can be performed in $m \times n$ ways.
  • Addition Principle (ပေါင်းလဒ်နိယာမ): Pairwise disjoint cases with $n_1, n_2, \dots, n_k$ ways give $n_1 + n_2 + \cdots + n_k$ total ways.
  • Factorial Identity (ဖက်တိုရီယယ်): $n! = n(n-1)\cdots 2\cdot 1 = n(n-1)!$, with $0! = 1$.
