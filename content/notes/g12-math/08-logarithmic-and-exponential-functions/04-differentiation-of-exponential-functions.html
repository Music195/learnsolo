Differentiation of Exponential Functions

Concept — The Natural Exponential Function $y = e^x$ Is Its Own Derivative

Among all exponential functions $y = b^x$ ($b > 0, b \neq 1$), the natural exponential function $y = e^x$ (with base $e \approx 2.71828$) possesses the unique property in all of mathematics that its rate of change at every point equals its value at that point:

$$\frac{d}{dx}(e^x) = e^x$$

For a general base $b > 0$, the derivative of $y = b^x$ is proportional to $b^x$ with proportionality constant $\ln b$: $\dfrac{d}{dx}(b^x) = b^x\ln b$.

Core Formulas — Exponential Derivatives & Variable-Base-and-Exponent Rule
  • Natural Exponential Function ($b = e$): For any differentiable function $u = u(x)$: $$\frac{d}{dx}(e^x) = e^x, \qquad \frac{d}{dx}\big(e^{u(x)}\big) = e^{u(x)} \cdot \frac{du}{dx}$$
  • General Base-$b$ Exponential Function ($b > 0, b \neq 1$): $$\frac{d}{dx}(b^x) = b^x\ln b, \qquad \frac{d}{dx}\big(b^{u(x)}\big) = b^{u(x)}\ln b \cdot \frac{du}{dx}$$
  • Variable in BOTH Base and Exponent ($y = [f(x)]^{g(x)}$ — Example 10):
    When $x$ appears in both the base and the exponent (e.g., $y = x^{\sin x}$ or $y = x^{\ln x}$), neither the power rule $nx^{n-1}$ nor the exponential rule $b^x\ln b$ applies directly! Always use logarithmic differentiation: $$\ln y = g(x)\ln f(x) \implies \frac{1}{y}\frac{dy}{dx} = g'(x)\ln f(x) + g(x)\frac{f'(x)}{f(x)}$$
Proof — Implicit Derivation of $\dfrac{d}{dx}(b^x) = b^x\ln b$

Let $y = b^x$ ($b > 0, b \neq 1$). Taking the base-$b$ logarithm of both sides gives:

$$x = \log_b y$$

Differentiating both sides with respect to $x$ (using $\dfrac{d}{dy}(\log_b y) = \dfrac{1}{y}\log_b e$ and the chain rule):

$$1 = \frac{1}{y}\log_b e \cdot \frac{dy}{dx}$$

Solving for $\dfrac{dy}{dx}$ and using the reciprocal base-change identity $\dfrac{1}{\log_b e} = \log_e b = \ln b$:

$$\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{y}{\log_b e} = y\log_e b = b^x\ln b \quad \blacksquare$$

In the special case $b = e$, since $\ln e = 1$, this simplifies immediately to $\dfrac{d}{dx}(e^x) = e^x$.

Interactive Demo — Why $y = e^x$ Matches Its Own Tangent Slope Everywhere

Adjust base $b$ and point $x_0$. Compare $f(x) = b^x$ (solid blue) with its derivative curve $f'(x) = b^x\ln b$ (dashed teal). When $b = e \approx 2.72$, $\ln b = 1$ and the two curves merge into one, with tangent slope $e^{x_0}$ equal to the height $e^{x_0}$!

Worked Examples — Textbook Examples 8, 9 & 10

Example 8 (Differentiating Natural Exponential Functions): Differentiate with respect to $x$:
(a) $e^{\sin x}$   (b) $x^2 e^{3x}$   (c) $e^{2x}\sin 3x$   (d) $\dfrac{e^{2x}}{1 - 2x}$.

Show step-by-step solution

(a) $\dfrac{d}{dx}(e^{\sin x}) = e^{\sin x} \cdot \dfrac{d}{dx}(\sin x) = \cos x\,e^{\sin x}$.

(b) By the product rule: $\dfrac{d}{dx}(x^2 e^{3x}) = x^2(3e^{3x}) + e^{3x}(2x) = (3x^2 + 2x)e^{3x}$.

(c) By the product rule: $\dfrac{d}{dx}(e^{2x}\sin 3x) = e^{2x}(3\cos 3x) + \sin 3x(2e^{2x}) = e^{2x}(3\cos 3x + 2\sin 3x)$.

(d) By the quotient rule:

$$\frac{d}{dx}\left(\frac{e^{2x}}{1 - 2x}\right) = \frac{(1 - 2x)(2e^{2x}) - e^{2x}(-2)}{(1 - 2x)^2} = \frac{(2 - 4x + 2)e^{2x}}{(1 - 2x)^2} = \frac{(4 - 4x)e^{2x}}{(1 - 2x)^2}$$

Answer: (a) $\cos x\,e^{\sin x}$, (b) $(3x^2 + 2x)e^{3x}$, (c) $e^{2x}(3\cos 3x + 2\sin 3x)$, (d) $\dfrac{(4 - 4x)e^{2x}}{(1 - 2x)^2}$.

Example 9 (Base-$b$ Exponentials & Implicit Differentiation): Find $\dfrac{dy}{dx}$ for:
(a) $y = 3^{x^2 - x}$   (b) $y = \log_{10}(2x + 1)$   (c) $y = 2^{\sin x + \cos x}$   (d) $xe^y + \ln(xy) = \sin x$.

Show step-by-step solution

(a) $\dfrac{dy}{dx} = 3^{x^2 - x}\ln 3 \cdot \dfrac{d}{dx}(x^2 - x) = (2x - 1)3^{x^2 - x}\ln 3$.

(b) $\dfrac{dy}{dx} = \dfrac{1}{2x + 1}\log_{10} e \cdot \dfrac{d}{dx}(2x + 1) = \dfrac{2}{2x + 1}\log_{10} e$.

(c) $\dfrac{dy}{dx} = 2^{\sin x + \cos x}\ln 2 \cdot (\cos x - \sin x)$.

(d) First expand $\ln(xy) = \ln x + \ln y$ so that $xe^y + \ln x + \ln y = \sin x$. Differentiating implicitly with respect to $x$:

$$\left(x e^y\frac{dy}{dx} + e^y(1)\right) + \frac{1}{x} + \frac{1}{y}\frac{dy}{dx} = \cos x$$ $$\left(xe^y + \frac{1}{y}\right)\frac{dy}{dx} = \cos x - e^y - \frac{1}{x} \implies \frac{xye^y + 1}{y}\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{x\cos x - xe^y - 1}{x}$$ $$\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{y(x\cos x - xe^y - 1)}{x(xye^y + 1)}$$

Answer: (a) $(2x-1)3^{x^2-x}\ln 3$, (b) $\dfrac{2\log_{10}e}{2x+1}$, (c) $(\cos x - \sin x)2^{\sin x + \cos x}\ln 2$, (d) $\dfrac{y(x\cos x - xe^y - 1)}{x(xye^y + 1)}$.

Example 10 (Differentiating $y = x^{\sin x}$ via Logarithmic Differentiation): Differentiate $y = x^{\sin x}$ ($x > 0$) with respect to $x$.

Show step-by-step solution

Take the natural logarithm of both sides to bring the variable exponent $\sin x$ down:

$$\ln y = \ln(x^{\sin x}) = \sin x \cdot \ln x$$

Differentiate both sides with respect to $x$ using the product rule on the right:

$$\frac{1}{y}\frac{dy}{dx} = \sin x \cdot \left(\frac{1}{x}\right) + \ln x \cdot (\cos x)$$

Multiply by $y = x^{\sin x}$:

$$\frac{dy}{dx} = x^{\sin x}\left(\frac{\sin x}{x} + \cos x\ln x\right) = x^{\sin x - 1}\sin x + x^{\sin x}\cos x\ln x$$

Answer: $\dfrac{dy}{dx} = x^{\sin x - 1}\sin x + x^{\sin x}\cos x\ln x$.

Common Mistakes to Avoid
  • Never Apply the Power Rule to $b^x$ or $x^x$: The derivative of $3^x$ is $3^x\ln 3$, NOT $x \cdot 3^{x - 1}$! And the derivative of $x^{\sin x}$ is NOT $(\sin x)x^{\sin x - 1}$ alone (which misses the second term $x^{\sin x}\cos x\ln x$).
  • Remembering $\ln b$ vs. $\log_b e$: When differentiating $b^x$, multiply by $\ln b$; when differentiating $\log_b x$, multiply $\frac{1}{x}$ by $\log_b e = \frac{1}{\ln b}$.
Exam Tip — Second-Order Differential Equations with $e^{ax}\cos bx$ (Exercise 8.4 #2)

In problems like "Given $y = e^x\cos x$, prove that $\frac{d^2y}{dx^2} - 2\frac{dy}{dx} + 2y = 0$", substitute $y = e^x\cos x$ right back into $\frac{dy}{dx} = e^x\cos x - e^x\sin x = y - e^x\sin x$ before differentiating a second time! Then $\frac{d^2y}{dx^2} = \frac{dy}{dx} - (e^x\cos x + e^x\sin x) = \frac{dy}{dx} - y - \left(y - \frac{dy}{dx}\right) = 2\frac{dy}{dx} - 2y$, completing the proof in two lines!

Key Takeaways (မှတ်သားရန် အချက်များ)
  • Natural Exponential Derivative: $\dfrac{d}{dx}(e^u) = e^u\dfrac{du}{dx}$.
  • General Base-$b$ Exponential Derivative: $\dfrac{d}{dx}(b^u) = b^u\ln b\,\dfrac{du}{dx}$.
  • Variable Base & Exponent ($y = u^v$): Take $\ln y = v\ln u$ first and differentiate implicitly.
