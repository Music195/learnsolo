Proofs of Divisibility

Exposing the Induction Hypothesis by Splitting the Base

Mathematical induction is a powerful tool for proving that an exponential expression like $3^n - 1$ is always a multiple of $2$ (or more generally, $a^n - 1$ is divisible by $d = a - 1$) for every natural number $n \in \mathbb{N}$.

In Step 2, we assume $a^k - 1$ is a multiple of $d$. To prove that $a^{k+1} - 1$ is also a multiple of $d$, we split the multiplier $a$ into $(d + 1)$: $$a^{k+1} - 1 = a \cdot a^k - 1 = (d + 1)a^k - 1 = \underbrace{d \cdot a^k}_{\text{multiple of } d} + \underbrace{(a^k - 1)}_{\text{multiple of } d \text{ by } P(k)}$$ Since the sum of two multiples of $d$ is always a multiple of $d$, the inductive step is complete!

The Algebraic Splitting Identity for Divisibility

$$a^{k+1} - 1 = (a - 1)a^k + (a^k - 1)$$ $$a^{k+1} - b^{k+1} = a(a^k - b^k) + b^k(a - b)$$

• Initial Step for $n \ge m$: If a statement holds only for $n \ge m$ (where $m \in \mathbb{N}$), prove the base case at $n = m$ instead of $n = 1$.
• Divisibility Rule: If $A = d \cdot q_1$ and $B = d \cdot q_2$ ($q_1, q_2 \in \mathbb{Z}$), then $A + B = d(q_1 + q_2)$ is a multiple of $d$.

  1. Base case ($n = 1$): $3^1 - 1 = 2 = 2 \times 1$, which is a multiple of $2$.
  2. Assume $3^k - 1 = 2m$ for some integer $m$.
  3. For $n = k + 1$: $3^{k+1} - 1 = 3 \cdot 3^k - 1 = (2 + 1)3^k - 1 = 2 \cdot 3^k + (3^k - 1) = 2 \cdot 3^k + 2m = 2(3^k + m)$, which is a multiple of $2$.

Example 1 (Textbook Page 17, Example 3)

Use the mathematical induction principle to prove that $3^n - 1$ is a multiple of $2$ for all natural numbers $n$.

Show solution
  1. Let $P(n)$ denote the statement "$3^n - 1$ is a multiple of $2$".
  2. (1) For $n = 1$: $3^1 - 1 = 2$, which is a multiple of $2$. Thus $P(1)$ is true.
  3. (2) Assume $P(k)$ is true for $n = k$: $3^k - 1$ is a multiple of $2$.
    For $n = k + 1$, rewrite $3^{k+1} - 1$: $$3^{k+1} - 1 = 3 \cdot 3^k - 1 = 2 \cdot 3^k + (3^k - 1)$$ Since $2 \cdot 3^k$ is a multiple of $2$ and $3^k - 1$ is a multiple of $2$ by hypothesis, their sum $3^{k+1} - 1$ is also a multiple of $2$. Thus $P(k + 1)$ is true.
  4. (3) Conclusion: Hence, by the principle of mathematical induction, $P(n)$ is true for all natural numbers $n$.

Proved true for all $n \in \mathbb{N}$

Example 2 (Divisibility of $5^n - 1$ by $4$)

Prove by mathematical induction that $5^n - 1$ is divisible by $4$ for all natural numbers $n$.

Show solution
  1. (1) For $n = 1$: $5^1 - 1 = 4$, which is divisible by $4$.
  2. (2) Assume true for $n = k$: $5^k - 1 = 4m$ for some integer $m$.
    For $n = k + 1$: $$5^{k+1} - 1 = 5 \cdot 5^k - 1 = (4 + 1)5^k - 1 = 4 \cdot 5^k + (5^k - 1) = 4 \cdot 5^k + 4m = 4(5^k + m)$$ Since $5^k + m$ is an integer, $5^{k+1} - 1$ is divisible by $4$.
  3. (3) Conclusion: By mathematical induction, $5^n - 1$ is divisible by $4$ for all $n \in \mathbb{N}$.

Proved true for all $n \in \mathbb{N}$

State Why Both Parts Are Divisible: In your exam proof, always explicitly write: "Since $2 \cdot 3^k$ is a multiple of $2$ and $3^k - 1$ is a multiple of $2$ by the induction hypothesis, their sum is also a multiple of $2$."
Alternative Substitution Method: If $3^k - 1 = 2m$, you can isolate $3^k = 2m + 1$ and substitute directly into $3^{k+1} - 1$: $$3(2m + 1) - 1 = 6m + 2 = 2(3m + 1)$$ This always works for any divisibility induction problem!
