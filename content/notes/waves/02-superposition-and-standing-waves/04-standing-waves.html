Standing Waves

Two identical waves moving in opposite directions make a wave that doesn't move

Take two waves with the same amplitude, wavelength and speed, travelling in opposite directions (for example a wave and its reflection). Add them. The result does not travel at all: it just swells and shrinks in place. This is a standing wave (or stationary wave). A wave that does move along is a travelling wave.

  • Nodes: points that never move. The two waves always cancel there.
  • Antinodes: points that vibrate the most, with amplitude $2A$ (twice the amplitude of each wave).
  • Everything between two neighbouring nodes moves in phase (all up together); across a node the motion is reversed.

Spacing (remember this pattern): node to node = $\lambda/2$, antinode to antinode = $\lambda/2$, node to the next antinode = $\lambda/4$. Here $\lambda$ is the wavelength of the two original travelling waves.

Adding $y_1 = A\sin 2\pi\left(\frac tT - \frac x\lambda\right)$ and $y_2 = A\sin 2\pi\left(\frac tT + \frac x\lambda\right)$

$$y = 2A\cos\frac{2\pi x}{\lambda}\,\sin\frac{2\pi t}{T}$$

The amplitude at position $x$ is $\left|2A\cos\dfrac{2\pi x}{\lambda}\right|$: $2A$ at antinodes ($x = 0, \lambda/2, \dots$), 0 at nodes ($x = \lambda/4, 3\lambda/4, \dots$). There is no $x - vt$ inside, so nothing travels.

  1. Use $\sin P + \sin Q = 2\sin\dfrac{P+Q}{2}\cos\dfrac{P-Q}{2}$ with $P = 2\pi(t/T - x/\lambda)$, $Q = 2\pi(t/T + x/\lambda)$.
  2. $\dfrac{P+Q}{2} = \dfrac{2\pi t}{T}$ and $\dfrac{P-Q}{2} = -\dfrac{2\pi x}{\lambda}$; cosine is even.
  3. So $y = 2A\cos\dfrac{2\pi x}{\lambda}\sin\dfrac{2\pi t}{T}$: each point does SHM, with an amplitude that depends on where it is.
  4. Nodes: $\cos\dfrac{2\pi x}{\lambda} = 0$, i.e. $x = \dfrac\lambda4, \dfrac{3\lambda}4, \dots$, which are $\lambda/2$ apart.

Standing waves (λ = 4 cm)

Example 1

A standing wave is made by two waves of wavelength 0.60 m. Find the distance between neighbouring nodes and between a node and the nearest antinode.

Show solution
  1. Node to node: $\lambda/2 = 0.30$ m.
  2. Node to antinode: $\lambda/4 = 0.15$ m.

0.30 m and 0.15 m

Example 2

In a standing wave, the distance from a node to the nearest antinode is 5.0 cm, and each travelling wave has amplitude 3.0 cm. Find the wavelength and the amplitude at an antinode.

Show solution
  1. $\lambda/4 = 5.0$ cm, so $\lambda = 20$ cm.
  2. At an antinode the amplitudes add: $2 \times 3.0$.

$\lambda = 20$ cm; antinode amplitude 6.0 cm

Example 3

Two sources A and B, 10 cm apart, send out waves of wavelength 4.0 cm and amplitude $A$, in antiphase (when one pushes up, the other pushes down). Is the midpoint of AB a node or an antinode? How many antinodes are there strictly between A and B?

Show solution
  1. The midpoint is the same distance from both sources, so the two waves arrive with the same delay. They started in antiphase, so they are still in antiphase: they cancel. The midpoint is a node.
  2. Antinodes are $\lambda/4 = 1$ cm from a node, then every $\lambda/2 = 2$ cm: at 4 cm and 6 cm from A, then 2 cm and 8 cm, then 0 and 10 cm (the sources themselves).
  3. Strictly between A and B: 2, 4, 6, 8 cm.

The midpoint is a node; 4 antinodes between A and B

Example 4

With the same set-up, what happens on the line outside AB (beyond B, say)?

Show solution
  1. Beyond B both waves travel in the same direction, so no standing wave forms.
  2. The wave from A has travelled 10 cm more than the wave from B: $10/4 = 2.5$ wavelengths, i.e. half a turn extra on top of whole turns.
  3. That half turn and the antiphase start cancel each other out, so the two waves are in phase and add.

A travelling wave with amplitude $2A$, moving away from the sources

Example 5

With the same set-up, find the amplitude at a point 0.5 cm from A (between A and B).

Show solution
  1. The nearest node is the one at $x = 1$ cm (nodes at 1, 3, 5, 7, 9 cm), and $x = 0$ is an antinode.
  2. The amplitude goes like $2A\left|\sin\dfrac{2\pi d}{\lambda}\right|$ where $d$ is the distance from a node: $d = 0.5$ cm, $\dfrac{2\pi \times 0.5}{4} = \dfrac\pi4$.
  3. $2A\sin\dfrac\pi4 = 2A \times \dfrac{\sqrt2}{2}$.

$\sqrt2\,A \approx 1.4A$

The $\lambda$ in "node to node = $\lambda/2$" is the wavelength of the travelling waves that make the standing wave. The standing wave's "loops" are each only half a wavelength long.
Draw a standing wave as a row of loops (the shape at its largest, plus its upside-down copy). Each loop is $\lambda/2$ long; nodes at the pinches, antinodes at the fattest points.
  • Two equal waves moving in opposite directions → standing wave.
  • Nodes don't move; antinodes have amplitude $2A$.
  • Node–node $= \lambda/2$, antinode–antinode $= \lambda/2$, node–antinode $= \lambda/4$.
