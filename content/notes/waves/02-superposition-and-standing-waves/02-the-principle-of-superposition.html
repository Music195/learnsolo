The Principle of Superposition

Where two waves meet, their displacements simply add

Two balls that collide bounce off each other. Two waves don't: they pass straight through each other and come out unchanged on the other side. This is called the independence of waves.

While they overlap, the medium has to do both vibrations at once. The result is easy: at each point, the displacement is the sum of the two displacements (with signs). This is the principle of superposition, and the combined wave is the resultant wave.

  • A pulse of $+3$ cm meets a pulse of $+1$ cm: where the peaks overlap, $y = +4$ cm.
  • A pulse of $+3$ cm meets a pulse of $-1$ cm: $y = +3 + (-1) = +2$ cm. It is a sum, not an average and not a subtraction of sizes.

Superposition

$$y = y_1 + y_2$$

$y_1$, $y_2$: the displacements each wave would give on its own, at the same point and the same instant, with their signs. $y$: the actual displacement.

  1. Draw each wave as if the other were not there, at the same instant.
  2. At a few points (where one wave is zero, at peaks, at corners), add the two heights with signs.
  3. Join the summed points: that is the resultant wave.
  4. After they separate, each wave carries on with its own shape and speed, as if they had never met.

Two pulses meet: dashed = each on its own, solid = the actual rope

Example 1

At one point, wave 1 gives $y_1 = +3$ cm and wave 2 gives $y_2 = -1$ cm. Find the actual displacement.

Show solution
  1. $y = y_1 + y_2 = 3 + (-1)$.

$y = +2$ cm (not the average, 1 cm)

Example 2

Two identical pulses of height 2.0 cm, one moving right and one moving left, overlap exactly. What height does the rope reach?

Show solution
  1. $y = 2.0 + 2.0$ at the centre.

4.0 cm

Example 3

A pulse of $+2.0$ cm and an identically shaped pulse of $-2.0$ cm overlap exactly. What does the rope look like at that instant? Has the wave vanished?

Show solution
  1. $y = 2.0 + (-2.0) = 0$ everywhere: the rope is momentarily flat.
  2. But the pieces are still moving (the energy is now all kinetic), so a moment later the two pulses reappear and move apart.

Flat for an instant; the pulses have not vanished and come out unchanged

Example 4

Two pulses on a rope, each 2 cm wide and moving at 1.0 cm/s towards each other, have their centres 6 cm apart at $t = 0$. When do their centres coincide, and when have they completely separated again?

Show solution
  1. They close the gap at $1.0 + 1.0 = 2.0$ cm/s, so the centres meet after $6/2.0 = 3.0$ s.
  2. They stop touching when the centres are 2 cm apart on the other side: a total closing of $6 + 2 = 8$ cm, taking 4.0 s.

Centres coincide at 3.0 s; separated after 4.0 s

Example 5

Wave A gives $y_A = 0.30\sin\omega t$ and wave B gives $y_B = 0.30\sin\omega t$ at a point (same phase). Wave C gives $y_C = -0.30\sin\omega t$ there (opposite phase). Find the amplitude at that point for A + B and for A + C.

Show solution
  1. A + B: $0.60\sin\omega t$, amplitude 0.60.
  2. A + C: $0$ at all times.

0.60 (in phase: they strengthen) and 0 (antiphase: they cancel)

Always add with the signs. "3 cm and 1 cm on opposite sides" gives 2 cm, not 4 cm and not 1.5 cm.
Everything in the rest of this course (standing waves, beats, interference) is just superposition applied carefully.
  • Waves pass through each other unchanged.
  • While overlapping: $y = y_1 + y_2$, with signs.
  • In phase: they strengthen; antiphase: they cancel.
