Transverse and Longitudinal Waves

Compare the direction the medium vibrates with the direction the wave travels

  • Transverse wave: the medium vibrates at right angles to the direction of travel. A wave on a rope: the rope moves up and down, the wave moves along.
  • Longitudinal wave: the medium vibrates along the direction of travel. Push a long spring (a slinky) back and forth along its length: bunched-up regions (compressions) and stretched regions (rarefactions) travel along it. This is why a longitudinal wave is also called a compression wave.

Sound is a longitudinal wave: air molecules are pushed back and forth along the direction the sound travels, making regions of high and low pressure.

A longitudinal wave is hard to draw, so we draw it as if it were transverse: take each piece's displacement along the line and turn it 90° anticlockwise. A displacement in the $+x$ direction (forward) is drawn up; a displacement in the $-x$ direction is drawn down. Then all the graph tools from the last topic work again.

Reading the transverse picture of a longitudinal wave

$$\text{up} = \text{moved forward } (+x) \qquad \text{down} = \text{moved backward } (-x)$$

Compression (densest): where $y = 0$ and the graph slopes downward (pieces on the left moved forward, pieces on the right moved back, so they squeeze together). Rarefaction (sparsest): where $y = 0$ and the graph slopes upward.

  1. Look at a point where $y = 0$ with the graph going down from left to right.
  2. Just to its left, $y > 0$: those pieces have moved forward (to the right), towards the point.
  3. Just to its right, $y < 0$: those pieces have moved backward (to the left), also towards the point.
  4. Both sides move in, so the medium is squeezed together there: a compression. Where the graph goes up through $y = 0$, both sides move away: a rarefaction.
  5. The crests and troughs are pieces that moved the most, but the density there is normal.

Example 1

Classify: (a) a wave on a guitar string; (b) sound in air; (c) a pulse pushed along a slinky from one end.

Show solution
  1. (a) The string moves sideways, the wave runs along it: transverse.
  2. (b) Air moves back and forth along the direction of travel: longitudinal.
  3. (c) The coils move along the spring: longitudinal.

(a) transverse; (b) longitudinal; (c) longitudinal

Example 2

In the transverse picture of a longitudinal wave, a piece at $x = 3$ cm is drawn at $y = -0.5$ cm. Where is it really?

Show solution
  1. Down in the picture means moved in the $-x$ direction.

At $x = 2.5$ cm: moved 0.5 cm backwards from its resting place

Example 3

The transverse picture is $y = A\sin\dfrac{2\pi x}{\lambda}$ at one instant, with $\lambda = 4.0$ m, for $0 \le x \le 4$ m. Where is the medium most compressed, and where is it least dense?

Show solution
  1. $y = 0$ at $x = 0$, 2.0 and 4.0 m.
  2. At $x = 2.0$ m the graph goes down (from crest to trough): compression.
  3. At $x = 0$ and 4.0 m the graph goes up: rarefaction.

Densest at 2.0 m; least dense at 0 and 4.0 m

Example 4

For the same snapshot, the wave moves in the $+x$ direction. Which way is the piece at $x = 2.0$ m (the compression) moving?

Show solution
  1. Shift the picture slightly to the right: at $x = 2.0$ the curve arriving comes from the left, where $y > 0$.
  2. So its displacement is about to become positive: it moves forward ($+x$), with the wave. In the picture this is "up"; in reality it is "to the right".

In the $+x$ direction, at its top speed (it is at $y = 0$)

Example 5

A pupil says a crest in the transverse picture of a sound wave is "where the air is squashed most". Correct them.

Show solution
  1. A crest is a piece that has moved forward the most; its neighbours moved almost as much in the same direction, so the spacing there is normal.
  2. Squeezing happens where neighbours move towards each other: at $y = 0$ on a downward slope.

Compressions are at $y = 0$ on the downward slopes, not at the crests

The transverse picture of sound is a graph, not a shape in space. The air never moves up and down; "up" only means "moved forward".
To find compressions quickly, draw little arrows: right for every $y > 0$, left for every $y < 0$. Where the arrows point at each other: compression. Where they point away: rarefaction.
  • Transverse: vibration ⟂ travel. Longitudinal: vibration ∥ travel (sound).
  • Draw a longitudinal wave as transverse: forward = up, backward = down.
  • Compression: $y = 0$, graph going down; rarefaction: $y = 0$, graph going up (left to right).
