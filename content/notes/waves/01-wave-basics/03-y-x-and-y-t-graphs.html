y–x and y–t Graphs

A y–x graph is a photo of the whole wave; a y–t graph is a diary of one piece

Wave graphs come in two kinds that look almost the same. Mixing them up is the most common mistake in wave problems, so always read the label on the horizontal axis first.

  • y–x graph (the wave shape): time is frozen. It's like a photo of the stadium wave: every person at one instant. The horizontal axis is position, so the repeat length you read off is the wavelength $\lambda$.
  • y–t graph (the vibration of one piece): we pick one piece of the medium and plot its displacement as time passes, like a strobe photo of one person standing up and sitting down. The horizontal axis is time, so the repeat length is the period $T$.

On both graphs, the height from the rest line to a crest is the amplitude $A$.

A y–x graph doesn't show directly which way each piece is moving next. To find out, shift the whole wave a tiny bit in the direction it travels and see whether the piece ends up higher or lower.

Reading the two graphs

$$y\text{–}x:\ \text{repeat} = \lambda \qquad y\text{–}t:\ \text{repeat} = T \qquad v = \frac{\lambda}{T}$$

You need one of each (or $\lambda$ and $T$ from somewhere) to find the wave speed. The amplitude is the same on both.

  1. Each piece copies the piece just "upstream" of it (the side the wave comes from).
  2. So, a moment later, a piece will have the displacement that its upstream neighbour has now.
  3. Moving the whole shape a little in the travel direction puts the upstream neighbour's displacement at our piece. If that is higher, the piece is about to move up; if lower, down.
  4. A piece at a crest or trough is momentarily at rest (it is turning round).

Wave moving right: $A = 2.0$ cm, $\lambda = 4.0$ cm, $T = 2.0$ s

Example 1

A y–x graph shows crests 0.60 m apart, with crests at $y = 5$ cm. What can you read from it?

Show solution
  1. The repeat along $x$ is the wavelength.
  2. The crest height is the amplitude.

$\lambda = 0.60$ m, $A = 5$ cm (not the period)

Example 2

The y–t graph of the piece at $x = 0$ starts at $y = 0$, goes up to 2.0 cm, back down to −2.0 cm, and returns to 0 at $t = 2.0$ s. The wavelength is 4.0 cm. Find the wave speed.

Show solution
  1. One full repeat on a y–t graph is the period: $T = 2.0$ s.
  2. $v = \lambda/T = 4.0/2.0$.

$v = 2.0\ \mathrm{cm/s}$

Example 3

For the wave in Example 2, travelling in the $+x$ direction, sketch the y–x graph at $t = 0$ between $x = 0$ and 4.0 cm.

Show solution
  1. At $t = 0$ the piece at $x = 0$ has $y = 0$ and is about to move up (from the y–t graph).
  2. It copies its upstream neighbour (on the left, $x < 0$), so that neighbour must be above the line now. So just to the right of $x = 0$ the wave is below the line.
  3. So: $y = 0$ at $x = 0$, a trough $-2.0$ cm at $x = 1.0$ cm, $0$ at 2.0 cm, a crest $+2.0$ cm at 3.0 cm, $0$ at 4.0 cm.

Trough at 1.0 cm, crest at 3.0 cm (the "upside-down sine" shape). Drawing a crest first is the usual mistake.

Example 4

On a y–x graph of a wave moving to the right, a piece is on the rising side just before a crest (the crest is to its right). Is it moving up or down?

Show solution
  1. Shift the wave slightly to the right. The part of the curve that arrives at our piece comes from its left, which is lower.

Moving down

Example 5

A y–t graph has a repeat of 0.50 s. Someone says "the wavelength is 0.50". What's wrong?

Show solution
  1. The horizontal axis of a y–t graph is time, so its repeat is the period.

0.50 s is the period $T$ (so $f = 2.0$ Hz); a y–t graph doesn't give $\lambda$

The two graphs can look identical, but the y–t graph of a wave moving right is the mirror image of its y–x graph near the origin. Never copy one graph to answer a question about the other: work out the direction first.
Before reading anything, say out loud what the horizontal axis is: "x, so this is a photo, repeat = $\lambda$" or "t, so this is one piece, repeat = $T$".
  • y–x: all pieces at one instant; repeat = wavelength.
  • y–t: one piece over time; repeat = period.
  • To find which way a piece moves on a y–x graph, shift the wave slightly in its travel direction.
