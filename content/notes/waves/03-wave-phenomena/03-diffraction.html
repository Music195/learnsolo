Diffraction

Waves spread round obstacles and through gaps, most strongly when the gap is about one wavelength

When a wave passes through a gap or past the edge of an obstacle, it doesn't just carry straight on: it spreads into the region "behind" the obstacle, where a straight line from the source would not reach. This is diffraction.

Huygens' principle explains it. The points in the gap send out wavelets in all directions. In the middle of a wide gap the wavelets add up to a straight front, but at the edges nothing cancels the sideways part, so the front curls round into the shadow.

  • Gap much wider than $\lambda$: the wave goes mostly straight through; only a little bending at the edges.
  • Gap about $\lambda$ or narrower: the gap acts like a single point source; circular waves spread out in all forward directions.

That is why you can hear someone round a corner but not see them. Sound has wavelengths around 1 m, similar to doorways, so it diffracts strongly. Light has wavelengths around $5 \times 10^{-7}$ m, far smaller than a door, so it travels in straight lines and casts sharp shadows.

When is diffraction strong?

$$\text{gap width } w \lesssim \lambda \ \Rightarrow\ \text{strong spreading}$$

A rough guide to the spread: most of the wave lies within an angle $\theta$ of straight ahead where $\sin\theta \approx \lambda/w$. A smaller gap or a longer wavelength spreads more.

  1. Every point across the gap is a source of circular wavelets.
  2. Straight ahead, wavelets from all across the gap arrive together and add up.
  3. At a sideways angle, wavelets from different parts of the gap travel different distances. When the gap is wide, those differences cover many wavelengths and the wavelets cancel, so little goes sideways.
  4. When the gap is narrow (about $\lambda$), the differences are small at every angle, so no direction cancels: the wave spreads in all forward directions.

Example 1

Sound of frequency 340 Hz travels at 340 m/s towards a doorway 0.90 m wide. Will it diffract strongly?

Show solution
  1. $\lambda = v/f = 340/340 = 1.0$ m.
  2. The gap (0.90 m) is about one wavelength.

Yes: the sound spreads into the whole room beyond the door

Example 2

Light of wavelength $5.0 \times 10^{-7}$ m passes through the same doorway. Compare the gap with the wavelength.

Show solution
  1. $w/\lambda = 0.90/(5.0 \times 10^{-7}) = 1.8 \times 10^{6}$.

The gap is almost two million wavelengths wide: no noticeable diffraction, a sharp-edged patch of light

Example 3

Water waves with $\lambda = 2.0$ cm pass through a gap of 1.0 cm in one tank and 10 cm in another. In which tank do they spread more?

Show solution
  1. 1.0 cm is half a wavelength; 10 cm is five wavelengths.

Through the 1.0 cm gap (circular waves spread out)

Example 4

Ocean waves of wavelength 20 m enter a harbour through an opening 40 m wide. Estimate the angle to either side of straight ahead within which most of the wave spreads.

Show solution
  1. $\sin\theta \approx \lambda/w = 20/40 = 0.50$.

About 30° each side, so boats moored behind the harbour wall still feel the waves

Example 5

AM radio uses wavelengths of hundreds of metres; FM uses about 3 m. Which is received better behind a hill?

Show solution
  1. Hills are hundreds of metres across: comparable with AM wavelengths, much bigger than FM ones.

AM: its long waves diffract round the hill

Diffraction changes the direction and shape of the wavefronts, but not the speed, frequency or wavelength: it all happens in one medium.
Always compare the gap (or obstacle) with the wavelength. "Big gap" or "small gap" means nothing on its own; $w/\lambda$ is what matters.
  • Diffraction: waves bend round edges and spread through gaps.
  • Strongest when the gap is about one wavelength or less.
  • Sound diffracts noticeably in everyday life; visible light hardly does.
