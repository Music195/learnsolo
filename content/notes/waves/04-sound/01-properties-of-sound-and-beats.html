Properties of Sound and Beats

Sound is a longitudinal wave in air; two close notes together swell and fade

Sound is a longitudinal wave: a vibrating object pushes the air back and forth, making compressions and rarefactions that travel outwards. It needs a medium, so there is no sound in a vacuum. It travels faster in liquids and solids than in air.

The speed of sound in air rises with temperature:

  • $V = 331.5 + 0.6t$ m/s, where $t$ is the temperature in °C. At room temperature this is about 340 m/s.

Every sound has three properties:

  • Loudness: set by the amplitude. A bigger amplitude gives a louder sound.
  • Pitch: set by the frequency. A higher frequency gives a higher note. Humans hear roughly 20 Hz to 20 000 Hz; above that is ultrasound.
  • Quality (timbre): set by the shape of the waveform. A piano and a violin playing the same note at the same loudness still sound different.

Beats. Play two notes with slightly different frequencies together. Sometimes their compressions arrive together and add (loud); a little later one has slipped half a vibration behind and they cancel (quiet). The sound swells and fades regularly: these are beats. The number of beats per second is the difference between the two frequencies.

Speed of sound and beat frequency

$$V = 331.5 + 0.6t \qquad f_{\text{beat}} = |f_1 - f_2|$$

$V$: speed of sound in air (m/s); $t$: temperature (°C). $f_{\text{beat}}$: number of loud–quiet cycles per second; $f_1$, $f_2$: the two frequencies (Hz).

  1. Start at a loud moment: both sources are in step.
  2. The next loud moment comes when the faster source has made exactly one more vibration than the slower, so they are in step again. Call this time $T_b$.
  3. In time $T_b$ the sources make $f_1T_b$ and $f_2T_b$ vibrations: $f_1T_b - f_2T_b = 1$ (with $f_1 > f_2$).
  4. So $T_b = \dfrac{1}{f_1 - f_2}$ and the beat frequency is $f_{\text{beat}} = \dfrac{1}{T_b} = f_1 - f_2$.

Example 1

Find the speed of sound in air at 15 °C.

Show solution
  1. $V = 331.5 + 0.6 \times 15 = 331.5 + 9.0$.

$V = 340.5\ \mathrm{m/s} \approx 341$ m/s

Example 2

On a 30 °C day, find the wavelength of a 500 Hz sound in air.

Show solution
  1. $V = 331.5 + 0.6 \times 30 = 349.5$ m/s.
  2. $\lambda = V/f = 349.5/500$.

$\lambda \approx 0.70$ m

Example 3

Two tuning forks of 440 Hz and 443 Hz are struck together. How many beats are heard per second, and how long is one beat?

Show solution
  1. $f_{\text{beat}} = 443 - 440 = 3$ Hz.
  2. One beat lasts $1/3$ s.

3 beats per second; about 0.33 s each

Example 4

A guitar string sounded with a 440 Hz tuning fork gives 2 beats per second. When the string is tightened slightly (raising its frequency), the beats become 3 per second. Find the string's original frequency.

Show solution
  1. 2 beats/s means the string is 438 Hz or 442 Hz.
  2. Raising 438 Hz would move it towards 440 Hz and reduce the beats. The beats increased, so the string was already above 440 Hz.

442 Hz

Example 5

You see lightning and hear the thunder 3.0 s later. The air is at 15 °C. How far away was the lightning?

Show solution
  1. Light arrives almost instantly, so the delay is the sound's travel time.
  2. $d = Vt = 340.5 \times 3.0 \approx 1.0 \times 10^{3}$ m.

About 1.0 km

Beats are only heard when the two frequencies are close (a difference of a few hertz). Far-apart frequencies just sound like two separate notes.
Musicians tune by ear using beats: adjust until the beats slow down and disappear, and the frequencies match.
  • Sound: longitudinal, needs a medium; $V = 331.5 + 0.6t$ in air.
  • Loudness ↔ amplitude; pitch ↔ frequency; quality ↔ waveform.
  • Beats: $f_{\text{beat}} = |f_1 - f_2|$.
