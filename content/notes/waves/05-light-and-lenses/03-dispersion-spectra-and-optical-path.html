Dispersion, Spectra and Optical Path

Glass bends violet more than red, so a prism splits white light into colours

The refractive index of glass or water is slightly different for each colour: it is larger for shorter wavelengths. So violet light slows down more and bends more than red light. When white light passes through a prism, each colour comes out at a different angle and the light spreads into a band of colours, the spectrum. This splitting is dispersion.

A rainbow is dispersion in raindrops. Sunlight enters each drop, reflects once off the back and comes out again, with red leaving at about 42° to the direction of the sunlight and violet at about 40°. So with the Sun behind you, red is on the outside of the bow and violet on the inside.

Spectra come in two main kinds:

  • Continuous spectrum: all colours blending smoothly, from hot solids and liquids (a filament lamp, the Sun's surface).
  • Line spectrum: only a few sharp, bright colours, from hot thin gases. Each element has its own set of lines (sodium gives a strong yellow line at 589 nm), like a fingerprint.

Optical path. In a material, light is slower and its wavelength is $\lambda/n$, so a length $L$ of glass holds as many wavelengths as a length $nL$ of air. We call $nL$ the optical path length: the distance light would travel in a vacuum in the same time. It is the tool for comparing paths through different materials in interference problems.

Dispersion and optical path

$$n_{\text{violet}} > n_{\text{red}} \qquad \text{optical path} = nL$$

$n$: refractive index (depends slightly on colour). $L$: real distance travelled in the material. Number of wavelengths along the path: $\dfrac{L}{\lambda/n} = \dfrac{nL}{\lambda}$, where $\lambda$ is the vacuum wavelength.

  1. In the material, light travels at $c/n$, so crossing a length $L$ takes time $t = \dfrac{L}{c/n} = \dfrac{nL}{c}$.
  2. In that time, light in a vacuum would go $ct = nL$.
  3. The wavelength in the material is $\lambda/n$, so the number of wavelengths in $L$ is $\dfrac{nL}{\lambda}$: the same as in a vacuum length $nL$.

Light through a 60° prism (n from 1.50 for red to 1.60 for violet: exaggerated)

Example 1

For a glass, $n = 1.51$ for red light and $n = 1.53$ for violet light. Which colour bends more on entering the glass, and which travels faster in it?

Show solution
  1. Larger $n$: more bending and lower speed $c/n$.

Violet bends more; red travels faster

Example 2

White light enters glass at an angle of incidence of 30°. $n_{\text{red}} = 1.50$, $n_{\text{violet}} = 1.53$. Find the angles of refraction for each colour.

Show solution
  1. Red: $\sin\theta = 0.50/1.50 = 0.333$, $\theta \approx 19.5°$.
  2. Violet: $\sin\theta = 0.50/1.53 = 0.327$, $\theta \approx 19.1°$.

19.5° (red) and 19.1° (violet): about 0.4° apart

Example 3

Find the optical path length of 2.0 cm of glass with $n = 1.5$.

Show solution
  1. $nL = 1.5 \times 2.0$.

3.0 cm

Example 4

Light crosses 12 cm of water ($n = 4/3$) and then 6.0 cm of glass ($n = 1.5$). Find the total optical path.

Show solution
  1. Water: $\dfrac43 \times 12 = 16$ cm. Glass: $1.5 \times 6.0 = 9.0$ cm.

25 cm (the real distance is 18 cm)

Example 5

Light of vacuum wavelength 600 nm crosses a glass sheet 3.0 μm thick ($n = 1.5$). How many wavelengths fit in the sheet? How many would fit in the same thickness of air?

Show solution
  1. Optical path $= 1.5 \times 3.0 = 4.5$ μm; $4.5\ \mu\mathrm{m}/0.60\ \mu\mathrm{m} = 7.5$.
  2. In air: $3.0/0.60 = 5.0$.

7.5 wavelengths in glass, 5.0 in air

It is violet (short wavelength) that bends most in a prism. This is the opposite of diffraction, where long wavelengths spread more.
In interference problems with materials, always compare optical paths $nL$, not real lengths.
  • Dispersion: $n$ depends on colour (bigger for violet), so prisms and raindrops split white light.
  • Hot solids: continuous spectrum. Hot gases: line spectrum.
  • Optical path $= nL$: the vacuum distance with the same number of wavelengths.
