Spherical Mirrors

A concave mirror behaves like a convex lens; a convex mirror like a concave lens

A spherical mirror is a piece cut from a shiny sphere of radius $R$. The centre of that sphere is the centre of curvature C.

  • Concave mirror (shiny on the inside, like a spoon's bowl): reflects parallel light so that it meets at the focal point F in front of the mirror. It is converging, like a convex lens. Used in torches (bulb at F gives a parallel beam), make-up mirrors and telescopes.
  • Convex mirror (shiny on the outside, like the back of a spoon): spreads parallel light as if it came from F behind the mirror. It is diverging, like a concave lens. It always gives a smaller upright image of a wide view, so it's used for car wing mirrors and road-corner mirrors.

For mirrors that are not too curved, the focal length is half the radius: $f = R/2$, so F is halfway between the mirror and C.

The ray rules are like those for lenses, except that light comes back:

  • parallel to the axis → reflects through F (concave), or away from F behind the mirror (convex);
  • through F (or heading towards F behind a convex mirror) → reflects parallel;
  • hitting the centre of the mirror → reflects at the same angle on the other side of the axis.

The same formulas as for lenses work, with $b$ measured in front of the mirror for a real image.

Mirror formulas

$$f = \frac R2 \qquad \frac1a + \frac1b = \frac1f \qquad m = \frac ba$$

Concave, real image (in front): as written. Concave, virtual image (behind, when $a < f$): $\frac1a - \frac1b = \frac1f$. Convex mirror (always virtual, behind): $\frac1a - \frac1b = -\frac1f$.

  1. A ray parallel to the axis hits the mirror at P. The radius CP is the normal there, so the ray reflects with equal angles $\theta$ either side of CP.
  2. The parallel ray and the axis make alternate angles, so the angle PCF is also $\theta$. With the reflected ray making angle $\theta$ with CP, triangle CFP is isosceles: CF = FP.
  3. For a ray close to the axis, FP is almost the same as F to the mirror. So CF ≈ (distance from F to mirror), and F is halfway: $f = R/2$.

Example 1

A concave mirror has radius of curvature 20 cm. Find its focal length.

Show solution
  1. $f = R/2 = 20/2$.

$f = 10$ cm

Example 2

An object is 30 cm in front of a concave mirror with $f = 10$ cm. Find the image.

Show solution
  1. $\dfrac1b = \dfrac1{10} - \dfrac1{30} = \dfrac2{30}$, so $b = 15$ cm (real, in front).
  2. $m = 15/30 = 0.50$.

15 cm in front of the mirror; real, inverted, half size

Example 3

Your face is 5.0 cm from a concave make-up mirror with $f = 10$ cm. Describe your image.

Show solution
  1. $a < f$: virtual. $\dfrac15 - \dfrac1b = \dfrac1{10}$, so $b = 10$ cm behind the mirror.
  2. $m = 10/5.0 = 2$.

Upright, virtual, twice the size, 10 cm behind the mirror

Example 4

An object is 10 cm in front of a convex mirror with focal length 10 cm. Find the image.

Show solution
  1. $\dfrac1{10} - \dfrac1b = -\dfrac1{10}$, so $\dfrac1b = \dfrac2{10}$ and $b = 5.0$ cm.
  2. $m = 5.0/10 = 0.50$.

5.0 cm behind the mirror; virtual, upright, half size

Example 5

Where should the bulb of a torch be placed in front of its concave mirror ($R = 6.0$ cm) to make a parallel beam?

Show solution
  1. Light from F reflects parallel. $f = R/2 = 3.0$ cm.

At the focal point, 3.0 cm from the mirror

Don't mix up the pairs: a concave mirror converges (like a convex lens), and a convex mirror diverges (like a concave lens).
For a mirror, real images form in front (where the light actually goes back to), and virtual images appear behind the mirror.
  • $f = R/2$.
  • Concave mirror: converging; real image if $a > f$, magnified virtual image if $a < f$.
  • Convex mirror: diverging; always a smaller, upright, virtual image.
  • Same formulas as lenses: $\frac1a \pm \frac1b = \pm\frac1f$, $m = b/a$.
