Lens Ray Diagrams

Three easy rays are enough to find any image

A convex lens (thicker in the middle) bends parallel light so that it meets at a point, the focal point F: it is a converging lens. A concave lens (thinner in the middle) spreads parallel light out as if it came from a focal point on the near side: a diverging lens. The distance from the lens to F is the focal length $f$. Every lens has a focal point on each side, at the same distance.

To find where a lens forms an image of an object, draw rays from the tip of the object. Three rays are easy (treat the lens as a thin vertical line; the bending happens there):

  • Ray 1: parallel to the axis → after the lens, goes through F on the far side (convex), or spreads out as if it came from F on the near side (concave).
  • Ray 2: through the centre of the lens → goes straight on.
  • Ray 3: through F on the near side (convex), or heading for F on the far side (concave) → leaves parallel to the axis.

Where the rays meet is the image of the tip.

  • Real image: the light really meets there. It can be caught on a screen, and it is upside down (inverted).
  • Virtual image: the rays leaving the lens spread apart; they only seem to come from a point, found by extending them backwards (dashed lines). It can't be put on a screen, and it is upright.

Where the image is (object distance $a$, focal length $f$)

$$\text{convex: } a > f \Rightarrow \text{real, inverted} \qquad a < f \Rightarrow \text{virtual, upright, larger}$$

Convex: $a > 2f$ gives a smaller real image (camera, eye); $a = 2f$ the same size; $f < a < 2f$ a larger real image (projector); $a = f$ no image (light leaves parallel); $a < f$ a magnified virtual image (magnifying glass). Concave: always a smaller, upright, virtual image.

  1. Ray 1: this is the definition of the focal point: light parallel to the axis is sent through (or away from) F.
  2. Ray 2: at its centre, a thin lens is like a thin flat sheet of glass, so the ray isn't turned.
  3. Ray 3: light paths are reversible. Run Ray 1 backwards: light through F leaves parallel.
  4. All rays from one point of the object meet at one point of the image, so any two of them locate it; the third is a check.

Drag the tip of the object (O). f = 10 cm; F marks the focal points

Example 1

An object stands 20 cm from a convex lens with $f = 10$ cm. Describe the image.

Show solution
  1. $a = 2f$. Ray 1 (through F′) and Ray 2 (through the centre) meet 20 cm beyond the lens, as far below the axis as the tip is above.

Real, inverted, the same size, 20 cm behind the lens

Example 2

An object is 30 cm from a convex lens with $f = 10$ cm. Describe the image.

Show solution
  1. $a > 2f$: the rays cross between $f$ and $2f$ on the far side.

Real, inverted, smaller, between 10 cm and 20 cm behind the lens (in fact at 15 cm). This is how a camera works.

Example 3

You hold a magnifying glass ($f = 10$ cm) 5 cm above a page. What do you see, and why?

Show solution
  1. $a < f$: after the lens the rays spread apart and never meet.
  2. Traced back, they seem to come from a point further away than the page, and higher.

An upright, magnified, virtual image (in fact twice the size, 10 cm below the lens)

Example 4

What kind of image does a concave lens always form of a real object?

Show solution
  1. Every ray leaves the lens spreading outwards, so they never meet; traced back, they meet between F and the lens on the object side.

Virtual, upright and smaller, between the lens and the near focal point

Example 5

A slide projector forms a large picture on a screen with a convex lens. Where is the slide placed, and why is it put in upside down?

Show solution
  1. A larger real image needs $f < a < 2f$.
  2. Real images are inverted, so inverting the slide makes the picture upright.

Between $f$ and $2f$ from the lens, upside down so the inverted image is the right way up

In ray diagrams, bend every ray at the centre line of the lens, not at its curved surfaces. And use F on the correct side: Ray 1 of a convex lens goes through the far F; for a concave lens it lines up with the near F.
Ray 1 and Ray 2 are always the easiest pair. Use Ray 3 as a check.
  • Three rays: parallel → F; through the centre → straight; through F → parallel.
  • Real images are inverted and can be caught on a screen; virtual images are upright and can't.
  • Convex: real if $a > f$, virtual if $a < f$. Concave: always virtual and smaller.
