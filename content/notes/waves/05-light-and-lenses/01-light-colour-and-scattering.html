Light, Colour and Scattering

Light is a transverse wave; its wavelength decides its colour

Light is an electromagnetic wave: a transverse wave of electric and magnetic fields. Unlike sound it needs no medium, so it crosses empty space from the Sun. In a vacuum it travels at

  • $c = 3.0 \times 10^{8}$ m/s, about a million times faster than sound.

The light our eyes can detect, visible light, has wavelengths from about 380 nm (violet) to about 770 nm (red), where $1\ \mathrm{nm} = 10^{-9}$ m. In between come blue, green, yellow and orange. Light of one wavelength is monochromatic; sunlight, a mixture of all of them, looks white. Just beyond red is infrared, and beyond violet is ultraviolet.

In a material with refractive index $n$, light slows to $c/n$. Its frequency stays the same, so its wavelength shrinks to $\lambda/n$. The colour we see is fixed by the frequency, so red light is still red under water.

Scattering. Tiny particles such as air molecules bounce light off in all directions, and they scatter short wavelengths (blue) much more strongly than long ones. That is why:

  • the sky is blue: wherever you look, you see sunlight that air has scattered towards you, and that is mostly blue;
  • sunsets are red: low sunlight passes through much more air, so most of the blue is scattered out of the beam before it reaches you;
  • clouds are white: water droplets are much bigger than the wavelength and scatter all colours about equally.

Light in a vacuum and in a medium

$$c = f\lambda \qquad v = \frac cn \qquad \lambda_{\text{medium}} = \frac{\lambda}{n}$$

$c = 3.0 \times 10^{8}$ m/s. $f$: frequency (unchanged in any medium). $\lambda$: wavelength in a vacuum (or air, $n \approx 1.0$). $n$: absolute refractive index (water 1.33, glass about 1.5).

  1. Entering a medium, the frequency doesn't change (as for any wave crossing a boundary).
  2. The speed becomes $v = c/n$.
  3. $\lambda_{\text{medium}} = \dfrac vf = \dfrac{c/n}{f} = \dfrac{\lambda}{n}$.

Pick a wavelength (in a vacuum) and a medium

Example 1

Find the frequency of blue-green light of wavelength 500 nm.

Show solution
  1. $f = c/\lambda = \dfrac{3.0 \times 10^{8}}{5.0 \times 10^{-7}}$.

$f = 6.0 \times 10^{14}$ Hz

Example 2

Orange light of wavelength 600 nm in air enters water ($n = 1.33$). Find its wavelength and frequency in the water.

Show solution
  1. $\lambda_w = 600/1.33 \approx 451$ nm.
  2. $f = c/\lambda = 3.0 \times 10^{8}/(6.0 \times 10^{-7})$, the same in air and water.

About 451 nm; $5.0 \times 10^{14}$ Hz (unchanged)

Example 3

Find the speed of light in glass of refractive index 1.5.

Show solution
  1. $v = c/n = 3.0 \times 10^{8}/1.5$.

$v = 2.0 \times 10^{8}$ m/s

Example 4

Light takes about 500 s to reach the Earth from the Sun. Estimate the Sun–Earth distance.

Show solution
  1. $d = ct = 3.0 \times 10^{8} \times 500$.

$d \approx 1.5 \times 10^{11}$ m

Example 5

Explain why the midday sky is blue but the Sun looks red at sunset.

Show solution
  1. Air molecules scatter short (blue) wavelengths much more than long (red) ones.
  2. Midday: the light reaching you from the sky (not from the Sun directly) is scattered light, mostly blue.
  3. Sunset: the direct sunlight crosses a very long path of air, losing most of its blue on the way. What remains is mainly red and orange.

Scattering removes blue from the beam and spreads it over the sky

The wavelength of light changes when it enters a material, but its frequency (and so its colour) does not. Wavelengths quoted for colours are vacuum (or air) values.
$1\ \mathrm{nm} = 10^{-9}$ m, so 500 nm $= 5.0 \times 10^{-7}$ m. Convert before using $c = f\lambda$.
  • Light: transverse electromagnetic wave, $c = 3.0 \times 10^{8}$ m/s, no medium needed.
  • Visible: about 380 nm (violet) to 770 nm (red).
  • In a medium: $v = c/n$, $\lambda \to \lambda/n$, $f$ unchanged.
  • Blue scatters most: blue sky, red sunset.
