Inertia and Inertial Force

An observer who is accelerating sees an extra force that nobody is pushing with

Three words sound alike, so keep them apart:

  • Inertia: when no net force acts on an object, it keeps its state of motion. At rest, it stays at rest; moving, it keeps moving the same way.
  • The law of inertia is Newton's first law: if the net force on an object is $0$, it stays at rest or keeps moving in a straight line at constant velocity.
  • Inertial force: an apparent force seen by an observer who is accelerating.

Think of a bus that suddenly brakes: you lurch forwards. Nothing in the bus pushed you. Your body simply kept moving (inertia) while the bus slowed down. To someone standing on the pavement there is no extra force at all. But to you, riding in the bus, it feels like a force pushed you forwards. That felt force is the inertial force.

So an inertial force exists only for an observer who is accelerating. An observer at rest (or moving at constant velocity) never sees one.

Inertial force

$$\vec{F} = -m\vec{a}$$

$m$: mass of the object you are watching. $\vec{a}$: acceleration of the observer (not of the object). The minus sign means: opposite to the observer's acceleration. Size: $ma$.

  1. A ball sits on the smooth floor of a bus. No horizontal force acts on it, so from the ground it keeps its velocity.
  2. The bus (and you inside it) speeds up with acceleration $a$ forwards. Relative to you, the ball's acceleration is $0 - a = -a$: it seems to speed up backwards.
  3. You want to explain this with Newton's second law, so you say "a force of size $ma$ pushes it backwards". That is the inertial force $-m\vec{a}$.
  4. Compare with the equation of motion $m\vec{a} = \vec{F}$. The two look similar but:
    • $m$ is the same in both: the mass of the object you are watching.
    • In $m\vec{a} = \vec{F}$, $\vec{F}$ is the total of all forces (gravity, tension, normal force, friction, and inertial force if there is one). In $\vec{F} = -m\vec{a}$, $\vec{F}$ is only the inertial force, like $mg$ is only gravity.
    • In $m\vec{a} = \vec{F}$, $\vec{a}$ is the object's acceleration. In $\vec{F} = -m\vec{a}$, $\vec{a}$ is the observer's acceleration.

Example 1

A bus brakes with an acceleration of size $3.0\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$. What inertial force does a $60\ \mathrm{kg}$ passenger feel, and which way?

Show solution
  1. The observer (the passenger, moving with the bus) accelerates backwards at $3.0\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$.
  2. Inertial force: size $ma = 60 \times 3.0$, opposite to the observer's acceleration.

$180\ \mathrm{N}$, forwards

Example 2

Someone standing on the pavement watches the same passenger. Does that person see the $180\ \mathrm{N}$ force?

Show solution
  1. The person on the pavement is not accelerating, so for them there is no inertial force.
  2. They explain the lurch by inertia: the passenger keeps moving while the bus slows down, until the seat belt or the seat in front pushes back on them.

No; an inertial force appears only for an accelerating observer

Example 3

A $2.0\ \mathrm{kg}$ box sits on the floor of a truck that speeds up at $1.5\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$. The box doesn't slide. Seen from the truck, what forces act on the box horizontally?

Show solution
  1. From the truck the box is at rest, so the horizontal forces balance.
  2. Inertial force: $2.0 \times 1.5 = 3.0\ \mathrm{N}$ backwards. Static friction from the floor must cancel it.

Inertial force $3.0\ \mathrm{N}$ backwards and static friction $3.0\ \mathrm{N}$ forwards

Example 4

In Example 3, write the horizontal equation for an observer on the ground instead.

Show solution
  1. From the ground the box speeds up with the truck: its acceleration is $1.5\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$ forwards.
  2. The only horizontal force is friction $f$: $ma = F$ gives $2.0 \times 1.5 = f$.

$2.0 \times 1.5 = f$, so $f = 3.0\ \mathrm{N}$: the same answer, with no inertial force

Never put an inertial force into an equation written by an observer at rest. First decide who is watching. Only if that observer accelerates do you add the force $ma$ (opposite to the observer's acceleration).
When you draw the inertial force as an arrow, the arrow already shows the direction, so just label it with its size $ma$. The minus sign in $-m\vec{a}$ is only there to say "opposite to the observer".
  • Inertia: with no net force, an object keeps its state of motion.
  • Inertial force $= -m\vec{a}$: it exists only for an accelerating observer, with size $ma$, opposite to the observer's acceleration.
  • In $m\vec{a} = \vec{F}$, $\vec{a}$ belongs to the object and $\vec{F}$ is the total force; in $\vec{F} = -m\vec{a}$, $\vec{a}$ belongs to the observer.
