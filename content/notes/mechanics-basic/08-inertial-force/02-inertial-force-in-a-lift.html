Inertial Force in a Lift

The same object, two observers, two different-looking equations

A ball of mass $m$ hangs from the ceiling of a lift on a string (tension $T$). The lift accelerates upwards at $a$.

  • Observer at rest outside the lift: the ball moves with the lift, so its acceleration is $a$ upwards. Write the equation of motion $ma = T - mg$.
  • Observer inside the lift: this observer accelerates upwards at $a$, so they see an inertial force $ma$ downwards. And to them the ball is not moving: its acceleration is $0$. Write $m \times 0 = T - mg - ma$, a balance of forces.

Move $ma$ across and the two equations are the same, so both observers get the same tension. The common mistake is to mix them: writing $ma$ on the left and an inertial force on the right.

A ball hanging in a lift accelerating upwards at $a$

$$\text{outside: } ma = T - mg \qquad \text{inside: } 0 = T - mg - ma$$

Both give $T = m(g + a)$. Inside the lift, gravity $mg$ and the inertial force $ma$ together act like a stronger gravity, the apparent gravity $m(g + a)$. If the lift accelerates downwards, change the sign of $a$.

  1. Draw the real forces first, for any observer: gravity $mg$ down, tension $T$ up.
  2. Outside, at rest: the observer doesn't accelerate, so no inertial force. The ball's acceleration is $a$ (up). Taking up as positive: $ma = T - mg$.
  3. Inside the lift: the observer's acceleration is $a$ upwards, so add the inertial force $ma$ downwards. The ball looks still, so the left side is $m \times 0$: $0 = T - mg - ma$.
  4. An equation with $0$ on the left is a balance of forces: once you are used to it, simply say "$T$ balances $mg$ and $ma$".

A $2.0\ \mathrm{kg}$ ball hanging in a lift

Example 1

A $2.0\ \mathrm{kg}$ ball hangs in a lift that accelerates upwards at $1.2\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$. Find the tension, using an observer inside the lift.

Show solution
  1. Inertial force $ma = 2.0 \times 1.2 = 2.4\ \mathrm{N}$ downwards; the ball looks still.
  2. Balance: $T = mg + ma = 19.6 + 2.4$.

$T = 22\ \mathrm{N}$

Example 2

Solve Example 1 again, this time as an observer at rest outside the lift.

Show solution
  1. No inertial force. The ball accelerates upwards at $1.2\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$.
  2. $2.0 \times 1.2 = T - 2.0 \times 9.8$.

$T = 22\ \mathrm{N}$, the same tension

Example 3

A $50\ \mathrm{kg}$ person stands on bathroom scales in a lift that accelerates downwards at $2.0\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$. What force do the scales measure?

Show solution
  1. Inside the lift: inertial force $50 \times 2.0 = 100\ \mathrm{N}$ upwards (opposite to the lift's acceleration).
  2. Balance: $N + 100 = 50 \times 9.8 = 490$.

$N = 390\ \mathrm{N}$: the person feels lighter

Example 4

The lift cable breaks and the lift falls freely ($a = g$ downwards). What is the tension in the string holding the ball?

Show solution
  1. Inside: inertial force $mg$ upwards exactly cancels gravity $mg$ downwards.
  2. So $T = m(g - g) = 0$: the apparent gravity is zero.

$T = 0$: everything in the lift floats ("weightless")

Example 5

The lift moves upwards at a steady $3.0\ \mathrm{m/s}$. Find the tension for the $2.0\ \mathrm{kg}$ ball.

Show solution
  1. Steady velocity means $a = 0$: no inertial force, and the ball's acceleration is $0$ for every observer.

$T = mg = 19.6\ \mathrm{N}$: only acceleration matters, not velocity

Inside the lift, writing $ma = T - mg - ma$ is wrong. The observer inside sees the ball at rest, so the left side is $m \times 0$. Use an inertial force or the ball's acceleration $a$, never both.
Inside an accelerating vehicle, combine gravity and the inertial force into one "apparent gravity". In a lift with acceleration $a$ upwards it is $m(g + a)$ downwards; everything inside behaves as if $g$ became $g + a$.
  • Outside (at rest): $ma = T - mg$, with $a$ the ball's acceleration.
  • Inside: add an inertial force $ma$ opposite to the lift's acceleration; the ball is still, so the forces balance.
  • Both observers get the same answer: $T = m(g + a)$.
