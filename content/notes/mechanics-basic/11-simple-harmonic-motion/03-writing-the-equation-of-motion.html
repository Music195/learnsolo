Writing the Equation of Motion for SHM

Set it up carefully and every spring problem ends as $ma = -kx$

The hard part of SHM is writing the equation of motion with the right signs. Three habits make it reliable:

  1. Choose the $+$ direction and the origin, and stick to them. A good origin is the equilibrium position: where the object could rest if placed gently.
  2. Hooke's law uses the change in length from the natural length, not the position $x$. Work out the stretch (or squash) first, from the picture.
  3. If $x$ is negative on the side you're drawing, the length is $-x$, not $x$. Write $k \times (-x)$ and let the signs sort themselves out.

Do this, and whether the spring is horizontal or vertical, stretched or compressed, $+$ to the left or the right, or even with two blocks on a slope, the equation always ends up as $(\text{total mass}) \times a = -kx$, with $x$ measured from the equilibrium position.

Any object on a spring, $x$ measured from equilibrium

$$ma = -kx$$

Vertical spring: the spring is already stretched by $d$ at equilibrium, with $kd = mg$; at position $x$ (down $+$) the stretch is $d + x$, and $mg$ cancels $kd$. Gravity changes where the centre is, but not the period $2\pi\sqrt{m/k}$.

  1. Vertical spring, down $+$. At equilibrium the stretch is $d$ and $kd = mg$. At position $x$ the stretch is $d + x$, so the spring pulls up with $k(d + x)$. Then $ma = mg - k(d + x) = (mg - kd) - kx = -kx$.
  2. Same thing above equilibrium, $x < 0$: the stretch is $d + x$ again (smaller than $d$) and the same algebra gives $ma = -kx$. Writing "$k(d - x)$" here is the usual mistake: $x$ is already negative.
  3. Two blocks on a slope (angle $\theta$, up the slope $+$). Block A (mass $M$) sits on a spring at the bottom; block B (mass $m$) rests against A, uphill, not joined to anything. At equilibrium the spring is squashed by $d$, with $kd = (M + m)g\sin\theta$.
  4. At position $x$ the squash is $d - x$. Taking each block separately (B pushes A with $N$ and A pushes B with $N$):
    A: $Ma = k(d - x) - N - Mg\sin\theta$
    B: $ma = N - mg\sin\theta$.
  5. Add them, since they move together: $(M + m)a = k(d - x) - (M + m)g\sin\theta = -kx$. (B is not joined to the spring, so no spring force acts on B.)

Drag the block: the equation always ends as $-kx$

Example 1

Left is $+$. A block on a smooth floor is joined to a spring fixed to a wall on its left. The block is $0.10\ \mathrm{m}$ to the right of the natural-length point ($k = 20\ \mathrm{N/m}$). Write the equation of motion.

Show solution
  1. Position: $x = -0.10\ \mathrm{m}$ (right of O, and left is $+$). Stretch: $-x = 0.10\ \mathrm{m}$.
  2. The spring pulls left ($+$) with $k(-x)$: $ma = k(-x) = -kx$.

$ma = -kx$, here $ma = +2.0\ \mathrm{N}$

Example 2

A $1.0\ \mathrm{kg}$ mass hangs on a spring with $k = 49\ \mathrm{N/m}$. How far is the spring stretched at equilibrium?

Show solution
  1. $kd = mg$: $d = 9.8/49$.

$d = 0.20\ \mathrm{m}$

Example 3

For the hanging mass in Example 2, write the equation of motion with down $+$ and $x$ from equilibrium, and find the period.

Show solution
  1. $ma = mg - k(d + x) = -kx$, so $a = -49x$.
  2. $\omega = 7.0\ \mathrm{rad/s}$, $T = 2\pi/7.0$.

$1.0\,a = -49x$; $T \approx 0.90\ \mathrm{s}$, the same as on a smooth floor

Example 4

In the slope set-up ($M = 1.0\ \mathrm{kg}$, $m = 0.50\ \mathrm{kg}$, $k = 49\ \mathrm{N/m}$, $\theta = 30^\circ$), find the period, and the push between the blocks when they are at $x = 0$.

Show solution
  1. $(M + m)a = -kx$: $\omega = \sqrt{49/1.5} = 5.7\ \mathrm{rad/s}$, $T = 2\pi/5.7 \approx 1.1\ \mathrm{s}$.
  2. At $x = 0$, $a = 0$, so B's equation gives $N = mg\sin\theta = 0.50 \times 9.8 \times 0.5$.

$T \approx 1.1\ \mathrm{s}$; $N \approx 2.5\ \mathrm{N}$

$ma = -kx$ holds when $x$ is measured from the equilibrium position. If a problem puts the origin at the natural length of a vertical spring instead, the equation becomes $ma = mg - kx$, which is still SHM, but about a different centre.
Before writing any equation, write the spring's stretch or squash as a separate line, like "squash $= d - x$". Then the force is $k \times$ (that), and its direction comes from the picture.
  • Hooke's law uses the change from the natural length.
  • With $x$ from equilibrium, every case gives (mass) $\times\, a = -kx$.
  • Gravity on a vertical spring or slope shifts the centre but doesn't change the period.
  • For blocks that move together, write one equation each and add them.
