The Simple Pendulum

A pendulum swinging through a small angle is SHM

A simple pendulum is a small mass $m$ on a light string of length $L$, swinging to and fro. Strictly, it moves along part of a vertical circle. But when the swing is small, the motion along the arc is simple harmonic motion.

The key step is an approximation. When a question says "the angle is small enough", it is telling you to use

$$\sin\theta \approx \theta \approx \tan\theta \quad (\theta \text{ in radians})$$

This isn't a formula to memorise but a picture: on a circle of radius 1, for a tiny angle, the half-chord ($\sin\theta$), the arc ($\theta$) and the tangent line ($\tan\theta$) are almost the same length.

With it, the equation of motion along the arc becomes $a = -\dfrac{g}{L}x$, which is the SHM condition. Remember the result next to the spring's period: spring $2\pi\sqrt{m/k}$, pendulum $2\pi\sqrt{L/g}$. The pendulum's period doesn't depend on the mass or (for small swings) on how far it swings.

Simple pendulum (small swings)

$$ma = -mg\sin\theta \approx -\frac{mg}{L}x \qquad T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{L}{g}}$$

$x = L\theta$: distance along the arc from the lowest point. $\omega = \sqrt{g/L}$. Valid when $\theta$ is small (in practice, up to about $10^\circ$–$15^\circ$).

  1. Measure $x$ along the arc from the lowest point, $+$ to the right. At angle $\theta$, split gravity: $mg\cos\theta$ along the string (cancelled by the tension and the circular motion), and $mg\sin\theta$ along the arc, pointing back towards the lowest point.
  2. Along the arc: $ma = -mg\sin\theta$, so $a = -g\sin\theta$. As it stands, this is not yet of the form $-\omega^2x$.
  3. Small angle: $\sin\theta \approx \theta$, so $a = -g\theta$.
  4. The arc length is $x = L\theta$ (radius × angle), so $\theta = x/L$ and $a = -\dfrac{g}{L}x$.
  5. This is $a = -\omega^2x$ with $\omega = \sqrt{g/L}$, so $T = \dfrac{2\pi}{\omega} = 2\pi\sqrt{\dfrac{L}{g}}$.

How good is the small-angle formula?

Example 1

Find the period of a simple pendulum of length $1.0\ \mathrm{m}$.

Show solution
  1. $T = 2\pi\sqrt{1.0/9.8} = 2\pi \times 0.319$.

$T \approx 2.0\ \mathrm{s}$

Example 2

How long must a pendulum be to have a period of $1.0\ \mathrm{s}$?

Show solution
  1. $T^2 = 4\pi^2L/g$, so $L = \dfrac{gT^2}{4\pi^2} = \dfrac{9.8 \times 1.0}{39.5}$.

$L \approx 0.25\ \mathrm{m}$

Example 3

A pendulum's mass is doubled and its length made 4 times as long. What happens to its period?

Show solution
  1. The mass doesn't appear in $T = 2\pi\sqrt{L/g}$.
  2. $T \propto \sqrt L$, and $\sqrt4 = 2$.

The period doubles

Example 4

On the Moon, $g$ is about $1/6$ of the Earth's. How does the period of the same pendulum change?

Show solution
  1. $T \propto 1/\sqrt g$; $g$ becomes $1/6$, so $T$ becomes $\sqrt6$ times as long.

About $2.4$ times longer

Example 5

The pendulum hangs in a lift accelerating upwards at $a$. What is its period? (Hint: the topic on inertial force.)

Show solution
  1. Inside the lift the apparent gravity is $g + a$; it plays the role of $g$.

$T = 2\pi\sqrt{\dfrac{L}{g + a}}$, shorter than in a still lift

$\sin\theta \approx \theta$ only works with $\theta$ in radians and only for small angles. At large swings the real period is longer than $2\pi\sqrt{L/g}$, and then the motion is the vertical circular motion from the circular-motion topic, not SHM.
The route is the same as for a spring: equation of motion → (here, approximate) → $a = -\omega^2x$ → $T = 2\pi/\omega$. Any problem that ends in $a = -(\text{constant})\,x$ is SHM.
  • Small angle: $\sin\theta \approx \theta \approx \tan\theta$ (radians).
  • Along the arc: $a = -\dfrac{g}{L}x$, so $T = 2\pi\sqrt{L/g}$.
  • Spring: $2\pi\sqrt{m/k}$; pendulum: $2\pi\sqrt{L/g}$, independent of mass.
