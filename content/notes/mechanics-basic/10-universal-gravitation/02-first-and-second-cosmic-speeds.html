The First and Second Cosmic Speeds

Fast enough to circle the Earth, and fast enough never to come back

Throw something sideways from just above the Earth's surface. Slowly, and it falls back. Faster, and it lands further away. Fast enough, and the ground curves away beneath it as fast as it falls: it keeps going round. Faster still, and it escapes.

  • The first cosmic speed $v_1$ is the speed of a satellite in a circular orbit just above the Earth's surface. "Just above the surface" on a planetary scale means the orbit's radius can be taken as the Earth's radius $R$. (It is still far above mountains and buildings!)
  • The second cosmic speed $v_2$ (escape speed) is the smallest launch speed from the surface for which an object never comes back.

$v_1$ comes from the equation of motion for circular motion. $v_2$ comes from conservation of energy, using $U = -GMm/r$.

Cosmic speeds for a planet of mass $M$ and radius $R$

$$v_1 = \sqrt{\frac{GM}{R}} = \sqrt{gR} \qquad v_2 = \sqrt{\frac{2GM}{R}} = \sqrt{2gR}$$

For the Earth ($g = 9.8\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$, $R = 6.4 \times 10^6\ \mathrm{m}$): $v_1 \approx 7.9\ \mathrm{km/s}$, $v_2 \approx 11.2\ \mathrm{km/s}$. Use the $GM$ forms when $G$ and $M$ are given, and the $g$ forms when only $g$ is given ($GM = gR^2$).

  1. First cosmic speed. The satellite (mass $m$) moves in a circle of radius $R$; the only force on it is universal gravitation, towards the centre. Equation of motion: $m\dfrac{v_1^2}{R} = G\dfrac{Mm}{R^2}$, so $v_1 = \sqrt{\dfrac{GM}{R}}$.
  2. If $G$ and $M$ aren't given, use "universal gravitation at the surface is gravity": $G\dfrac{Mm}{R^2} = mg$, so $GM = gR^2$ and $v_1 = \sqrt{gR}$.
  3. Second cosmic speed. Launch with speed $v_2$ from the surface. Energy at the start: $\tfrac12 mv_2^2 - G\dfrac{Mm}{R}$.
  4. Never coming back means it reaches infinity. At infinity $U = 0$. The smallest launch speed is the one where it arrives there with speed $0$, so the energy there is $0$.
  5. $\tfrac12 mv_2^2 - G\dfrac{Mm}{R} = 0$, so $v_2 = \sqrt{\dfrac{2GM}{R}} = \sqrt{2gR}$, which is $\sqrt{2}$ times $v_1$.

Example 1

Find the first cosmic speed for the Earth, using $g = 9.8\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$ and $R = 6.4 \times 10^6\ \mathrm{m}$.

Show solution
  1. $v_1 = \sqrt{gR} = \sqrt{9.8 \times 6.4 \times 10^6} = \sqrt{6.27 \times 10^7}$.

$v_1 \approx 7.9 \times 10^3\ \mathrm{m/s}$ (7.9 km/s)

Example 2

Find the second cosmic speed for the Earth.

Show solution
  1. $v_2 = \sqrt{2}\,v_1 = 1.414 \times 7.9$.

$v_2 \approx 11.2\ \mathrm{km/s}$

Example 3

How long does a satellite moving at $v_1$ take to go once round the Earth?

Show solution
  1. $T = \dfrac{2\pi R}{v_1} = \dfrac{2\pi \times 6.4 \times 10^6}{7.9 \times 10^3} \approx 5.1 \times 10^3\ \mathrm{s}$.

About $85$ minutes

Example 4

A satellite moves in a circle of radius $2R$ round the Earth. Find its speed in terms of $v_1$.

Show solution
  1. Same equation of motion with $r = 2R$: $v = \sqrt{\dfrac{GM}{2R}} = \dfrac{v_1}{\sqrt 2}$.

$v = v_1/\sqrt2 \approx 5.6\ \mathrm{km/s}$: further out means slower

Don't try to find $v_2$ with a force equation; the object isn't moving in a circle. "Never comes back" is an energy condition: total energy $\ge 0$.
The answer's form depends on what the question gives. If it gives the gravitational constant $G$ and the Earth's mass $M$, answer with $\sqrt{GM/R}$; if it only gives $g$, answer with $\sqrt{gR}$.
  • $v_1$: circular orbit at radius $R$, from the equation of motion, $\sqrt{GM/R} = \sqrt{gR} \approx 7.9\ \mathrm{km/s}$.
  • $v_2$: escape, from energy $= 0$, $\sqrt{2GM/R} = \sqrt{2gR} \approx 11.2\ \mathrm{km/s}$.
  • Between $v_1$ and $v_2$ the path is an ellipse; at or above $v_2$ it escapes.
