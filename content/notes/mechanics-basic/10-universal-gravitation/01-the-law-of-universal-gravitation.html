The Law of Universal Gravitation

Every two masses pull on each other, weaker and weaker as they get further apart

Universal gravitation is the pull that any two objects with mass exert on each other. The gravity you feel right now is one example: it is the Earth's universal gravitation acting on you.

The pull is proportional to both masses and falls off with the square of the distance between their centres: twice as far, a quarter of the pull. The constant $G$ is tiny, which is why you don't notice two people pulling each other together.

Because the force changes with distance, the potential energy is no longer simply $mgh$. We work it out the same way $mgh$ was found, from the work done by an outside force, but now the force changes along the way, so we need an integral. The result is negative, $-\dfrac{GMm}{r}$, because the zero level is chosen infinitely far away, and every closer place is "below" it.

Universal gravitation between masses $M$ and $m$ a distance $r$ apart

$$F = G\frac{Mm}{r^2} \qquad U = -G\frac{Mm}{r}$$

$G = 6.67 \times 10^{-11}\ \mathrm{N\,m^2/kg^2}$: the gravitational constant. $r$: distance between the centres. $U$: potential energy of universal gravitation, zero at infinity. At the Earth's surface (radius $R$), universal gravitation is ordinary gravity: $G\dfrac{Mm}{R^2} = mg$, so $GM = gR^2$.

  1. First, $mgh$ again. Lift a mass $m$ slowly from height $0$ to $h$. The outside force always balances gravity, so it is $mg$ upwards. The change in potential energy is the work it does: $U(h) - U(0) = \displaystyle\int_0^h mg\,dx = mgh$.
  2. Now universal gravitation. Start with $m$ infinitely far from $M$, where the pull is zero; call $U(\infty) = 0$. Move it slowly in to a distance $r$.
  3. At distance $x$, gravity pulls $m$ inwards with $G\dfrac{Mm}{x^2}$. To move slowly, the outside force must hold it back with the same size, outwards. Measuring $x$ outwards, the outside force is $+G\dfrac{Mm}{x^2}$.
  4. The change in potential energy is the work done by the outside force as $x$ goes from $\infty$ to $r$: $U(r) - U(\infty) = \displaystyle\int_\infty^r G\frac{Mm}{x^2}\,dx = \left[-G\frac{Mm}{x}\right]_\infty^r = -G\frac{Mm}{r}$. (The power $x^{-2}$ goes up by one to $x^{-1}$ when integrated.)
  5. So $U(r) = -G\dfrac{Mm}{r}$. It is negative, and rises towards $0$ as $r$ grows.

Example 1

Two $50\ \mathrm{kg}$ people stand $1.0\ \mathrm{m}$ apart. Find the gravitational pull between them.

Show solution
  1. $F = 6.67 \times 10^{-11} \times \dfrac{50 \times 50}{1.0^2}$.

$F \approx 1.7 \times 10^{-7}\ \mathrm{N}$: far too small to notice

Example 2

How big is the Earth's gravity at a height equal to one Earth radius above the surface, compared with at the surface?

Show solution
  1. The distance from the centre doubles: $r = 2R$.
  2. $F \propto 1/r^2$, so it becomes $1/4$: $g/4 = 2.45\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$.

A quarter of the surface value

Example 3

Use $g = 9.8\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$ and $R = 6.4 \times 10^6\ \mathrm{m}$ to find $GM$ for the Earth, then the Earth's mass.

Show solution
  1. $GM = gR^2 = 9.8 \times (6.4 \times 10^6)^2 \approx 4.0 \times 10^{14}\ \mathrm{m^3/s^2}$.
  2. $M = \dfrac{4.0 \times 10^{14}}{6.67 \times 10^{-11}}$.

$M \approx 6.0 \times 10^{24}\ \mathrm{kg}$

Example 4

How much energy is needed to move a mass $m$ from the Earth's surface ($r = R$) to $r = 2R$? Give the answer using $m$, $g$ and $R$.

Show solution
  1. $\Delta U = \left(-\dfrac{GMm}{2R}\right) - \left(-\dfrac{GMm}{R}\right) = \dfrac{GMm}{2R}$.
  2. Put in $GM = gR^2$: $\Delta U = \dfrac{mgR}{2}$.

$\tfrac12 mgR$, less than $mgR$ because gravity weakens on the way up

$mgh$ only works close to the surface, where $g$ hardly changes. For satellites and planets use $-\dfrac{GMm}{r}$, and remember $r$ is measured from the centre of the Earth, not from the surface.
If a problem gives $g$ and $R$ but not $G$ or $M$, use $GM = gR^2$ to replace $GM$ everywhere.
  • $F = GMm/r^2$, with $G = 6.67 \times 10^{-11}\ \mathrm{N\,m^2/kg^2}$.
  • $U = -GMm/r$, zero at infinity.
  • At the surface: $GMm/R^2 = mg$, so $GM = gR^2$.
