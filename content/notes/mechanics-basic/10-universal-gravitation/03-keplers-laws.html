Kepler's Laws

Three rules for how planets move, found from careful observation

Kepler studied years of measurements of the planets and found three laws. They came before Newton, and Newton's law of universal gravitation was built to explain them. Problems on gravitation and orbits often use them together.

  • First law: each planet moves in an ellipse, with the Sun at one focus. (It just says "an ellipse, not necessarily a circle"; you rarely calculate with it, so it's the one people forget.)
  • Second law: the line from the Sun to a planet sweeps out equal areas in equal times (the areal velocity is constant). So a planet moves fastest when it is nearest the Sun.
  • Third law: the square of the period is proportional to the cube of the semi-major axis (half the long axis of the ellipse): $T^2 \propto a^3$, the same constant for every planet of the Sun.

Kepler's second and third laws

$$\tfrac12 r_1v_1 = \tfrac12 r_2v_2 \ \ \text{(nearest and furthest points)} \qquad \frac{T^2}{a^3} = \text{constant}$$

At the nearest and furthest points the velocity is at right angles to the line to the Sun, so the area swept in one second is a thin triangle, $\tfrac12 rv$. $a$: semi-major axis; for a circle, $a$ is the radius. For orbits around a body of mass $M$: $\dfrac{T^2}{a^3} = \dfrac{4\pi^2}{GM}$.

  1. Take a planet of mass $m$ in a circular orbit of radius $r$ (so $a = r$). The third law says $T^2 = kr^3$ for some constant $k$.
  2. Circular motion: the force towards the Sun is $F = mr\omega^2 = mr\left(\dfrac{2\pi}{T}\right)^2 = \dfrac{4\pi^2mr}{T^2}$.
  3. Put in $T^2 = kr^3$: $F = \dfrac{4\pi^2}{k}\cdot\dfrac{m}{r^2}$. The force falls as $1/r^2$ and is proportional to the planet's mass $m$.
  4. By action and reaction, the planet pulls the Sun just as hard, so the force must also be proportional to the Sun's mass $M$. Take $M$ out of the constant: $\dfrac{4\pi^2}{k} = GM$.
  5. That gives $F = G\dfrac{Mm}{r^2}$, the law of universal gravitation, with $G$ the same for every pair of objects.

Equal areas in equal times

Example 1

A comet's nearest distance from the Sun is $1.0\ \mathrm{AU}$ and its furthest is $5.0\ \mathrm{AU}$. How many times faster is it at the nearest point than at the furthest?

Show solution
  1. Second law: $\tfrac12 \times 1.0 \times v_1 = \tfrac12 \times 5.0 \times v_2$.

$v_1 = 5.0\,v_2$: 5 times faster

Example 2

Find the semi-major axis of the comet's orbit in Example 1, and its period (the Earth: $a = 1\ \mathrm{AU}$, $T = 1$ year).

Show solution
  1. The long axis is $1.0 + 5.0 = 6.0\ \mathrm{AU}$, so $a = 3.0\ \mathrm{AU}$.
  2. Third law: $T^2 = 3.0^3 = 27$ (in years and AU), so $T = \sqrt{27}$.

$a = 3.0\ \mathrm{AU}$; $T \approx 5.2$ years

Example 3

Satellite X orbits the Earth in a circle 4 times as large in radius as satellite Y. Compare their periods.

Show solution
  1. $T^2 \propto a^3$: $\left(\dfrac{T_X}{T_Y}\right)^2 = 4^3 = 64$.

$T_X = 8\,T_Y$

Example 4

The Moon goes round the Earth in a circle of radius $3.8 \times 10^8\ \mathrm{m}$ with a period of $2.4 \times 10^6\ \mathrm{s}$. Find $GM$ for the Earth.

Show solution
  1. $GM = \dfrac{4\pi^2a^3}{T^2} = \dfrac{4\pi^2 \times (3.8 \times 10^8)^3}{(2.4 \times 10^6)^2}$.

$GM \approx 3.8 \times 10^{14}\ \mathrm{m^3/s^2}$, close to $gR^2 \approx 4.0 \times 10^{14}$

In the third law use the semi-major axis, half the long axis: $a = \dfrac{r_{\text{near}} + r_{\text{far}}}{2}$. Using the far distance, or the whole long axis, is a very common slip. And the constant $T^2/a^3$ is only shared by orbits around the same central body.
Circular orbit: use the equation of motion, no Kepler needed. Ellipse: speeds at the near and far points come from the second law together with conservation of energy; periods come from the third law.
  • 1st: orbits are ellipses with the Sun at a focus.
  • 2nd: equal areas in equal times; at the near and far points $r_1v_1 = r_2v_2$.
  • 3rd: $T^2/a^3$ is the same for all orbits around one body; it equals $4\pi^2/GM$.
