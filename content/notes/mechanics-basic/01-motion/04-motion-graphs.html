x, v, a and Their Graphs

Slopes go down the chain, areas go back up

Position $x$, velocity $v$ and acceleration $a$ are linked in a chain. Each one is the rate of change of the one before: $v$ is how fast $x$ changes, and $a$ is how fast $v$ changes. In maths words, going down the chain is differentiating (微分) with respect to time.

Going back up the chain is the reverse, integrating (積分). On a graph, differentiating means taking the slope, and integrating means finding the area under the graph.

The three graphs have names: the $x$–$t$ graph, the $v$–$t$ graph and the $a$–$t$ graph. The $v$–$t$ graph is the most useful one, because it shows two things at once: its slope is $a$ and its area is the displacement.

The chain $x \to v \to a$

$$x \;\xrightarrow{\ \text{slope}\ }\; v \;\xrightarrow{\ \text{slope}\ }\; a \qquad\qquad a \;\xrightarrow{\ \text{area}\ }\; \Delta v \qquad v \;\xrightarrow{\ \text{area}\ }\; \Delta x$$

Slope of the $x$–$t$ graph $= v$; slope of the $v$–$t$ graph $= a$. Area under the $v$–$t$ graph $=$ displacement $\Delta x$; area under the $a$–$t$ graph $=$ change in velocity $\Delta v$. Area below the $t$-axis counts as negative.

  1. The slope rules come straight from the definitions $v = \dfrac{dx}{dt}$ and $a = \dfrac{dv}{dt}$ (notes 02 and 03).
  2. For the area rule: if the velocity is a constant $v$ for a time $t$, the displacement is $vt$. On the $v$–$t$ graph, that is a rectangle of height $v$ and width $t$: its area.
  3. If $v$ changes, cut the time into thin strips. In each strip $v$ is almost constant, so the strip's displacement is almost its area $v\,\Delta t$.
  4. Add up all the strips: total displacement $=$ total area. Making the strips thinner makes it exact; this is integration.
  5. The same argument on the $a$–$t$ graph gives: area $=$ change in velocity.

Set the acceleration in three 2-second stages, then move the time $t$

Example 1

On an $x$–$t$ graph, a car's line goes straight from $(0\ \mathrm{s}, 0\ \mathrm{m})$ to $(5\ \mathrm{s}, 40\ \mathrm{m})$. Find its velocity.

Show solution
  1. A straight $x$–$t$ line has a constant slope: $\dfrac{40 - 0}{5 - 0}$.

$v = 8.0\ \mathrm{m/s}$ (constant)

Example 2

A cart moves at a constant $3.0\ \mathrm{m/s}$ for $4.0\ \mathrm{s}$. Use the $v$–$t$ graph to find the displacement.

Show solution
  1. The graph is a horizontal line at $v = 3.0$; the area under it from $0$ to $4.0$ is a rectangle.
  2. Area $= 3.0 \times 4.0$.

$12\ \mathrm{m}$

Example 3

A train starts from rest, and its velocity rises steadily to $10\ \mathrm{m/s}$ in $20\ \mathrm{s}$. Find the acceleration and the distance covered.

Show solution
  1. Slope of the $v$–$t$ line: $a = \dfrac{10}{20} = 0.50\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$.
  2. Area: a triangle, $\tfrac{1}{2} \times 20 \times 10 = 100$.

$a = 0.50\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$; $100\ \mathrm{m}$

Example 4

An $a$–$t$ graph shows $a = 2.0\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$ from $0$ to $3.0\ \mathrm{s}$. The object started at $1.0\ \mathrm{m/s}$. Find its velocity at $3.0\ \mathrm{s}$.

Show solution
  1. Area under the $a$–$t$ graph $= 2.0 \times 3.0 = 6.0$: this is the change in velocity.
  2. $v = 1.0 + 6.0$.

$v = 7.0\ \mathrm{m/s}$

The area gives the change, not the value itself. Area under $v$–$t$ is the displacement, so add the starting position to get $x$. Area under $a$–$t$ is $\Delta v$, so add the starting velocity (Example 4).
When a motion problem looks hard, sketch the $v$–$t$ graph first. One picture gives you the acceleration (slope) and the displacement (area) without any formula.
  • Slope of $x$–$t$ $= v$; slope of $v$–$t$ $= a$ (differentiating).
  • Area under $v$–$t$ $= \Delta x$; area under $a$–$t$ $= \Delta v$ (integrating).
  • Area below the $t$-axis counts as negative.
