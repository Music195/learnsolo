Acceleration

Acceleration is how fast the velocity changes

When the lights turn green, a car pulls away: its velocity grows second by second. When it brakes, its velocity shrinks. Whenever the velocity changes, the object has an acceleration (加速度).

Acceleration is the change in velocity per unit time: how many $\mathrm{m/s}$ the velocity gains (or loses) every second. Its unit is therefore $\mathrm{m/s}$ per $\mathrm{s}$, written $\mathrm{m/s^2}$.

Just as with velocity, there is an average acceleration over a time interval and an instantaneous acceleration at one moment. Acceleration is a vector: on a line it has a sign.

Average and instantaneous acceleration

$$\bar{a} = \frac{v_2 - v_1}{t_2 - t_1} = \frac{\Delta v}{\Delta t} \qquad\qquad a = \lim_{\Delta t \to 0} \frac{\Delta v}{\Delta t} = \frac{dv}{dt}$$

$v_1, v_2$ velocities at times $t_1, t_2$; $a$ in $\mathrm{m/s^2}$. On a $v$–$t$ graph, acceleration is the slope: a straight line means constant acceleration.

  1. Acceleration is built exactly like velocity, one step up: velocity was the change in position per time, acceleration is the change in velocity per time.
  2. On a $v$–$t$ graph, the line through $(t_1, v_1)$ and $(t_2, v_2)$ has slope $\dfrac{v_2 - v_1}{t_2 - t_1}$: the average acceleration.
  3. Let $t_2$ approach $t_1$: the line becomes the tangent, and its slope is the instantaneous acceleration $a = \dfrac{dv}{dt}$.
  4. So $a$ is the derivative of $v$, which is itself the derivative of $x$.

A car drives between two traffic lights: drag the corners of its $v$–$t$ graph

Example 1

A car starts from rest and reaches $20\ \mathrm{m/s}$ in $5.0\ \mathrm{s}$. Find its average acceleration.

Show solution
  1. $\bar{a} = \dfrac{20 - 0}{5.0}$.

$4.0\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$

Example 2

A car moving at $20\ \mathrm{m/s}$ brakes and stops in $4.0\ \mathrm{s}$. Find its acceleration.

Show solution
  1. After − before: $\bar{a} = \dfrac{0 - 20}{4.0} = -5.0$.
  2. The minus sign: the acceleration points opposite to the motion, so the car slows down.

$-5.0\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$

Example 3

A ball is thrown upwards at $15.0\ \mathrm{m/s}$. One second later it moves up at $5.2\ \mathrm{m/s}$. Taking up as positive, find its acceleration.

Show solution
  1. $\bar{a} = \dfrac{5.2 - 15.0}{1.0} = -9.8$.

$-9.8\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$: the gravitational acceleration, pointing down

Example 4

A cart has velocity $-2.0\ \mathrm{m/s}$, and $2.0\ \mathrm{s}$ later $-6.0\ \mathrm{m/s}$. Find its acceleration. Is it speeding up or slowing down?

Show solution
  1. $\bar{a} = \dfrac{-6.0 - (-2.0)}{2.0} = -2.0\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$.
  2. Its speed went from $2.0$ to $6.0$: it is speeding up, in the negative direction.

$-2.0\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$, and it is speeding up

A negative acceleration does not always mean "slowing down". It means the acceleration points in the negative direction. If the velocity is also negative, the object speeds up (Example 4). Slowing down means $v$ and $a$ have opposite signs.
Read $\mathrm{m/s^2}$ as "metres per second, every second". $a = 2\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$ means the velocity grows by $2\ \mathrm{m/s}$ each second: $0, 2, 4, 6, \dots$
  • Acceleration = change in velocity per time, $a = \Delta v / \Delta t$, in $\mathrm{m/s^2}$.
  • It is the slope of the $v$–$t$ graph; instantaneous: $a = dv/dt$.
  • Speeding up: $v$ and $a$ have the same sign. Slowing down: opposite signs.
