Relative Velocity

How fast does the other one seem to move, seen from me?

Sitting in a train, a train passing the other way flashes by. A train going the same way at almost the same speed seems to crawl: you can see the faces of its passengers. The velocity something seems to have, seen by a moving observer, is its relative velocity (相対速度).

"The relative velocity of B with respect to A" (Aに対するBの相対速度) is easier to read as "B's velocity seen from A": A is "me", the observer, and B is "the other one". The rule is a subtraction: the other minus me.

Adding velocities is the opposite case: walking at $1\ \mathrm{m/s}$ inside a train moving at $20\ \mathrm{m/s}$ gives $21\ \mathrm{m/s}$ seen from the ground. This composition of velocities (速度の合成) is an addition, and the order doesn't matter. The subtraction is where mistakes happen.

Velocity of B seen from A

$$v_{AB} = v_B - v_A$$

$v_A$ velocity of the observer A ("me"), $v_B$ velocity of the object B ("the other one"), both measured from the ground, with signs. Read $v_{AB}$ as "from A, B". The order matters: $v_{BA} = v_A - v_B = -v_{AB}$.

  1. Seen from the ground, in a time $t$, A moves $v_A t$ and B moves $v_B t$.
  2. A carries its own view with it. The gap from A to B changes by B's movement minus A's movement: $v_B t - v_A t$.
  3. Divide by $t$: B's velocity seen from A is $v_B - v_A$.
  4. Check with the trains: going the same way at nearly the same speed, $v_B - v_A$ is small; going opposite ways, the signs differ and the sizes add up.

Set the two trains' velocities (right is $+$), then ride in train A

Example 1

Car A drives right at $20\ \mathrm{m/s}$ and car B drives right at $15\ \mathrm{m/s}$. Find the velocity of B relative to A (right is positive).

Show solution
  1. "Relative to A": A is me, B is the other one.
  2. $v_{AB} = v_B - v_A = 15 - 20 = -5$.

$-5\ \mathrm{m/s}$: from A, B seems to drift backwards (left) at $5\ \mathrm{m/s}$

Example 2

Train A goes right at $20\ \mathrm{m/s}$; train B comes the other way at $15\ \mathrm{m/s}$. Find B's velocity seen from A, and A's velocity seen from B.

Show solution
  1. With signs: $v_A = +20$, $v_B = -15$.
  2. From A: $v_{AB} = v_B - v_A = -15 - 20 = -35$.
  3. From B: $v_{BA} = v_A - v_B = 20 - (-15) = +35$.

$-35\ \mathrm{m/s}$ and $+35\ \mathrm{m/s}$: each sees the other rush past at $35\ \mathrm{m/s}$

Example 3

A boat moves at $4.0\ \mathrm{m/s}$ relative to the water, in a river flowing at $3.0\ \mathrm{m/s}$. Find its velocity relative to the bank when it goes downstream and when it goes upstream.

Show solution
  1. This is composition: add the velocities, downstream positive.
  2. Downstream: $4.0 + 3.0 = 7.0$. Upstream: $-4.0 + 3.0 = -1.0$.

$7.0\ \mathrm{m/s}$ downstream; $1.0\ \mathrm{m/s}$ upstream

Example 4

Rain falls straight down at $8.0\ \mathrm{m/s}$. You run east at $6.0\ \mathrm{m/s}$. How fast does the rain seem to fall, seen by you, and from which side does it come?

Show solution
  1. The rule works for vectors too: $\vec{v}_{\text{you, rain}} = \vec{v}_{\text{rain}} - \vec{v}_{\text{you}}$.
  2. East part: $0 - 6.0 = -6.0$ (towards the west). Down part: $8.0$.
  3. Size: $\sqrt{6.0^2 + 8.0^2} = 10$.

$10\ \mathrm{m/s}$, slanting towards you from the front (east): that's why you tilt your umbrella forward

Always the other minus me: $v_{AB} = v_B - v_A$, where A is the observer. Swapping the order gives the right size but the wrong sign. Read the question carefully to decide who is "me".
Imagine sitting in A and ask: "if I were standing still, what would B do?" In the demo, "Seen from train A" does exactly this: A stays still and the ground slides backwards.
  • Relative velocity = velocity seen by a moving observer.
  • $v_{AB} = v_B - v_A$: the other minus me.
  • Composition (adding velocities) is an addition; relative velocity is a subtraction.
