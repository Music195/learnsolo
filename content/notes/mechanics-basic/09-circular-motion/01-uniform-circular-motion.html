Uniform Circular Motion

Steady speed around a circle still needs a force, pointing at the centre

In uniform circular motion an object goes round a circle at a constant speed. The speed doesn't change, but the direction does all the time, so the velocity changes and there must be an acceleration.

Picture rolling a football along a chalk circle on a playground. One kick starts it, and it rolls straight on, along the tangent. To keep it on the circle you must keep giving it little taps towards the inside. So:

  • the velocity points along the tangent to the circle;
  • the acceleration (and the force causing it) points to the centre of the circle.

The force that points to the centre is called the centripetal force. It is not a new kind of force. It is a role that some real force plays: gravity, tension, friction, a normal force, or a component of one of them. Never draw "centripetal force" as an extra arrow next to the real forces.

Uniform circular motion (radius $r$)

$$T = \frac{2\pi r}{v} = \frac{2\pi}{\omega} \qquad v = r\omega \qquad a = r\omega^2 = \frac{v^2}{r} = v\omega$$

$T$: period, the time for one lap (s). $\omega$: angular velocity, the angle turned per second (rad/s). $v$: speed, along the tangent. $a$: acceleration, towards the centre. The equation of motion towards the centre: $m\dfrac{v^2}{r} = F$, where $F$ is the total force towards the centre.

  1. One lap is a distance $2\pi r$ at speed $v$, so $T = 2\pi r / v$. In angles, one lap is $2\pi$ radians at $\omega$ radians per second, so $T = 2\pi/\omega$. Setting the two equal gives $v = r\omega$.
  2. The same thing from maths: an arc of radius $r$ and angle $\theta$ has length $r\theta$. In one second the angle is $\omega$, so the distance travelled each second is $r\omega$.
  3. For the acceleration, take two velocity arrows $\vec{v}_1$ and $\vec{v}_2$ a short time $\Delta t$ apart. Both have size $v$, and they differ in direction by the angle $\omega\Delta t$.
  4. Put them tail to tail. The change $\Delta\vec{v}$ joins their tips and points towards the centre of the circle. For a small angle the chord is nearly the arc: $|\Delta\vec{v}| \approx v \cdot \omega\Delta t$.
  5. So $a = |\Delta\vec{v}|/\Delta t = v\omega$. Use $v = r\omega$ to get the other forms: $a = r\omega^2 = v^2/r$.

Velocity along the tangent, acceleration to the centre

Example 1

A ball goes round a circle of radius $2.0\ \mathrm{m}$ at $4.0\ \mathrm{m/s}$. Find its period and angular velocity.

Show solution
  1. $T = 2\pi r / v = 2\pi \times 2.0 / 4.0 = \pi \approx 3.1\ \mathrm{s}$.
  2. $\omega = v/r = 4.0/2.0$.

$T \approx 3.1\ \mathrm{s}$, $\omega = 2.0\ \mathrm{rad/s}$

Example 2

For the ball in Example 1 (mass $0.50\ \mathrm{kg}$), find the acceleration and the total force needed towards the centre.

Show solution
  1. $a = v^2/r = 16/2.0 = 8.0\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$ (check: $r\omega^2 = 2.0 \times 4.0 = 8.0$).
  2. $F = ma = 0.50 \times 8.0$.

$a = 8.0\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$ towards the centre; $F = 4.0\ \mathrm{N}$

Example 3

A turntable makes $30$ turns per minute. Find $\omega$, and the speed of a coin $0.10\ \mathrm{m}$ from the centre.

Show solution
  1. 30 turns per minute is one turn every $2.0\ \mathrm{s}$, so $T = 2.0\ \mathrm{s}$ and $\omega = 2\pi/T = \pi\ \mathrm{rad/s}$.
  2. $v = r\omega = 0.10 \times \pi$.

$\omega = \pi \approx 3.1\ \mathrm{rad/s}$; $v \approx 0.31\ \mathrm{m/s}$

Example 4

That coin ($m = 0.010\ \mathrm{kg}$) turns with the turntable without slipping. Which force is the centripetal force, and how big is it?

Show solution
  1. Only static friction from the turntable acts sideways on the coin, so friction is the centripetal force.
  2. $f = mr\omega^2 = 0.010 \times 0.10 \times \pi^2$.

Static friction, about $9.9 \times 10^{-3}\ \mathrm{N}$, towards the centre

Example 5

If the same object goes round the same circle twice as fast, what happens to the force needed?

Show solution
  1. $F = mv^2/r$, and $v$ doubles, so $v^2$ becomes 4 times bigger.

4 times the force

"Centripetal force" is not a type of force like gravity or tension. Don't write "centripetal force and gravity act on it". Ask instead: which real force (or which part of it) points to the centre? That is the centripetal force.
You only need to remember one form of the acceleration. Derive the others with $v = r\omega$. Use $v^2/r$ when the problem gives a speed, and $r\omega^2$ when it gives a period or turns per second.

  • Acceleration: to the centre, $a = r\omega^2 = v^2/r$.
  • Period $T = 2\pi r/v = 2\pi/\omega$.
