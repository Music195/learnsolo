Centrifugal Force and the Conical Pendulum

Ride along with the circling object and you feel a force pushing outwards

An object in circular motion accelerates towards the centre. An observer who rides along with it is accelerating too, so (as in the topic on inertial force) they see an inertial force opposite to their acceleration: outwards, with size $m\dfrac{v^2}{r}$. This inertial force in circular motion is called the centrifugal force.

A conical pendulum is a ball on a string of length $L$ that goes round in a horizontal circle, the string making an angle $\theta$ with the vertical. You can solve it from either view:

  • Observer at rest: only the tension $S$ and gravity $mg$ act. Horizontally, the part $S\sin\theta$ is the centripetal force, so write the equation of motion $m\dfrac{v^2}{r} = S\sin\theta$. Vertically nothing moves: $S\cos\theta = mg$.
  • Observer riding with the ball: the ball looks still, so all the forces balance: $S$, $mg$, and the centrifugal force $m\dfrac{v^2}{r}$ outwards. Horizontally $S\sin\theta = m\dfrac{v^2}{r}$, the same equation.

Centrifugal force, and the conical pendulum ($r = L\sin\theta$)

$$F_{\text{cf}} = m\frac{v^2}{r} = mr\omega^2 \qquad v = \sin\theta\sqrt{\frac{gL}{\cos\theta}} \qquad T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{L\cos\theta}{g}}$$

$F_{\text{cf}}$: centrifugal force, outwards, seen only by an observer moving round with the object. Tension $S = \dfrac{mg}{\cos\theta}$. $T$: period of the circle.

  1. Split the tension: $S\cos\theta$ up, $S\sin\theta$ towards the centre.
  2. Vertical (no movement): $S\cos\theta = mg$.
  3. Towards the centre (ground view, equation of motion): $m\dfrac{v^2}{r} = S\sin\theta$.
  4. Divide the second equation by the first to remove $S$: $\dfrac{v^2}{gr} = \tan\theta$, so $v^2 = gr\tan\theta$. Put in $r = L\sin\theta$: $v^2 = gL\dfrac{\sin^2\theta}{\cos\theta}$, so $v = \sin\theta\sqrt{\dfrac{gL}{\cos\theta}}$.
  5. Period: $T = \dfrac{2\pi r}{v} = \dfrac{2\pi L\sin\theta}{\sin\theta\sqrt{gL/\cos\theta}}$. The $\sin\theta$ cancels: $T = 2\pi\sqrt{\dfrac{L\cos\theta}{g}}$.

A conical pendulum ($L = 1.0\ \mathrm{m}$, $m = 0.50\ \mathrm{kg}$)

Example 1

A conical pendulum has $L = 1.0\ \mathrm{m}$, $m = 0.50\ \mathrm{kg}$ and $\theta = 60^\circ$. Find the radius of the circle and the tension.

Show solution
  1. $r = L\sin 60^\circ = 0.87\ \mathrm{m}$.
  2. $S = mg/\cos 60^\circ = 0.50 \times 9.8 / 0.50$.

$r \approx 0.87\ \mathrm{m}$, $S = 9.8\ \mathrm{N}$ (twice the weight)

Example 2

For the pendulum in Example 1, find the speed of the ball.

Show solution
  1. $v = \sin 60^\circ\sqrt{\dfrac{9.8 \times 1.0}{0.50}} = 0.866 \times \sqrt{19.6} = 0.866 \times 4.43$.

$v \approx 3.8\ \mathrm{m/s}$

Example 3

Find the period for the pendulum in Example 1.

Show solution
  1. $T = 2\pi\sqrt{\dfrac{1.0 \times 0.50}{9.8}} = 2\pi \times 0.226$.

$T \approx 1.4\ \mathrm{s}$

Example 4

Write the horizontal equation for an observer riding with the ball in Example 1, and check it gives the same speed.

Show solution
  1. The ball looks still. Outwards: the centrifugal force $m\dfrac{v^2}{r}$. Inwards: $S\sin\theta$. They balance: $S\sin\theta = m\dfrac{v^2}{r}$.
  2. $9.8 \times 0.866 = 0.50 \times v^2 / 0.866$, so $v^2 = 14.7$.

$v \approx 3.8\ \mathrm{m/s}$, the same as Example 2

Example 5

What happens to the period if the ball swings round at a bigger angle $\theta$?

Show solution
  1. $T = 2\pi\sqrt{L\cos\theta/g}$; a bigger $\theta$ makes $\cos\theta$ smaller.

The period gets shorter (and it doesn't depend on the mass)

Centripetal force (inwards) is the role of a real force, seen by any observer. Centrifugal force (outwards) is an inertial force, seen only by an observer going round with the object. Never draw both in the same picture.
You could write $v = \sqrt{gL\sin\theta\tan\theta}$, which is also correct. But keeping $v = \sin\theta\sqrt{gL/\cos\theta}$ makes it easy to see the $\sin\theta$ cancel when you work out the period.
  • Centrifugal force $= mv^2/r$ outwards: an inertial force for an observer going round.
  • Conical pendulum: $S\cos\theta = mg$ and $m\dfrac{v^2}{r} = S\sin\theta$, with $r = L\sin\theta$.
  • $T = 2\pi\sqrt{L\cos\theta/g}$, independent of mass.
