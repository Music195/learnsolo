Using Both Conservation Laws

A block slides up a ramp that can move: momentum and energy together

A small block (mass $m$) slides at $v_0$ towards a ramp (mass $M$) with a smooth curved face. The ramp is not fixed: it sits on a smooth floor. The block pushes the ramp forwards as it climbs, reaches a highest point, slides back down, and leaves the ramp. Two laws work at the same time:

  • Momentum (horizontal): the only horizontal forces are the normal forces between block and ramp, an internal pair. So horizontal momentum is conserved.
  • Mechanical energy: for the pair, only gravity does work. (The normal forces do work on each object, but the two works cancel, just like the string in topic 06.)

At the highest point, the block stops moving relative to the ramp: for an instant they have the same velocity. It does not stop completely.

Block $m$ and free ramp $M$ on a smooth floor

$$mv_0 = (m + M)V, \qquad \tfrac{1}{2}mv_0^2 = \tfrac{1}{2}(m + M)V^2 + mgh \quad\Rightarrow\quad h = \frac{Mv_0^2}{2(m + M)g}$$

After it slides off, kinetic energy is back to $\tfrac{1}{2}mv_0^2$, so it behaves like an elastic collision ($e = 1$): block $u = \dfrac{(m - M)v_0}{m + M}$, ramp $U = \dfrac{2mv_0}{m + M}$.

  1. Highest point: same velocity $V$, so momentum gives $V = \dfrac{mv_0}{m + M}$.
  2. Energy: $mgh = \tfrac{1}{2}mv_0^2 - \tfrac{1}{2}(m + M)V^2 = \tfrac{1}{2}mv_0^2\left(1 - \dfrac{m}{m + M}\right)$, so $h = \dfrac{Mv_0^2}{2(m + M)g}$.
  3. After it leaves (back on the floor, $U$ is the same as at the start): $mv_0 = mu + MU$ and $\tfrac{1}{2}mv_0^2 = \tfrac{1}{2}mu^2 + \tfrac{1}{2}MU^2$. Solving with squares is slow.
  4. Shortcut: kinetic energy is conserved overall, which is exactly what an elastic collision means. So use $e = 1$ instead of the energy equation: $u - U = -(v_0 - 0)$.
  5. Together with momentum (no squares now): $u = \dfrac{(m - M)v_0}{m + M}$.

Send a block at a ramp that can slide (curved face, radius 1 m)

Example 1

A $1.0\ \mathrm{kg}$ block slides at $3.0\ \mathrm{m/s}$ onto a free $2.0\ \mathrm{kg}$ ramp. How high does it rise?

Show solution
  1. Highest point: $1.0 \times 3.0 = 3.0V$, so $V = 1.0\ \mathrm{m/s}$.
  2. $\tfrac{1}{2}(1.0)(3.0)^2 = \tfrac{1}{2}(3.0)(1.0)^2 + 1.0 \times 9.8 \times h$, so $4.5 = 1.5 + 9.8h$.

$h \approx 0.31\ \mathrm{m}$

Example 2

If the ramp in Example 1 were fixed, how high would the block rise? Why is the answer different?

Show solution
  1. Fixed: all the kinetic energy becomes potential: $h = \dfrac{v_0^2}{2g} = \dfrac{9.0}{19.6}$.
  2. With a free ramp, some kinetic energy stays as the motion of both objects at the top.

$0.46\ \mathrm{m}$; the free ramp keeps some energy moving

Example 3

In Example 1, find the velocities of the block and the ramp after the block leaves the ramp.

Show solution
  1. Treat it as elastic: $u - U = -3.0$. Momentum: $u + 2.0U = 3.0$.
  2. Subtract the first from the second: $3.0U = 6.0$, so $U = 2.0$ and $u = 2.0 - 3.0 = -1.0$.

Block $1.0\ \mathrm{m/s}$ backwards; ramp $2.0\ \mathrm{m/s}$ forwards

Example 4

With letters, the block's velocity afterwards is $u = \dfrac{(m - M)v_0}{m + M}$. A question asks for its speed. What should you write?

Show solution
  1. A speed is a size, never negative. If $M > m$, the expression is negative.
  2. Without knowing which mass is bigger, use the absolute value.

$\dfrac{|m - M|}{m + M}\,v_0$

At the highest point the block is at rest relative to the ramp, not relative to the floor. Writing "$v = 0$ at the top" ignores that the ramp (and the block with it) is still moving, and gives $h = \dfrac{v_0^2}{2g}$, which is wrong here.
Whenever the total kinetic energy at the end equals the total at the start, you may replace the energy equation (with its squares) by $e = 1$. It's linear, so it's much faster to solve with momentum.
  • Check each law: horizontal momentum (internal forces only) and energy (only gravity does work on the pair).
  • Highest point: same velocity for both, $h = \dfrac{Mv_0^2}{2(m+M)g}$.
  • Kinetic energy conserved overall ⇒ use $e = 1$ with momentum.
