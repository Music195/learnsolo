The Velocity of the Centre of Mass

The centre of mass keeps a constant velocity; seen from it, a collision is simple

Two objects at positions $x_1$ and $x_2$ have a centre of mass G, the mass-weighted average position. When the objects move, G moves too, with the centre-of-mass velocity $v_G$.

The top of the formula for $v_G$ is $m_1v_1 + m_2v_2$: the total momentum. So whenever momentum is conserved (a collision, a push apart), $v_G$ doesn't change. Before, during and after the collision, G glides along at the same steady speed.

Now watch the collision from a moving observer travelling with G (the centre-of-mass frame; an observer at rest on the ground is the laboratory frame). From G, the two objects come in, bounce, and go out with each velocity simply multiplied by $-e$. The messy algebra of the laboratory frame becomes one multiplication.

Centre of mass and its velocity

$$x_G = \frac{m_1x_1 + m_2x_2}{m_1 + m_2} \qquad v_G = \frac{m_1v_1 + m_2v_2}{m_1 + m_2}$$

Momentum conserved ⇒ $v_G$ constant. Velocities seen from G: $u = v - v_G$. In a collision with coefficient $e$, seen from G: $u' = -e\,u$ for each object. To get back to the ground's view, add $v_G$: $v' = v_G - e(v - v_G)$.

  1. Replace each $x$ by $v$ in the centre-of-mass formula (each $x$ changes at rate $v$): that gives $v_G$. Its top is the total momentum.
  2. Seen from G, the velocities are $u_1 = v_1 - v_G$ and $u_2 = v_2 - v_G$. Their total momentum is $m_1u_1 + m_2u_2 = (m_1v_1 + m_2v_2) - (m_1 + m_2)v_G = 0$.
  3. So from G, momentum is $0$ before and after: $m_1u_1 = -m_2u_2$ and $m_1u_1' = -m_2u_2'$.
  4. Relative velocities don't depend on the observer, so $u_1' - u_2' = -e(u_1 - u_2)$. Using the zero-momentum relations, both sides share the same factor, giving $u_1' = -eu_1$ and $u_2' = -eu_2$.

One collision, two views: from the ground and from G

Example 1

A $2.0\ \mathrm{kg}$ ball moves at $4.0\ \mathrm{m/s}$ towards a $3.0\ \mathrm{kg}$ ball at rest. Find $v_G$.

Show solution
  1. $v_G = \dfrac{2.0 \times 4.0 + 3.0 \times 0}{5.0}$.

$1.6\ \mathrm{m/s}$ (before and after the collision)

Example 2

In Example 1, find each ball's velocity seen from G, before the collision.

Show solution
  1. $u_1 = 4.0 - 1.6 = 2.4$, $u_2 = 0 - 1.6 = -1.6$.
  2. Check: $2.0 \times 2.4 + 3.0 \times (-1.6) = 0$.

$+2.4\ \mathrm{m/s}$ and $-1.6\ \mathrm{m/s}$: they approach G from both sides

Example 3

In Example 1 the collision has $e = 0.50$. Use G to find both velocities afterwards, as seen from the ground.

Show solution
  1. From G: multiply by $-e$: $u_1' = -1.2$, $u_2' = +0.80$.
  2. Add $v_G = 1.6$ back: $v_1' = 0.40$, $v_2' = 2.4$.

$0.40\ \mathrm{m/s}$ and $2.4\ \mathrm{m/s}$, the same as solving momentum + restitution

Example 4

Two balls in a long box keep colliding with each other again and again, each time with $0 < e < 1$ (only internal forces). What happens in the end?

Show solution
  1. From G, each collision multiplies the velocities by $-e$. After $n$ collisions they are $e^n$ times the start, and $e^n \to 0$.
  2. So from G both stop. Seen from the ground, both end up moving at $v_G$.

Both move together at $v_G$, the centre-of-mass velocity

$v_G$ is constant only while momentum is conserved. If an external force acts (friction from the floor, a wall pushing one object), $v_G$ changes, and the "multiply by $-e$" shortcut is not allowed across that time.
For one collision, momentum + restitution is fine. For many collisions, switch to G's view: subtract $v_G$, multiply by $-e$ once per collision, then add $v_G$ back.
  • $v_G = \dfrac{m_1v_1 + m_2v_2}{m_1 + m_2}$: total momentum ÷ total mass.
  • Momentum conserved ⇒ $v_G$ is constant.
  • Seen from G, a collision multiplies each velocity by $-e$; repeated collisions end with both at $v_G$.
