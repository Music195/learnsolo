Impulse and Momentum

Force × time changes the momentum $m\vec{v}$

The impulse of a force is the force multiplied by the time it acts: $\vec{I} = \vec{F}\,t$. Don't mix it up with work ($W = Fx$, force × distance). The two are different in two ways:

  • Units: work is in $\mathrm{N \cdot m} = \mathrm{J}$; impulse is in $\mathrm{N \cdot s}$.
  • Direction: work is a dot product of two vectors, so it's a scalar with no direction. Impulse is a vector (force) times a scalar (time), so it's a vector. You have to add and subtract impulses as arrows.

The momentum of an object is $\vec{p} = m\vec{v}$, also a vector. The key result: the change in momentum equals the impulse it receives.

On a graph of force against time (an F–t graph), the impulse is the area under the graph. In a real collision the force rises and falls very quickly in a shape no simple function describes, so we can't calculate that area directly. Instead, we find the impulse from the change in momentum.

Impulse and momentum

$$\vec{I} = \vec{F}\,\Delta t \qquad m\vec{v}\,' - m\vec{v} = \vec{I}$$

$\vec{I}$ impulse in $\mathrm{N \cdot s}$; $m\vec{v}$ momentum in $\mathrm{kg \cdot m/s}$ (the same unit). For a changing force, $\vec{I}$ is the area under the F–t graph, and the average force is $\bar{F} = \dfrac{I}{\Delta t}$.

  1. Start from the equation of motion $ma = F$, and write the acceleration as the rate of change of velocity: $m\dfrac{dv}{dt} = F$.
  2. Integrate both sides over time, from when the velocity is $v$ to when it is $v'$: $\displaystyle\int_v^{v'} m\,dv = \int F\,dt$.
  3. The left side is $mv' - mv$, the change in $mv$. We name $mv$ the momentum.
  4. The right side is the area under the F–t graph, the impulse. For a constant force it is simply $F\Delta t$. So: change in momentum = impulse.

Impulse as an area, and as an arrow (a $0.20\ \mathrm{kg}$ ball)

Example 1

A constant $20\ \mathrm{N}$ force pushes a $4.0\ \mathrm{kg}$ cart, at rest, for $3.0\ \mathrm{s}$. Find the impulse and the final speed.

Show solution
  1. $I = 20 \times 3.0 = 60\ \mathrm{N \cdot s}$.
  2. $4.0\,v - 0 = 60$.

$60\ \mathrm{N \cdot s}$; $15\ \mathrm{m/s}$

Example 2

A $0.20\ \mathrm{kg}$ ball hits a wall at $10\ \mathrm{m/s}$ and bounces straight back at $8.0\ \mathrm{m/s}$. Find the impulse from the wall.

Show solution
  1. Take "away from the wall" as positive: before $-10$, after $+8.0$.
  2. $I = 0.20 \times 8.0 - 0.20 \times (-10) = 1.6 + 2.0$.

$3.6\ \mathrm{N \cdot s}$, away from the wall (not $0.4$!)

Example 3

A $0.20\ \mathrm{kg}$ ball comes in horizontally at $30\ \mathrm{m/s}$. The bat sends it off at $30\ \mathrm{m/s}$, $60^\circ$ above the horizontal, back the way it came. Find the size of the impulse.

Show solution
  1. Momentum before and after: both $0.20 \times 30 = 6.0\ \mathrm{N \cdot s}$, with $120^\circ$ between the two arrows.
  2. Impulse = after − before: put the arrows tail to tail; the impulse goes from the tip of "before" to the tip of "after".
  3. That triangle is isosceles with a $120^\circ$ angle, so the third side is $2 \times 6.0 \times \sin 60^\circ = 6\sqrt{3}$.

$6\sqrt{3} \approx 10\ \mathrm{N \cdot s}$

Example 4

In Example 3 the ball touches the bat for $1.0 \times 10^{-2}\ \mathrm{s}$. Find the average force on the ball.

Show solution
  1. $\bar{F} = \dfrac{I}{\Delta t} = \dfrac{10.4}{1.0 \times 10^{-2}}$.

About $1.0 \times 10^3\ \mathrm{N}$

Momentum and impulse are vectors. When something bounces back, the change in momentum is the sum of the two sizes, not the difference (Example 2). When the direction changes at an angle, subtract the arrows (Example 3).
Pair them up: force × distance = work → change in kinetic energy (scalars), and force × time = impulse → change in momentum (vectors).
  • Impulse $\vec{I} = \vec{F}\Delta t$ (unit $\mathrm{N \cdot s}$) is the area under the F–t graph.
  • Momentum $\vec{p} = m\vec{v}$; change in momentum = impulse.
  • Both are vectors. Average force = impulse ÷ contact time.
