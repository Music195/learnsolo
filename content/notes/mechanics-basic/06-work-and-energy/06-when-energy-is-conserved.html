When Is Mechanical Energy Conserved?

Only when conservative forces alone do work

In an exam nobody tells you "use energy conservation here". You have to decide for yourself whether it is allowed. The rule is:

Mechanical energy is conserved when only conservative forces do work.

A conservative force is a force whose work doesn't depend on the path, only on where the object starts and ends. In high-school physics there are four: gravity, the elastic force of a spring, universal gravitation and the electric (Coulomb) force. Each has its own potential energy formula. That is the easy test: a force with a potential energy formula is conservative; a force without one is not.

Tension, normal forces, friction and hand pushes have no potential energy, so they are non-conservative. If one of them does work, the mechanical energy of that object is not conserved. (A normal force on a fixed surface does no work, so it doesn't break the rule.)

Condition for conservation of mechanical energy

$$K + U = \text{constant} \iff \text{only conservative forces do work}$$

Conservative (have a $U$): gravity, elastic force, universal gravitation, Coulomb force. Non-conservative: tension, normal force, friction, air resistance, pushes and pulls by hand.

  1. Take block A on a smooth table and block B hanging, joined by a string with tension $T$. B falls $h$ and both reach speed $v$. Use "change in $K$ = work done" for each block separately.
  2. A: the tension pulls it $h$ along the table (same direction): $\tfrac{1}{2}m_A v^2 = Th$. ($m_A g$ and $N$ are at right angles to the motion: no work.)
  3. B: gravity does $m_B g h$, and the tension (upwards, while B moves down) does $-Th$: $\tfrac{1}{2}m_B v^2 = m_B g h - Th$.
  4. Each block alone: the tension, a non-conservative force, does work, so neither block's mechanical energy is conserved. Writing $\tfrac{1}{2}m_B v^2 = m_B g h$ for B alone is wrong.
  5. Add the two equations: $+Th$ and $-Th$ cancel. $\tfrac{1}{2}m_A v^2 + \tfrac{1}{2}m_B v^2 = m_B g h$. For the pair, the tension's total work is zero, so energy conservation holds for the two together.

Move a $1\ \mathrm{kg}$ block from P down to Q along different routes ($\mu = 0.20$)

Example 1

Which of these forces are conservative: gravity, kinetic friction, the force of a spring, the tension in a string, air resistance?

Show solution
  1. Ask: does it have a potential energy formula? Gravity: $mgh$. Spring: yes (elastic potential energy, a later topic).
  2. Friction, tension and air resistance have none.

Conservative: gravity and the spring force. The others are non-conservative

Example 2

A ball swings on a string like a pendulum (no air resistance). Is its mechanical energy conserved?

Show solution
  1. Forces: gravity (conservative) and tension (non-conservative).
  2. The tension always points along the string, at right angles to the ball's motion, so it does no work.

Yes: only gravity does work

Example 3

A student writes, for the hanging block B (mass $3.0\ \mathrm{kg}$) of a block pair, "$\tfrac{1}{2} \times 3.0\,v^2 = 3.0 \times 9.8 \times 1.0$", and gets $v = 4.4\ \mathrm{m/s}$. The real answer is $3.4\ \mathrm{m/s}$. Explain.

Show solution
  1. The string pulls B upwards while B moves down, so the tension does negative work on B.
  2. So B's mechanical energy is not conserved: some of it goes through the string to the other block.

Tension does work on B alone; use energy conservation for both blocks together

Example 4

A $2.0\ \mathrm{kg}$ block goes from a point $3.0\ \mathrm{m}$ high to the ground, once straight down a slide and once along a long winding slide. Find the work done by gravity each time.

Show solution
  1. Gravity is conservative: its work depends only on the drop in height, $mgh$.
  2. $2.0 \times 9.8 \times 3.0$.

$58.8\ \mathrm{J}$ both times

Before using energy conservation, list every force that does work on the object (or group of objects) you chose. If a non-conservative force does work and doesn't cancel out, plain conservation is wrong; use "change in energy = work" instead (next note).
Make it a habit: "Which forces do work? Are they all conservative?" Asking this every time means you never have to guess whether energy is conserved.
  • Conservative force: its work doesn't depend on the path. Gravity, elastic, universal gravitation, Coulomb; each has a potential energy.
  • Mechanical energy is conserved only if conservative forces alone do work.
  • Tension inside a pair of connected objects does work on each, but the two works cancel for the pair.
