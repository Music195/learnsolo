Work

A force does work when the object moves along the force

In everyday life, "work" means any effort. In physics it has a strict meaning: when a force acts on an object and the object moves in the direction of the force, the force does work on the object.

Carrying a heavy bag along a level path is hard, but your upward lifting force does no work: the bag moves sideways, at right angles to your force. Only the part of the force along the movement counts.

Work is done by one particular force, so always say which one: "the work done by gravity", "the work done by friction". Several forces can act on the same object, each doing its own work.

Work done by a constant force

$$W = Fx\cos\theta = \vec{F}\cdot\vec{x}$$

$F$ size of the force in $\mathrm{N}$, $x$ distance moved in $\mathrm{m}$, $\theta$ the angle between the force and the displacement. Unit: the joule, $1\ \mathrm{J} = 1\ \mathrm{N \cdot m}$. Work is positive for $\theta < 90^\circ$, zero at $90^\circ$, and negative for $\theta > 90^\circ$.

  1. Simplest case: the force points the same way as the movement. Then $W = Fx$. A force of $1\ \mathrm{N}$ moving an object $1\ \mathrm{m}$ does $1\ \mathrm{J}$ of work.
  2. If the force is at an angle $\theta$, split it into a part along the movement, $F\cos\theta$, and a part at right angles, $F\sin\theta$.
  3. The object doesn't move at right angles, so that part does no work. The part along the movement does $W = (F\cos\theta)\,x$.
  4. In vector language, this is the dot product of the force vector and the displacement vector: $\vec{F}\cdot\vec{x} = |\vec{F}||\vec{x}|\cos\theta$.

A $2.0\ \mathrm{kg}$ block is pulled $2.0\ \mathrm{m}$ up a rough $30^\circ$ slope ($\mu = 0.30$)

Example 1

A box is pushed $3.0\ \mathrm{m}$ along a floor by a $20\ \mathrm{N}$ force in the direction of motion. Find the work done by the push.

Show solution
  1. $\theta = 0^\circ$: $W = 20 \times 3.0 \times \cos 0^\circ$.

$60\ \mathrm{J}$

Example 2

A sledge is pulled $5.0\ \mathrm{m}$ along level snow by a rope at $60^\circ$ above the horizontal, with tension $40\ \mathrm{N}$. Find the work done by the rope.

Show solution
  1. $W = 40 \times 5.0 \times \cos 60^\circ = 200 \times 0.5$.

$100\ \mathrm{J}$

Example 3

A block of mass $m$ is pulled a distance $L$ up a slope at angle $\theta$. Find the work done by gravity.

Show solution
  1. Gravity points straight down; the displacement points up the slope. The angle between them is $90^\circ + \theta$.
  2. $W = mgL\cos(90^\circ + \theta) = -mgL\sin\theta$.

$W = -mgL\sin\theta$ (negative: the block moves up, against gravity)

Example 4

A $2.0\ \mathrm{kg}$ block slides $4.0\ \mathrm{m}$ along a level floor with $\mu = 0.25$. Find the work done by the normal force and by kinetic friction.

Show solution
  1. Normal force: at $90^\circ$ to the movement, so $W_N = 0$.
  2. Friction: $\mu mg = 4.9\ \mathrm{N}$, opposite to the movement ($180^\circ$): $W_f = 4.9 \times 4.0 \times \cos 180^\circ$.

$W_N = 0$; $W_f = -19.6\ \mathrm{J}$

$\theta$ is the angle between the force and the displacement, not the angle of the slope. On a slope there are often two angles in the picture: check which one sits between the two arrows you care about.
Quick check for the sign: a force helping the motion does positive work; a force at right angles (like the normal force on a fixed surface) does zero work; a force against the motion (like kinetic friction) does negative work.
  • $W = Fx\cos\theta$, the dot product of force and displacement; unit joule ($\mathrm{J}$).
  • Only the part of the force along the movement does work.
  • Work can be positive, zero or negative; always say which force does it.
