When Friction Does Work

Friction turns mechanical energy into heat

Kinetic friction is non-conservative, so when it does work, mechanical energy is not conserved. You can still use energy, in one of two ways. The key is to know which one you are writing, so you don't count the same thing twice.

  1. Change in mechanical energy = work done by non-conservative forces. The work done by gravity is already inside $U$, so only friction (and any other non-conservative force) goes on the right. Kinetic friction's work is $-\mu N L$: it takes energy away.
  2. Count friction as heat. The energy friction takes away doesn't vanish: it becomes heat, $Q = \mu N L$ (always positive). Then everything is conserved again, and you can write increases = decreases with the heat as one of the increases.

Think of the money story again: U gives 5000 coins to K, through a third friend H. K only gets 4000 coins. H kept 1000 coins. The money K and U have together went down by 1000, but with H counted, nothing is lost. H is the heat.

Energy with kinetic friction

$$(K + U)_{\text{after}} - (K + U)_{\text{before}} = W_{\text{non-conservative}} \qquad Q = \mu N L$$

With friction only: $W_{\text{non-conservative}} = -\mu N L$, where $L$ is the distance slid. As gains and losses: (increases in $K$ and $U$) $+\ Q$ = (decreases in $K$ and $U$).

  1. Start from the work–energy relation: change in $K$ = work done by all forces.
  2. Split the work into the work of conservative forces and of non-conservative forces.
  3. The work of a conservative force is the drop in its potential energy: $W_{\text{cons}} = -(\text{change in } U)$. Move it to the left: change in $K$ + change in $U$ = $W_{\text{non-cons}}$.
  4. So if you use $U$ for gravity on the left, don't also put gravity's work on the right. That would count it twice.

A $1\ \mathrm{kg}$ block slides $4.0\ \mathrm{m}$ down a rough slope from rest

Example 1

A $2.0\ \mathrm{kg}$ block slides $5.0\ \mathrm{m}$ along a level floor ($\mu = 0.20$), starting at $6.0\ \mathrm{m/s}$. Find its speed at the end.

Show solution
  1. Heat: $Q = \mu mg L = 0.20 \times 2.0 \times 9.8 \times 5.0 = 19.6\ \mathrm{J}$.
  2. Decrease in $K$ = heat: $\tfrac{1}{2} \times 2.0 \times 6.0^2 - \tfrac{1}{2} \times 2.0\,v^2 = 19.6$.
  3. $36 - v^2 = 19.6$, so $v^2 = 16.4$.

$v \approx 4.0\ \mathrm{m/s}$

Example 2

A block of mass $m$ slides a distance $L$ down a slope at angle $\theta$ from rest, with coefficient of kinetic friction $\mu$. Find its speed $v$.

Show solution
  1. $N = mg\cos\theta$, so the heat is $Q = \mu mg\cos\theta \cdot L$.
  2. Decrease: $U$, by $mgL\sin\theta$. Increases: $K$ and $Q$.
  3. $\tfrac{1}{2}mv^2 + \mu mgL\cos\theta = mgL\sin\theta$.

$v = \sqrt{2gL(\sin\theta - \mu\cos\theta)}$

Example 3

Block A ($2.0\ \mathrm{kg}$) is on a rough $30^\circ$ slope ($\mu = 0.20$), tied over a pulley at the top to block B ($3.0\ \mathrm{kg}$), which hangs. From rest, B falls $1.0\ \mathrm{m}$ and A moves $1.0\ \mathrm{m}$ up the slope. Find their speed.

Show solution
  1. Increases: $K_A + K_B = \tfrac{1}{2}(5.0)v^2$; $U_A$ by $2.0 \times 9.8 \times 1.0\sin 30^\circ = 9.8$; heat $Q = 0.20 \times 2.0 \times 9.8\cos 30^\circ \times 1.0 \approx 3.39$.
  2. Decrease: $U_B$ by $3.0 \times 9.8 \times 1.0 = 29.4$.
  3. $2.5v^2 + 9.8 + 3.39 = 29.4$, so $v^2 \approx 6.48$.

$v \approx 2.5\ \mathrm{m/s}$

Example 4

For Example 2, a student writes $\tfrac{1}{2}mv^2 - mgL\sin\theta = mgL\sin\theta - \mu mgL\cos\theta$. What went wrong?

Show solution
  1. The left side already uses the change in $U$ (gravity's potential energy).
  2. The right side should only have non-conservative work. Gravity's work $mgL\sin\theta$ was counted a second time.

Gravity counted twice: as $U$ on the left and as work on the right

Two common mistakes: counting gravity twice (as $U$ and as work), and writing the heat as just $\mu N$. Heat is a force times the distance slid: $Q = \mu N L$, and on a slope $N = mg\cos\theta$, not $mg$.
In the gains-and-losses table, the heat $Q$ is always an increase. Friction reduces the mechanical energy, but the heat itself is energy that something (the surfaces) receives.
  • With friction, mechanical energy is not conserved: change in $K + U$ = work by non-conservative forces.
  • Friction's lost energy becomes heat $Q = \mu N L$; counting it makes "increases = decreases" work again.
  • Know which form you use, so gravity isn't counted twice.
