Kinetic Energy and Work

Energy is the ability to do work; a moving object has kinetic energy

When an object can do work on another object, we say it has energy. Energy is the ability to do work, so it is measured in joules too. An object with a lot of energy can push things around a long way, which is also why it can be dangerous.

A moving object has energy just because it moves: a rolling cart can push into a stack of books and shove a ruler along. This is kinetic energy, $K = \tfrac{1}{2}mv^2$.

It also works the other way. Do work on an object and its kinetic energy changes by exactly that amount: the change in kinetic energy equals the work done on it. Positive work speeds it up; negative work (like friction) slows it down.

Kinetic energy, and the work–energy relation

$$K = \tfrac{1}{2}mv^2 \qquad \tfrac{1}{2}mv^2 - \tfrac{1}{2}mv_0^2 = W$$

$m$ mass in $\mathrm{kg}$, $v$ speed in $\mathrm{m/s}$, $K$ in $\mathrm{J}$. Left: change in kinetic energy (after − before). Right: the total work done by all the forces on the object.

  1. A constant force $F$ pushes an object (mass $m$) a distance $x$ in its direction, and its speed changes from $v_0$ to $v$.
  2. Equation of motion: $ma = F$, so $a = \dfrac{F}{m}$.
  3. Constant acceleration: $v^2 - v_0^2 = 2ax = \dfrac{2Fx}{m}$.
  4. Multiply by $\tfrac{m}{2}$: $\tfrac{1}{2}mv^2 - \tfrac{1}{2}mv_0^2 = Fx$. The right side is the work done.
  5. Special case: a cart at speed $v$ pushes into something with force $F$ and stops after $x$. Then $0 - \tfrac{1}{2}mv^2 = -Fx$: it did work $Fx = \tfrac{1}{2}mv^2$ on the thing it hit. So $\tfrac{1}{2}mv^2$ measures its ability to do work: its kinetic energy.

A constant force $F$ acts on a cart over $5.0\ \mathrm{m}$: watch $K$ change

Example 1

Find the kinetic energy of a $1200\ \mathrm{kg}$ car at $20\ \mathrm{m/s}$ ($72\ \mathrm{km/h}$).

Show solution
  1. $K = \tfrac{1}{2} \times 1200 \times 20^2 = 600 \times 400$.

$2.4 \times 10^5\ \mathrm{J}$

Example 2

A $2.0\ \mathrm{kg}$ cart at rest is pushed with $10\ \mathrm{N}$ for $5.0\ \mathrm{m}$ on a smooth floor. Find its speed.

Show solution
  1. Work: $W = 10 \times 5.0 = 50\ \mathrm{J}$.
  2. $\tfrac{1}{2} \times 2.0 \times v^2 - 0 = 50$, so $v^2 = 50$.

$v \approx 7.1\ \mathrm{m/s}$

Example 3

A $4.0\ \mathrm{kg}$ box slides at $5.0\ \mathrm{m/s}$ and stops after $10\ \mathrm{m}$ because of friction. Find the friction force.

Show solution
  1. $0 - \tfrac{1}{2} \times 4.0 \times 5.0^2 = W_f = -f \times 10$.
  2. $-50 = -10f$.

$f = 5.0\ \mathrm{N}$

Example 4

A car doubles its speed. How many times bigger is its kinetic energy? How many times longer does it take to stop with the same braking force?

Show solution
  1. $K \propto v^2$: double $v$, four times $K$.
  2. Stopping: $Fx = K$, so with the same $F$ the distance $x$ is four times as long.

4 times the energy, 4 times the stopping distance

$K$ uses the speed squared, so it is never negative and it grows fast with speed. And the $W$ in the work–energy relation is the work done by all the forces together, including friction (negative).
The work–energy relation is great when a problem gives a distance and asks for a speed (or the other way round), with no time involved. You don't need the acceleration at all.
  • Energy is the ability to do work, measured in joules.
  • Kinetic energy $K = \tfrac{1}{2}mv^2$.
  • Change in kinetic energy = work done on the object: $\tfrac{1}{2}mv^2 - \tfrac{1}{2}mv_0^2 = W$.
