What a Force Does

You can't see a force, only what it does

What is a force? You can point at a pen, but you can't point at a force. We only know a force is there by what it does to an object. There are three things a force can do:

1. Change an object's shape: squash a balloon and it deforms. 2. Change an object's motion: start it, stop it, speed it up, slow it down or turn it. 3. Support an object: a shelf holds books up against gravity.

High-school mechanics is about the second one. "Changing the motion" means changing the velocity: its size, its direction, or both. A change of velocity is an acceleration. So a net force produces an acceleration, and no net force means no acceleration: that is the first law again, seen from the other side.

Force and change of motion

$$\vec{F}_{\text{net}} \ne \vec{0} \;\Longleftrightarrow\; \vec{v}\ \text{changes} \;\Longleftrightarrow\; \vec{a} \ne \vec{0}$$

$\vec{F}_{\text{net}}$ net force, $\vec{v}$ velocity, $\vec{a}$ acceleration. A change of direction at constant speed is also a change of velocity, so it also needs a force. The next note gives the exact size: $m\vec{a} = \vec{F}$.

  1. A force is recognised by its effects. In mechanics we use the effect "changes the motion".
  2. So if no net force acts, the motion doesn't change: the velocity stays constant, including staying at rest. This is the first law.
  3. If a net force acts, the motion changes: the velocity changes, so there is an acceleration. This is the start of the second law.
  4. The laws are not separate facts to memorise: they all follow from "a force is what changes motion".

A puck on smooth ice: give it short pushes and watch when its motion changes

Example 1

Which effect of a force is shown by each: (a) a sponge squashed in your hand, (b) a ball hit by a bat, (c) a lamp held up by its cord?

Show solution
  1. (a) The sponge changes shape. (b) The ball's velocity changes. (c) The cord holds the lamp against gravity.

(a) change of shape, (b) change of motion, (c) support

Example 2

A satellite goes round the Earth in a circle at a constant speed. Is a net force acting on it?

Show solution
  1. Its speed is constant, but its direction keeps changing, so its velocity changes.
  2. A changing velocity needs a net force. Here it is the Earth's gravity, pulling towards the centre.

Yes: gravity, which keeps turning its direction

Example 3

A lift moves upwards at a constant $2\ \mathrm{m/s}$. Compare the cable's tension with the lift's weight.

Show solution
  1. Constant velocity: the motion doesn't change, so the net force is zero.
  2. Only two forces act (tension up, gravity down), so they are equal.

The tension equals the weight, even though the lift is moving

Example 4

A ball thrown straight up stops for an instant at the top. Is the net force on it zero at that moment?

Show solution
  1. At the top, $v = 0$ but the velocity is still changing: a moment ago it was upwards, a moment later downwards.
  2. So there is an acceleration, $9.8\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$ down, and the net force is gravity $mg$.

No: gravity still acts. "At rest for an instant" is not "in equilibrium"

"The motion changes" means the velocity changes, and turning counts. An object moving at a steady speed round a bend is accelerating, so a net force must act on it (Example 2).
To decide whether a net force acts, look at the motion, not at what seems to be pushing. Constant velocity in a straight line: net force zero. Anything else (speeding up, slowing down, turning): a net force acts.
  • A force can change shape, change motion, or support an object.
  • In mechanics: a net force changes the velocity, so it causes an acceleration.
  • Turning at constant speed is a change of velocity too.
