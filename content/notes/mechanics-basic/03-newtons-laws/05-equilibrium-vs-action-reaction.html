Equilibrium vs Action–Reaction

Both pairs are equal and opposite; what differs is the object

Two forces in equilibrium (つり合い) and an action–reaction pair (作用反作用) look alike: both are on the same line, equal in size and opposite in direction. But they are completely different things, and mixing them up breaks every equation of motion.

The test is: which object does each force act on? Write each force as "who on whom". Balancing forces have the same "whom": they both act on one object. An action–reaction pair has "who" and "whom" swapped: each acts on the other object.

An apple resting on a table

$$\underbrace{N = W}_{\text{equilibrium: both on the apple}} \qquad\qquad \underbrace{N = N'}_{\text{action–reaction: on apple, on table}}$$

$W$ Earth on apple (gravity), $N$ table on apple (normal force), $N'$ apple on table. The reaction to $W$ is $W'$: the apple pulling the Earth (universal gravitation, 万有引力), acting at the Earth's centre.

  1. Gravity on the apple, as a sentence: "the Earth pulls the apple". The normal force: "the table pushes the apple".
  2. Same "whom" (the apple): these two balance. The apple is at rest, so $N = W$. This came from the balance of forces, not from the third law.
  3. Reaction to gravity: swap the words: "the apple pulls the Earth". Gravity is universal gravitation: every pair of masses attracts each other. So the reaction acts on the Earth, at its centre, not on the table.
  4. Reaction to the normal force: "the apple pushes the table", $N'$. It acts on the table.

An apple (0.10 kg) on a table (5.0 kg): choose a force to see what balances it and what its reaction is

Example 1

An apple rests on the floor. Name (1) the force that balances the gravity on the apple, and (2) the reaction to the gravity on the apple.

Show solution
  1. (1) Other forces on the apple: it touches only the floor, so the normal force from the floor.
  2. (2) Gravity is "Earth pulls apple"; swap: "apple pulls Earth".

(1) the normal force from the floor; (2) the apple's gravitational pull on the Earth

Example 2

A lamp hangs at rest from a cord. Which force balances the tension on the lamp, and what is the reaction to that tension?

Show solution
  1. Tension: "cord pulls lamp". The other force on the lamp is gravity, "Earth pulls lamp": they balance.
  2. Swap: "lamp pulls cord", downwards. It acts on the cord.

Gravity on the lamp balances it; the reaction is the lamp pulling the cord down

Example 3

A $50\ \mathrm{kg}$ person stands in a lift that accelerates upwards at $2.0\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$. Find the floor's push $N$ on the person, and the person's push on the floor.

Show solution
  1. The person accelerates, so the forces on them do not balance. Equation of motion (up positive): $50 \times 2.0 = N - 50 \times 9.8$, so $N = 590$.
  2. The person's push on the floor is the reaction to $N$: always equal, $590\ \mathrm{N}$ downwards.

$N = 590\ \mathrm{N}$ (more than $mg = 490\ \mathrm{N}$); the person pushes the floor with $590\ \mathrm{N}$

Example 4

Box A ($2.0\ \mathrm{kg}$) sits on box B ($3.0\ \mathrm{kg}$) on the floor. Find the force A puts on B and the force the floor puts on B.

Show solution
  1. On A: gravity $19.6$ down, B's push up. At rest, B pushes A up with $19.6\ \mathrm{N}$.
  2. Reaction (third law): A pushes B down with $19.6\ \mathrm{N}$.
  3. On B: gravity $29.4$ and A's push $19.6$ down, the floor's push up. At rest: floor $= 29.4 + 19.6 = 49$.

A pushes B with $19.6\ \mathrm{N}$; the floor pushes B with $49\ \mathrm{N}$

The normal force is not the reaction to gravity. They both act on the apple, so they are a balancing pair, and only while the apple isn't accelerating (Example 3). The reaction to gravity is the apple's pull on the Earth.
Write both forces as "who on whom" sentences and compare. Same "whom": a balancing pair (check whether it is really at rest). Names swapped: action and reaction (always equal).
  • Both pairs are equal, opposite and on one line; the difference is which object they act on.
  • Equilibrium: forces on the same object, only when it isn't accelerating.
  • Action–reaction: subject and object swapped, on different objects, always equal.
