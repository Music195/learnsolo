The First Law (Inertia)

With no net force, an object keeps doing what it was doing

Newton's three laws of motion (運動の3法則) are the foundation of all of mechanics. The first one is the law of inertia (慣性の法則):

When no force acts on an object, or the forces on it add up to zero, an object at rest stays at rest, and a moving object keeps moving in a straight line at constant speed (等速直線運動).

"At rest stays at rest" feels obvious. "Keeps moving forever" does not: a ball rolling on the floor always stops in the end. But that is because friction and air resistance act on it. Take every force away and nothing would slow it down.

Inertia (慣性) is the tendency of every object to keep its motion as it is. When a bus brakes suddenly, your body lurches forward: it is trying to keep moving at the old velocity.

The law of inertia

$$\vec{F}_{\text{net}} = \vec{0} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \vec{v} = \text{constant}$$

$\vec{F}_{\text{net}}$ the net force (合力, all forces on the object added as vectors), $\vec{v}$ its velocity. "Constant velocity" includes $\vec{v} = \vec{0}$ (at rest). No force and balanced forces behave the same way.

  1. Galileo found the idea without a perfect experiment, using a thought experiment (思考実験). Release a ball on a smooth ramp; it rolls down, across, and up a second ramp.
  2. Without friction it climbs back to the same height it started from, then rolls back.
  3. Make the second ramp gentler. The ball still climbs to the same height, so it must roll further along the ramp to get there.
  4. Make the second ramp completely flat. Now the ball can never reach that height again, so it never stops: it rolls on at constant speed forever.
  5. So motion doesn't need a force to keep it going. A force is needed only to change the motion.

Galileo's ramps (no friction): make the right-hand ramp gentler and gentler

Example 1

A bus brakes suddenly and the standing passengers lurch forwards. Has a force pushed them forwards?

Show solution
  1. No forward force acts on them. The bus slows down, but their bodies, by inertia, keep moving at the old velocity.
  2. Seen from the bus, they seem to move forwards; seen from the road, they just keep going while the bus slows.

No: they keep moving because of inertia

Example 2

A puck slides across perfectly smooth ice at $3.0\ \mathrm{m/s}$. Ignoring air, what is its velocity after $10\ \mathrm{s}$, and how far has it gone?

Show solution
  1. Along the ice, no force acts (smooth, no air). Up and down, gravity and the normal force balance. The net force is zero.
  2. So the velocity stays $3.0\ \mathrm{m/s}$, and in $10\ \mathrm{s}$ it goes $3.0 \times 10$.

Still $3.0\ \mathrm{m/s}$; $30\ \mathrm{m}$

Example 3

A car drives along a straight road at a steady $60\ \mathrm{km/h}$, with its engine running. What is the net force on it?

Show solution
  1. Constant velocity in a straight line, so by the first law the net force is zero.
  2. The engine's driving force is real, but friction and air resistance cancel it exactly.

Zero: the forward force and the resistance balance

Example 4

A space probe far from every star switches off its engine while moving at $15\ \mathrm{km/s}$. What happens to it?

Show solution
  1. Almost no force acts on it: no air, no friction, gravity far too weak.
  2. By inertia it keeps its velocity. It needs no fuel to keep going, only to change speed or direction.

It keeps moving in a straight line at $15\ \mathrm{km/s}$

The old idea "a moving object needs a force to keep it moving" is wrong. A force changes motion; it is not needed to keep motion going. If an object moves at constant velocity, the net force on it is zero, even though it is moving.
"Net force zero" has two possible pictures: at rest, or moving at constant velocity. You cannot tell which from the forces alone; you need to know how it was moving to begin with.
  • Net force zero: at rest stays at rest, moving keeps moving in a straight line at constant speed.
  • Inertia is every object's tendency to keep its motion.
  • Forces change motion; they are not needed to keep it going.
