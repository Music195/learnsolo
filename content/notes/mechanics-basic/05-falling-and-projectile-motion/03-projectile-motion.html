Projectile Motion

Split the motion: across it moves at constant velocity, up and down it's a vertical throw

Throw a ball at speed $v_0$ at an angle $\theta$ above the horizontal and it flies along a curve called a parabola. This is projectile motion. The trick is to never think about the curve as a whole. Look at the two directions separately:

  • Horizontal ($x$): no force acts that way, so $a_x = 0$. The ball moves at a constant $v_0\cos\theta$, like its shadow on the ground under a midday sun.
  • Vertical ($y$, up positive): only gravity acts, so $a_y = -g$. This is a vertical throw upwards, with starting speed $v_0\sin\theta$, like the ball's shadow on a wall.

Both directions share the same clock $t$. A horizontal throw is the special case $\theta = 0$: across it is constant velocity, and up and down it is free fall.

Thrown from the origin at $v_0$, angle $\theta$ (up positive)

$$\begin{aligned} v_x &= v_0\cos\theta, & x &= v_0\cos\theta \cdot t \\ v_y &= v_0\sin\theta - gt, & y &= v_0\sin\theta \cdot t - \tfrac{1}{2}gt^2 \end{aligned}$$

Top at $t_1 = \dfrac{v_0\sin\theta}{g}$, height $H = \dfrac{v_0^2\sin^2\theta}{2g}$. It lands at $2t_1$, a distance $L = \dfrac{v_0^2\sin 2\theta}{g}$ away, which is longest at $\theta = 45^\circ$.

  1. In the air, the only force is gravity $mg$, straight down. Equations of motion: $ma_x = 0$ and $ma_y = -mg$, so $a_x = 0$, $a_y = -g$.
  2. Split the starting velocity: $v_0\cos\theta$ across and $v_0\sin\theta$ up. With constant acceleration in each direction, the formulas above follow. (If you know calculus: integrate $a_x$ and $a_y$ twice with respect to $t$; the starting values are the constants of integration.)
  3. Top: $v_y = 0$, so $t_1 = \dfrac{v_0\sin\theta}{g}$. Height: area of the $v_y$–$t$ triangle, $H = \tfrac{1}{2} v_0\sin\theta \cdot t_1 = \dfrac{v_0^2\sin^2\theta}{2g}$.
  4. It lands at $2t_1$ (symmetry), so $L = v_0\cos\theta \cdot 2t_1 = \dfrac{2v_0^2\sin\theta\cos\theta}{g} = \dfrac{v_0^2\sin 2\theta}{g}$, using $2\sin\theta\cos\theta = \sin 2\theta$.
  5. $\sin 2\theta$ is at most $1$, when $2\theta = 90^\circ$. So the longest throw is at $\theta = 45^\circ$, with $L = \dfrac{v_0^2}{g}$.

Throw a ball: its shadows show the two separate motions

Example 1

A ball is thrown horizontally at $10\ \mathrm{m/s}$ from the top of a $19.6\ \mathrm{m}$ cliff. When and how far from the cliff does it land?

Show solution
  1. Up and down it is free fall: $19.6 = \tfrac{1}{2} \times 9.8\,t^2$, so $t = 2.0\ \mathrm{s}$.
  2. Across, constant velocity: $x = 10 \times 2.0$.

After $2.0\ \mathrm{s}$, $20\ \mathrm{m}$ from the cliff

Example 2

A ball is thrown at $19.6\ \mathrm{m/s}$ at $30^\circ$ above the horizontal. Find the time to the top and the greatest height.

Show solution
  1. Vertical start: $19.6 \sin 30^\circ = 9.8\ \mathrm{m/s}$. $t_1 = 9.8 / 9.8 = 1.0\ \mathrm{s}$.
  2. $H = \tfrac{1}{2} \times 9.8 \times 1.0$.

$1.0\ \mathrm{s}$; $4.9\ \mathrm{m}$

Example 3

In Example 2, how far away does the ball land?

Show solution
  1. It lands at $2t_1 = 2.0\ \mathrm{s}$.
  2. Across: $19.6\cos 30^\circ \times 2.0 \approx 17.0 \times 2.0$.

About $34\ \mathrm{m}$

Example 4

What is the farthest you can throw a ball on level ground at $14\ \mathrm{m/s}$, and at what angle?

Show solution
  1. $L = \dfrac{v_0^2 \sin 2\theta}{g}$ is largest when $\sin 2\theta = 1$, at $\theta = 45^\circ$.
  2. $L = \dfrac{14^2}{9.8} = 20$.

$20\ \mathrm{m}$, at $45^\circ$

At the top of the curve only the vertical velocity is $0$. The ball is still moving across at $v_0\cos\theta$, so its speed at the top is $v_0\cos\theta$, not $0$.
$\sin 2\theta$ is the same for $\theta$ and $90^\circ - \theta$, so throws at $30^\circ$ and $60^\circ$ land in the same place (try it in the demo). The $60^\circ$ throw goes higher and takes longer.
  • Split into $x$ (constant velocity $v_0\cos\theta$) and $y$ (vertical throw with $v_0\sin\theta$, $a = -g$).
  • Top: $v_y = 0$, at $t_1 = \dfrac{v_0\sin\theta}{g}$; it lands at $2t_1$.
  • Range $L = \dfrac{v_0^2\sin 2\theta}{g}$, longest at $45^\circ$.
