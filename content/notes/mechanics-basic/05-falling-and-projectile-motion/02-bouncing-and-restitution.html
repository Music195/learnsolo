Bouncing: the Coefficient of Restitution

$e$ says what fraction of the speed a ball keeps when it bounces

Drop a ball on the floor and it bounces back up, but usually more slowly than it hit. The coefficient of restitution $e$ (also called the coefficient of rebound) is the ratio of the speed just after the bounce to the speed just before.

  • A super ball keeps almost all its speed: $e$ is close to $1$.
  • A lump of clay goes splat and doesn't bounce at all: $e = 0$.

Here the ball hits something that can't move, like a floor or a wall. (Collisions between two moving objects come in a later topic.)

Coefficient of restitution (hitting a fixed floor or wall)

$$e = -\frac{v'}{v} \qquad (0 \le e \le 1)$$

$v$ velocity just before, $v'$ just after (same positive direction). The minus sign is there because the direction reverses. $e = 1$: elastic collision; $0 < e < 1$: inelastic; $e = 0$: perfectly inelastic.

  1. Take up as positive. The ball hits the floor at $-v_0$ and leaves at $+ev_0$.
  2. On the v–t graph, the flight before the bounce ends in a triangle with height $v_0$; the flight after starts a triangle with height $ev_0$. Both lines have the same slope $-g$, so the two triangles are similar, with ratio $1 : e$.
  3. Similar shapes: areas are in the ratio of the squares, $1 : e^2$. The areas are the heights, so $h_2 = e^2 h_1$.
  4. The time of each flight is in the ratio $1 : e$: each bounce is $e$ times as long as the one before.

Drop a ball from $5.0\ \mathrm{m}$ and change $e$

Example 1

A ball hits the floor at $8.0\ \mathrm{m/s}$ and leaves at $6.0\ \mathrm{m/s}$. Find $e$.

Show solution
  1. Down positive: $v = 8.0$, $v' = -6.0$. $e = -\dfrac{-6.0}{8.0}$.

$e = 0.75$ (an inelastic collision)

Example 2

A ball is dropped from $2.5\ \mathrm{m}$ onto a floor with $e = 0.60$. How high does it bounce?

Show solution
  1. $h_2 = e^2 h_1 = 0.36 \times 2.5$.

$0.90\ \mathrm{m}$

Example 3

A ball is thrown straight up from the floor at $v_0$. It comes back down and bounces with coefficient $e$. Find the height of the first bounce in terms of $v_0$, $e$ and $g$.

Show solution
  1. First flight: up to $H_1 = \dfrac{v_0^2}{2g}$, back to the floor at speed $v_0$ (symmetry).
  2. It leaves the floor at $ev_0$, so the next height is $\dfrac{(ev_0)^2}{2g} = e^2 H_1$.

$H_2 = \dfrac{e^2 v_0^2}{2g}$

Example 4

A ball dropped from $4.0\ \mathrm{m}$ bounces to $1.0\ \mathrm{m}$. Find $e$.

Show solution
  1. $\dfrac{h_2}{h_1} = e^2 = \dfrac{1.0}{4.0} = 0.25$.

$e = 0.50$

$e$ is a ratio of speeds, not of heights. The bounce height goes down by $e^2$: with $e = 0.5$ the ball keeps half its speed but only reaches a quarter of the height.
On a v–t graph (up positive), a bounce is a sudden jump from $-v$ up to $+ev$. Then the line continues with the same slope $-g$. Try drawing it yourself before you look at the demo.
  • $e = -\dfrac{v'}{v}$, between $0$ and $1$: elastic ($1$), inelastic, perfectly inelastic ($0$).
  • After a bounce the speed is $e$ times as big, so the height is $e^2$ times as big.
  • On the v–t graph, the triangles before and after a bounce are similar with ratio $1 : e$.
