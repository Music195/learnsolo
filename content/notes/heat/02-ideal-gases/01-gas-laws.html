The Gas Laws

Squeeze a gas and its pressure rises; heat it and it expands

A fixed amount of gas is described by three numbers: pressure $P$, volume $V$ and temperature $T$. Two experiments link them.

  • Boyle's law (temperature fixed): halve the volume and the pressure doubles. $PV$ stays the same.
  • Charles's law (pressure fixed): for every 1 °C of warming, the volume grows by $\frac{1}{273}$ of its volume at 0 °C. So $V = V_0\left(1 + \frac{t}{273}\right) = V_0\,\frac{273 + t}{273}$.

That "$273 + t$" is the temperature in kelvin, $T$. Following the line back, the volume would reach zero at $t = -273$ °C: absolute zero, the coldest anything can be. Measured from there, Charles's law is simply $V \propto T$.

Put the two laws together and you get one rule for any change of a fixed amount of gas: $PV/T$ stays the same.

A fixed amount of gas

$$P_1V_1 = P_2V_2 \qquad \frac{V_1}{T_1} = \frac{V_2}{T_2} \qquad \frac{P_1V_1}{T_1} = \frac{P_2V_2}{T_2}$$

Boyle (same $T$), Charles (same $P$), and the combined law. $T$ must be in kelvin: $T = t + 273$.

  1. Go from $(P_1, V_1, T_1)$ to $(P_2, V_2, T_2)$ in two steps through a middle state $(P_2, V', T_1)$.
  2. Step 1, same temperature (Boyle): $P_1V_1 = P_2V'$, so $V' = \dfrac{P_1V_1}{P_2}$.
  3. Step 2, same pressure (Charles): $\dfrac{V'}{T_1} = \dfrac{V_2}{T_2}$.
  4. Put in $V'$: $\dfrac{P_1V_1}{P_2T_1} = \dfrac{V_2}{T_2}$, so $\dfrac{P_1V_1}{T_1} = \dfrac{P_2V_2}{T_2}$.

Gas in a cylinder: start at $P = 1.0 \times 10^5$ Pa, $V = 3.0$ L, $T = 300$ K

Example 1

2.0 L of gas at $1.0 \times 10^5$ Pa is squeezed to 0.50 L at the same temperature. Find the new pressure.

Show solution
  1. Boyle: $P_2 = \dfrac{P_1V_1}{V_2} = \dfrac{1.0 \times 10^5 \times 2.0}{0.50}$.

$P_2 = 4.0 \times 10^5\ \mathrm{Pa}$

Example 2

3.0 L of gas at 27 °C is heated to 127 °C at constant pressure. Find the new volume.

Show solution
  1. Change to kelvin: 300 K and 400 K.
  2. Charles: $V_2 = V_1\,\dfrac{T_2}{T_1} = 3.0 \times \dfrac{400}{300}$.

$V_2 = 4.0\ \mathrm{L}$ (not $3.0 \times \frac{127}{27}$)

Example 3

A gas at $1.0 \times 10^5$ Pa, 6.0 L and 300 K is changed to $2.0 \times 10^5$ Pa and 450 K. Find its volume.

Show solution
  1. $V_2 = V_1 \times \dfrac{P_1}{P_2} \times \dfrac{T_2}{T_1} = 6.0 \times \dfrac12 \times \dfrac{450}{300}$.

$V_2 = 4.5\ \mathrm{L}$

Example 4

A sealed rigid can holds gas at $1.0 \times 10^5$ Pa and 27 °C. It is heated to 327 °C. Find the pressure.

Show solution
  1. The volume is fixed, so the combined law gives $\dfrac{P_1}{T_1} = \dfrac{P_2}{T_2}$.
  2. $P_2 = 1.0 \times 10^5 \times \dfrac{600}{300}$.

$P_2 = 2.0 \times 10^5\ \mathrm{Pa}$

Example 5

At 0 °C a gas has volume $V_0$. Using Charles's law, at what temperature would its volume be zero at the same pressure?

Show solution
  1. $V = V_0\left(1 + \dfrac{t}{273}\right) = 0$ when $\dfrac{t}{273} = -1$.

$t = -273\ ^\circ\mathrm{C}$, which is $0$ K (absolute zero)

The gas laws only work with kelvin. "From 27 °C to 54 °C" is not doubling the temperature: it is 300 K to 327 K, a rise of only 9 %.
You can always use the combined law $\frac{PV}{T} = \text{constant}$; anything that stays the same simply cancels. That's quicker than deciding which named law applies.
  • Boyle: $PV$ constant at fixed $T$. Charles: $V/T$ constant at fixed $P$.
  • Combined: $PV/T$ constant for a fixed amount of gas.
  • Always use kelvin: $T = t + 273$. Absolute zero is −273 °C.
