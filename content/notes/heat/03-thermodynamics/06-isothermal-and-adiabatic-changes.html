Isothermal and Adiabatic Changes

Isothermal: temperature fixed. Adiabatic: no heat in or out, so the temperature changes

  • Isothermal change: the temperature stays the same (slow, with the gas touching something at a fixed temperature). $\Delta U = 0$, so $Q = W$: any work the gas does is paid for by heat flowing in. On the P–V graph it follows $PV = $ constant, a curve called an isotherm. Isotherms further from the origin are hotter.
  • Adiabatic change: no heat goes in or out (the gas is insulated, or the change is too fast for heat to flow). $Q = 0$, so $\Delta U = -W$.

In an adiabatic expansion, the gas does work using its own internal energy, so it cools. In an adiabatic compression the work done on it raises its internal energy, so it warms: this is why a bicycle pump gets hot, and why a diesel engine can ignite fuel without a spark.

Because the temperature also falls as it expands, the pressure of an adiabatic gas drops faster than along an isotherm: adiabatic curves are steeper than isotherms through the same point.

Adiabatic change of an ideal gas (Poisson's law)

$$PV^{\gamma} = \text{constant} \qquad TV^{\gamma - 1} = \text{constant} \qquad \gamma = \frac{C_P}{C_V} = \frac53 \ (\text{monatomic})$$

Compare isothermal: $PV = $ constant. Since $\gamma > 1$, $P$ falls faster with $V$ on an adiabatic curve. The second form follows from the first using $P = nRT/V$.

  1. Isothermal: $\Delta T = 0$ so $\Delta U = 0$, and the first law gives $Q = W$. Expanding ($W > 0$) takes in heat; compressing gives heat out.
  2. Adiabatic: $Q = 0$, so $0 = \Delta U + W$, i.e. $\Delta U = -W$. Expansion: $W > 0$, $\Delta U < 0$, it cools.
  3. Steeper: expand from the same point by the same $\Delta V$. Isothermally $T$ stays put; adiabatically $T$ drops. Lower $T$ at the same $V$ means lower $P$, so the adiabatic curve is below the isotherm on the expansion side: it is steeper.
  4. Using Poisson: $P_1V_1^{\gamma} = P_2V_2^{\gamma}$ and $T_1V_1^{\gamma-1} = T_2V_2^{\gamma-1}$. (Deriving these needs calculus; here we just use them.)

Example 1

A monatomic gas expands adiabatically to 8 times its volume. By what factor do its pressure and its temperature change?

Show solution
  1. $PV^{5/3}$ constant: $P_2 = P_1 \times 8^{-5/3}$. Since $8^{1/3} = 2$, $8^{5/3} = 2^5 = 32$.
  2. $TV^{2/3}$ constant: $T_2 = T_1 \times 8^{-2/3} = T_1 / 2^2$.

Pressure × $\frac{1}{32}$, temperature × $\frac14$

Example 2

1.0 mol of a monatomic gas at 300 K is compressed adiabatically; 1000 J of work is done on it. Find its new temperature.

Show solution
  1. $Q = 0$ and $W = -1000$ J (work done on the gas), so $\Delta U = -W = +1000$ J.
  2. $\Delta T = \dfrac{\Delta U}{\tfrac32nR} = \dfrac{1000}{1.5 \times 8.31} \approx 80$ K.

$T \approx 380\ \mathrm{K}$

Example 3

A gas expands isothermally and does 300 J of work. Find $\Delta U$ and $Q$.

Show solution
  1. Isothermal: $\Delta U = 0$.
  2. $Q = \Delta U + W = 300$ J.

$\Delta U = 0$, $Q = 300\ \mathrm{J}$ taken in

Example 4

An isotherm and an adiabatic curve pass through the same point. Which is steeper, and why?

Show solution
  1. On expansion, the adiabatic gas does work with no heat coming in, so it cools.
  2. At the same volume, lower temperature means lower pressure.

The adiabatic curve is steeper

Example 5

A cycle: A → B constant-pressure expansion; B → C adiabatic expansion back to A's temperature; C → A isothermal compression. In which steps is heat taken in, and is the net work positive?

Show solution
  1. A → B: $W > 0$ and $T$ rises ($\Delta U > 0$), so $Q > 0$: heat taken in.
  2. B → C: adiabatic, $Q = 0$.
  3. C → A: isothermal compression: $\Delta U = 0$, $W < 0$, so $Q < 0$: heat given out.
  4. The cycle runs clockwise on the P–V graph (out at high pressure, back at low pressure), so the net work is the enclosed area and is positive.

Heat is taken in only on A → B; net work > 0

"No heat flows" does not mean "the temperature stays the same". In an adiabatic change the temperature changes because of the work. Constant temperature is the isothermal case, where heat does flow.
Exam numbers are chosen to make $V^{5/3}$ easy: a factor of 8 ($8^{1/3} = 2$) or 27 ($27^{1/3} = 3$). Take the cube root first, then the power.
  • Isothermal: $\Delta U = 0$, $Q = W$, $PV$ constant.
  • Adiabatic: $Q = 0$, $\Delta U = -W$; expansion cools, compression warms; $PV^{5/3}$ constant for a monatomic gas.
  • Adiabatic curves are steeper than isotherms; isotherms further out are hotter.
