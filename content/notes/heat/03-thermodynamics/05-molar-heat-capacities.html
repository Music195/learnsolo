Molar Heat Capacities and Mayer's Relation

Heat per mole per kelvin depends on how you heat: $C_P$ is bigger than $C_V$ by exactly $R$

For solids and liquids we used "heat per gram per kelvin". For gases it is easier to count in moles, and it matters whether the gas can expand while it warms.

  • Molar heat capacity at constant volume $C_V$: heat to warm 1 mol by 1 K in a rigid container. $Q = nC_V\,\Delta T$.
  • Molar heat capacity at constant pressure $C_P$: heat to warm 1 mol by 1 K with a free piston. $Q = nC_P\,\Delta T$.
  • Mayer's relation: $C_P = C_V + R$. The extra $R$ pays for the work of pushing the piston out.

The most useful fact of all: since $U$ depends only on $T$, $\Delta U = nC_V\,\Delta T$ for every change, not just constant-volume ones. Constant volume is merely the case where all the heat becomes $\Delta U$, which lets us measure it.

Ideal gas

$$\Delta U = nC_V\,\Delta T \ (\text{any change}) \qquad C_P = C_V + R \qquad \text{monatomic: } C_V = \tfrac32R,\ C_P = \tfrac52R$$

$C_V$, $C_P$ in J/(mol·K). The ratio $\gamma = C_P/C_V$ is $\frac53$ for a monatomic gas (used in the next note). For two-atom molecules $C_V = \frac52R$ and $C_P = \frac72R$, but Mayer's relation still holds.

  1. Warm $n$ mol by $\Delta T$ at constant pressure: $Q = nC_P\,\Delta T$.
  2. $\Delta U$ depends only on $\Delta T$, so it is the same as at constant volume: $\Delta U = nC_V\,\Delta T$.
  3. Work at constant pressure: $W = P\,\Delta V = nR\,\Delta T$.
  4. First law: $nC_P\,\Delta T = nC_V\,\Delta T + nR\,\Delta T$, so $C_P = C_V + R$.
  5. Monatomic: $\Delta U = \tfrac32nR\,\Delta T$ gives $C_V = \tfrac32R$, so $C_P = \tfrac52R$. At constant pressure $Q : \Delta U : W = \tfrac52 : \tfrac32 : 1 = 5 : 3 : 2$.

Warm 1 mol of gas by $\Delta T$

Example 1

A monatomic gas expands at constant pressure and does 200 J of work. Find $Q$ and $\Delta U$.

Show solution
  1. At constant pressure, $Q : \Delta U : W = 5 : 3 : 2$.
  2. $W = 2$ parts $= 200$ J, so 1 part $= 100$ J.

$Q = 500\ \mathrm{J}$, $\Delta U = 300\ \mathrm{J}$

Example 2

2.0 mol of a monatomic gas is warmed by 20 K at constant pressure. Find $Q$.

Show solution
  1. $Q = nC_P\,\Delta T = 2.0 \times \tfrac52 \times 8.31 \times 20$.

$Q \approx 8.3 \times 10^2\ \mathrm{J}$

Example 3

0.50 mol of a monatomic gas goes from 300 K to 500 K along a curved path on the P–V graph. Find $\Delta U$.

Show solution
  1. $\Delta U = nC_V\,\Delta T$ for any path: $0.50 \times \tfrac32 \times 8.31 \times 200$.

$\Delta U \approx 1.2 \times 10^3\ \mathrm{J}$

Example 4

A gas has $C_V = 20.8$ J/(mol·K). Find $C_P$ and $\gamma$.

Show solution
  1. $C_P = C_V + R = 20.8 + 8.31 \approx 29.1$ J/(mol·K).
  2. $\gamma = 29.1/20.8$.

$C_P \approx 29.1$ J/(mol·K), $\gamma \approx 1.4$ (a two-atom gas)

Example 5

A monatomic gas is given 1000 J of heat at constant pressure. How much becomes work?

Show solution
  1. $W/Q = 2/5$.

$W = 400\ \mathrm{J}$ (and $\Delta U = 600$ J)

$\Delta U$ always uses $C_V$, even when the change is at constant pressure. Writing $\Delta U = nC_P\,\Delta T$ is a common slip: that is $Q$, not $\Delta U$.
Remember $5 : 3 : 2$ for $Q : \Delta U : W$ in a constant-pressure change of a monatomic gas. Knowing any one of them gives the other two at once.
  • $\Delta U = nC_V\,\Delta T$ for any change of an ideal gas.
  • Mayer: $C_P = C_V + R$.
  • Monatomic: $C_V = \tfrac32R$, $C_P = \tfrac52R$, $\gamma = \tfrac53$; at constant $P$, $Q : \Delta U : W = 5 : 3 : 2$.
