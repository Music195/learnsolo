Constant-Volume and Constant-Pressure Changes

Same warming, different heat: at constant pressure some heat leaves as work

Two simple ways to heat a gas, both easy to draw on a P–V graph:

  • Constant volume (isochoric; a vertical line): the container is rigid, so $W = 0$ and all the heat goes into internal energy: $Q = \Delta U$.
  • Constant pressure (isobaric; a horizontal line): a free piston lets the gas expand, so it does work $W = P\,\Delta V$. Now $Q = \Delta U + P\,\Delta V$: more heat is needed for the same warming.

The trick that makes these quick: in $PV = nRT$, if one of $P$ or $V$ stays fixed, the change becomes $\Delta P\cdot V = nR\,\Delta T$ or $P\,\Delta V = nR\,\Delta T$. So you can swap $nR\,\Delta T$ for something you can read straight off the graph.

Monatomic ideal gas

$$\text{constant } V:\ Q = \Delta U = \frac32V\,\Delta P \qquad \text{constant } P:\ Q = \frac32P\,\Delta V + P\,\Delta V = \frac52P\,\Delta V$$

In terms of temperature: constant $V$, $Q = \frac32nR\,\Delta T$; constant $P$, $Q = \frac52nR\,\Delta T$. The heat per mole per kelvin, $\frac32R$ and $\frac52R$, are the molar heat capacities $C_V$ and $C_P$ (next note).

  1. Constant $V$. $PV = nRT$ with $V$ fixed: $V\,\Delta P = nR\,\Delta T$. $W = 0$, and $\Delta U = \tfrac32nR\,\Delta T = \tfrac32V\,\Delta P$. So $Q = \tfrac32V\,\Delta P$.
  2. Constant $P$. With $P$ fixed: $P\,\Delta V = nR\,\Delta T$. Work $W = P\,\Delta V$.
  3. $\Delta U = \tfrac32nR\,\Delta T = \tfrac32P\,\Delta V$.
  4. $Q = \Delta U + W = \tfrac32P\,\Delta V + P\,\Delta V = \tfrac52P\,\Delta V$. Of every 5 parts of heat, 3 parts warm the gas and 2 parts become work.

A $(2.0\ \mathrm{L},\ 1.0 \times 10^5\ \mathrm{Pa})$ → B (same V, $3.0 \times 10^5$ Pa) → C (5.0 L, same P)

Example 1

A monatomic gas goes from A ($2.0 \times 10^{-3}$ m³, $1.0 \times 10^5$ Pa) to B ($2.0 \times 10^{-3}$ m³, $3.0 \times 10^5$ Pa). Find the heat it takes in.

Show solution
  1. Constant volume: $W = 0$, so $Q = \Delta U = \tfrac32V\,\Delta P$.
  2. $Q = 1.5 \times 2.0 \times 10^{-3} \times 2.0 \times 10^5$.

$Q_{AB} = 600\ \mathrm{J}$

Example 2

It then goes from B to C ($5.0 \times 10^{-3}$ m³, $3.0 \times 10^5$ Pa). Find $W$, $\Delta U$ and $Q$ for B → C.

Show solution
  1. $W = P\,\Delta V = 3.0 \times 10^5 \times 3.0 \times 10^{-3} = 900$ J.
  2. $\Delta U = \tfrac32P\,\Delta V = 1350$ J.
  3. $Q = \Delta U + W = \tfrac52P\,\Delta V$.

$W = 900$ J, $\Delta U = 1350$ J, $Q_{BC} = 2250\ \mathrm{J}$

Example 3

1.0 mol of a monatomic gas is warmed by 10 K at constant volume. How much heat is needed?

Show solution
  1. $Q = \tfrac32nR\,\Delta T = 1.5 \times 1.0 \times 8.31 \times 10$.

$Q \approx 1.2 \times 10^2\ \mathrm{J}$

Example 4

The same gas is warmed by 10 K at constant pressure instead. Find the heat needed and the work done.

Show solution
  1. $Q = \tfrac52nR\,\Delta T = 2.5 \times 8.31 \times 10 \approx 208$ J.
  2. $W = P\,\Delta V = nR\,\Delta T = 8.31 \times 10 \approx 83$ J.

$Q \approx 2.1 \times 10^2$ J, $W \approx 83$ J

Example 5

In Examples 3 and 4 the temperature rise is the same. Why does constant pressure need more heat?

Show solution
  1. Same $\Delta T$ means the same $\Delta U$ (125 J) in both cases.
  2. At constant pressure the gas also expands and does work (83 J), which must be paid for with extra heat.

The extra heat (83 J) leaves the gas as work

$Q = \frac52P\,\Delta V$ only works at constant pressure, and $Q = \frac32V\,\Delta P$ only at constant volume. On a sloping line in the P–V graph neither one applies: use $Q = \Delta U + W$ with the area under the line.
On a P–V graph with numbers, you never need $n$ or $T$: replace $nR\,\Delta T$ by $\Delta(PV)$ and read $P$ and $V$ off the axes.
  • Constant volume: $W = 0$, $Q = \Delta U = \tfrac32V\,\Delta P = \tfrac32nR\,\Delta T$.
  • Constant pressure: $W = P\,\Delta V$, $Q = \tfrac52P\,\Delta V = \tfrac52nR\,\Delta T$.
  • Same $\Delta T$ needs more heat at constant pressure, because some becomes work.
