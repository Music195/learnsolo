Heat Engines and Efficiency

An engine takes in heat, turns part of it into work, and throws the rest away

A heat engine (a car engine, a power-station turbine) runs a gas round a cycle: it ends each lap in exactly the state it started, ready to go again.

  • After one full cycle the gas is back at the same temperature, so $\Delta U = 0$. The first law then says the net heat in equals the net work out: $W_{\text{net}} = Q_{\text{in}} - Q_{\text{out}}$.
  • On a P–V graph the net work is the area enclosed by the cycle. Going round clockwise gives positive work: an engine.
  • The efficiency is the fraction of the heat taken in that becomes useful work: $e = W_{\text{net}}/Q_{\text{in}}$.

To find $Q_{\text{in}}$, go round the cycle one step at a time, work out $Q$ for each step, and add up only the positive ones. Don't use the net heat: the heat thrown away ($Q_{\text{out}}$) is never zero for a real engine, so $e$ is always less than 1.

Efficiency of a heat engine

$$e = \frac{W_{\text{net}}}{Q_{\text{in}}} = \frac{Q_{\text{in}} - Q_{\text{out}}}{Q_{\text{in}}} = 1 - \frac{Q_{\text{out}}}{Q_{\text{in}}}$$

$Q_{\text{in}}$: total heat taken in during the steps where $Q > 0$. $Q_{\text{out}}$: total heat given out (as a positive number). $W_{\text{net}}$: area enclosed by the cycle.

  1. Add the first law $Q = \Delta U + W$ over every step of the cycle.
  2. The total $\Delta U$ is zero, because the gas returns to its starting temperature.
  3. So (sum of all $Q$) = (sum of all $W$): $Q_{\text{in}} - Q_{\text{out}} = W_{\text{net}}$.
  4. Divide by $Q_{\text{in}}$: $e = 1 - Q_{\text{out}}/Q_{\text{in}}$.

A rectangular cycle of a monatomic gas: A ($V_0$, $P_0$) → B → C → D → A, with C at $4V_0$

Example 1

In each cycle an engine takes in 500 J of heat and gives out 300 J. Find the work per cycle and the efficiency.

Show solution
  1. $W = Q_{\text{in}} - Q_{\text{out}} = 200$ J.
  2. $e = 200/500$.

$W = 200\ \mathrm{J}$, $e = 0.40$

Example 2

An engine with efficiency 0.25 does 100 J of work per cycle. How much heat does it take in and give out?

Show solution
  1. $Q_{\text{in}} = W/e = 100/0.25 = 400$ J.
  2. $Q_{\text{out}} = Q_{\text{in}} - W = 300$ J.

Takes in 400 J, gives out 300 J

Example 3

A monatomic gas goes round A ($V_0$, $P_0$) → B ($V_0$, $3P_0$) → C ($4V_0$, $3P_0$) → D ($4V_0$, $P_0$) → A. Find the efficiency.

Show solution
  1. A → B (constant $V$): $Q = \tfrac32V_0 \times 2P_0 = 3P_0V_0$ (in).
  2. B → C (constant $P$): $Q = \tfrac52 \times 3P_0 \times 3V_0 = 22.5P_0V_0$ (in).
  3. C → D: $Q = \tfrac32 \times 4V_0 \times (-2P_0) = -12P_0V_0$ (out). D → A: $Q = \tfrac52P_0 \times (-3V_0) = -7.5P_0V_0$ (out).
  4. $Q_{\text{in}} = 25.5P_0V_0$. $W_{\text{net}}$ = rectangle $= 2P_0 \times 3V_0 = 6P_0V_0$ (check: $25.5 - 19.5 = 6$).
  5. $e = 6/25.5 = 4/17$.

$e \approx 0.24$

Example 4

In Example 3, a student computes $e = W_{\text{net}} / (\text{sum of all } Q)$. What do they get, and why is it wrong?

Show solution
  1. The sum of all $Q$ over a cycle equals $W_{\text{net}}$ (because $\Delta U = 0$), so they get $6/6 = 1$.
  2. Efficiency compares the work with the heat you had to supply, which is $Q_{\text{in}}$ only.

They get 1 (100 %), which is impossible; divide by $Q_{\text{in}}$ only

Example 5

Gas in a well-insulated (vacuum-walled) flask is quickly compressed by a piston. Does its temperature stay the same?

Show solution
  1. Insulated: no heat flows, so the change is adiabatic, $Q = 0$.
  2. Work is done on the gas ($W < 0$), so $\Delta U = -W > 0$.

No: the temperature rises. "No heat flow" is not "same temperature".

Keep heat and temperature apart. Heat $Q$ is energy that flows; temperature is a property of the gas. A gas can warm up with no heat at all (adiabatic compression), and can take in heat with no warming (isothermal expansion).
For any cycle problem, make a table with one row per step and columns $\Delta U$, $W$, $Q$. Each column of $\Delta U$ must add up to 0, and the $W$ column must add up to the enclosed area: two free checks on your work.
  • Over a cycle $\Delta U = 0$, so $W_{\text{net}} = Q_{\text{in}} - Q_{\text{out}}$ = enclosed area.
  • $e = W_{\text{net}}/Q_{\text{in}} = 1 - Q_{\text{out}}/Q_{\text{in}}$; $Q_{\text{in}}$ adds only the steps with $Q > 0$.
  • Always less than 1: some heat is always given out.
