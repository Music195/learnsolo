Work Done by a Gas

When a gas pushes a piston out, it does work: the area under the P–V graph

A gas pushes on a piston with force $PS$. If the piston moves out by $\Delta x$, the gas has done work $PS\,\Delta x$. But $S\,\Delta x$ is just the increase in volume, so the work is $P\,\Delta V$.

  • Expansion ($\Delta V > 0$): the gas does positive work on the outside.
  • Compression ($\Delta V < 0$): the work done by the gas is negative; the outside does work on the gas.
  • Constant volume: nothing moves, so no work at all.

If the pressure changes during the expansion, split it into tiny steps: each step is a thin strip of area $P\,\Delta V$ on a P–V graph. Add them up and the work is the area under the graph. Different routes between the same two states give different areas, so different amounts of work.

Work done by the gas

$$W = P\,\Delta V \ \ (\text{constant } P) \qquad W = \text{area under the } P\text{–}V \text{ graph}$$

$W$: work done by the gas (J). $P$: pressure (Pa). $\Delta V$: volume change (m³). Positive for expansion, negative for compression.

  1. The gas pushes the piston (area $S$) with force $F = PS$.
  2. The piston moves out slowly by $\Delta x$: work $W = F\,\Delta x = PS\,\Delta x$.
  3. $S\,\Delta x = \Delta V$, so $W = P\,\Delta V$: a rectangle of height $P$ and width $\Delta V$ on the P–V graph.
  4. If $P$ changes, add up thin rectangles: the total is the area under the curve.

From A (1 L, $3 \times 10^5$ Pa) to B (3 L, $1 \times 10^5$ Pa) along different routes

Example 1

A gas at a constant $2.0 \times 10^5$ Pa expands from $1.0 \times 10^{-3}$ m³ to $3.0 \times 10^{-3}$ m³. Find the work it does.

Show solution
  1. $W = P\,\Delta V = 2.0 \times 10^5 \times 2.0 \times 10^{-3}$.

$W = 4.0 \times 10^2\ \mathrm{J}$

Example 2

A gas at a constant $1.5 \times 10^5$ Pa is compressed from 4.0 L to 2.0 L. Find the work done by the gas, and the work done on it.

Show solution
  1. $\Delta V = -2.0\ \mathrm{L} = -2.0 \times 10^{-3}$ m³.
  2. By the gas: $W = 1.5 \times 10^5 \times (-2.0 \times 10^{-3}) = -300$ J.

By the gas $-300$ J; on the gas $+300$ J

Example 3

On a P–V graph the gas goes in a straight line from (1.0 L, $3.0 \times 10^5$ Pa) to (3.0 L, $1.0 \times 10^5$ Pa). Find the work done by the gas.

Show solution
  1. The area under the line is a trapezium: average height × width.
  2. $W = \dfrac{3.0 + 1.0}{2} \times 10^5 \times 2.0 \times 10^{-3}$.

$W = 4.0 \times 10^2\ \mathrm{J}$

Example 4

A gas in a rigid container is heated and its pressure doubles. How much work does it do?

Show solution
  1. Rigid container: $\Delta V = 0$. On a P–V graph this is a vertical line with no area under it.

$W = 0$

Example 5

A piston of area $5.0 \times 10^{-3}$ m² is pushed out 0.10 m by gas at a constant $1.2 \times 10^5$ Pa. Find the work done by the gas.

Show solution
  1. $\Delta V = S\,\Delta x = 5.0 \times 10^{-3} \times 0.10 = 5.0 \times 10^{-4}$ m³.
  2. $W = P\,\Delta V = 1.2 \times 10^5 \times 5.0 \times 10^{-4}$ (or $F\,\Delta x = 600 \times 0.10$).

$W = 60\ \mathrm{J}$

Unlike $\Delta U$, the work depends on the route. From A to B, "right then down" gives the biggest area and "down then right" the smallest, even though the start and end are the same.
$1\ \mathrm{L} \times 10^5\ \mathrm{Pa} = 10^{-3}\ \mathrm{m^3} \times 10^5\ \mathrm{Pa} = 100$ J. So on a graph in litres and $10^5$ Pa, each square of area is 100 J.
  • Work by a gas: $W = P\,\Delta V$ at constant pressure; in general the area under the P–V graph.
  • Expansion: $W > 0$. Compression: $W < 0$. Constant volume: $W = 0$.
  • The work depends on the route, not just the start and end states.
