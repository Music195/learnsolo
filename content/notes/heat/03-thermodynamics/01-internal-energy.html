Internal Energy

A gas stores energy in the motion of its molecules

The internal energy $U$ of a gas is the total energy of all its molecules. For an ideal gas, the molecules don't pull on each other, so there's no stored "position" energy between them: $U$ is just the sum of their kinetic energies.

  • For a monatomic gas (single atoms, like helium or argon), each molecule has on average $\tfrac32kT$ of kinetic energy (from kinetic theory).
  • $N$ molecules: $U = N \times \tfrac32kT = \tfrac32nRT$, since $Nk = nN_Ak = nR$.
  • So for a fixed amount of ideal gas, $U$ depends only on $T$. Same temperature, same internal energy, however you got there.

This is why thermodynamics problems keep asking "did the temperature go up or down?": that alone tells you whether $U$ went up or down.

Monatomic ideal gas

$$U = \frac32nRT = \frac32PV \qquad \Delta U = \frac32nR\,\Delta T$$

$n$: moles. $T$: temperature (K). $\frac32PV$ comes from $PV = nRT$. For a gas of two-atom molecules (like N₂ or O₂) the molecules also spin, and $U = \frac52nRT$.

  1. Kinetic theory: one molecule has average kinetic energy $\tfrac12m\overline{v^2} = \tfrac32kT$.
  2. $N = nN_A$ molecules: $U = nN_A \times \tfrac32kT$.
  3. $N_Ak = R$, so $U = \tfrac32nRT$.
  4. A change in temperature $\Delta T$ changes $U$ by $\Delta U = \tfrac32nR\,\Delta T$; it depends only on the start and end temperatures, not on the route.

Internal energy of a monatomic gas

Example 1

Find the internal energy of 2.0 mol of helium at 300 K.

Show solution
  1. $U = \tfrac32nRT = 1.5 \times 2.0 \times 8.31 \times 300$.

$U \approx 7.5 \times 10^3\ \mathrm{J}$

Example 2

1.0 mol of a monatomic gas is heated from 300 K to 400 K. Find $\Delta U$.

Show solution
  1. $\Delta U = \tfrac32nR\,\Delta T = 1.5 \times 1.0 \times 8.31 \times 100$.

$\Delta U \approx 1.2 \times 10^3\ \mathrm{J}$

Example 3

A monatomic gas is at $1.0 \times 10^5$ Pa in $2.0 \times 10^{-2}$ m³. Find its internal energy without knowing $n$ or $T$.

Show solution
  1. $U = \tfrac32nRT = \tfrac32PV = 1.5 \times 1.0 \times 10^5 \times 2.0 \times 10^{-2}$.

$U = 3.0 \times 10^3\ \mathrm{J}$

Example 4

A gas goes from state A (300 K) to state B (500 K) along two different routes. Compare $\Delta U$ for the two routes.

Show solution
  1. $U$ depends only on $T$ (for a fixed amount of ideal gas).
  2. Both routes start at 300 K and end at 500 K.

$\Delta U$ is the same for both routes

Example 5

A gas expands from $V$ to $3V$ while its pressure falls from $3P$ to $P$. What happens to its internal energy?

Show solution
  1. $PV$ at the start: $3P \times V = 3PV$. At the end: $P \times 3V = 3PV$.
  2. $PV$ is unchanged, so $T$ is unchanged, so $U = \tfrac32PV$ is unchanged.

$\Delta U = 0$: the start and end temperatures are equal

$U$ depends on temperature, not on pressure or volume alone. A gas whose volume grows can have more, less or the same internal energy: check what happened to $T$ (or to $PV$).
When a problem gives pressures and volumes but no temperatures, use $\Delta U = \tfrac32\Delta(PV) = \tfrac32(P_2V_2 - P_1V_1)$.
  • Internal energy of an ideal gas = total kinetic energy of its molecules.
  • Monatomic: $U = \tfrac32nRT = \tfrac32PV$.
  • $\Delta U$ depends only on the change in $T$, never on the route.
