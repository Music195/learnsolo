Specific Heat and Heat Capacity

Some things need a lot of heat to warm up, others very little

Give the same amount of heat to 100 g of water and to 100 g of iron: the iron gets much hotter. Each substance has its own "how hard is it to warm up" number.

  • Heat is energy that flows because of a temperature difference. Its unit is the joule (J). Temperature (K or °C) is something else: a measure of how hot something is.
  • Specific heat $c$: the heat needed to raise 1 g of a substance by 1 K. Unit: J/(g·K). Water is about $4.2$, iron about $0.45$.
  • Heat capacity $C$: the heat needed to raise a whole object by 1 K. Unit: J/K.

Why have both? Specific heat belongs to a pure substance. But a real object, like a pot made of an alloy, with a lid, a thermometer stuck in it and some foam wrapped round it, isn't one substance. Its heat capacity, "J per K for the whole thing", is easy to measure once and then use in every calculation.

Note the unit: in heat problems, masses are usually in grams, like in chemistry, not kilograms.

Heat needed to raise the temperature by $\Delta T$

$$Q = mc\,\Delta T \qquad Q = C\,\Delta T \qquad C = mc$$

$Q$: heat (J). $m$: mass (g). $c$: specific heat (J/(g·K)). $C$: heat capacity (J/K). $\Delta T$: temperature change; a change of 1 K is the same as a change of 1 °C, so either can be used for $\Delta T$.

  1. By definition, 1 g needs $c$ joules for each kelvin.
  2. $m$ grams need $m$ times as much: $mc$ joules per kelvin.
  3. For $\Delta T$ kelvins: $Q = mc\,\Delta T$.
  4. "$mc$" is the heat per kelvin for the whole object: that is its heat capacity, $C = mc$. For an object made of several parts, add their heat capacities: $C = m_1c_1 + m_2c_2 + \cdots$.

Give the same heat to water and to a metal

Example 1

How much heat raises 200 g of water from 20 °C to 50 °C? ($c = 4.2$ J/(g·K))

Show solution
  1. $Q = mc\,\Delta T = 200 \times 4.2 \times 30$.

$Q = 2.52 \times 10^4\ \mathrm{J} \approx 2.5 \times 10^4\ \mathrm{J}$

Example 2

The same heat as in Example 1 goes into 200 g of iron ($c = 0.45$ J/(g·K)). How much does its temperature rise?

Show solution
  1. $\Delta T = \dfrac{Q}{mc} = \dfrac{25200}{200 \times 0.45}$.

$\Delta T = 280\ \mathrm{K}$: over 9 times as much as the water

Example 3

A pot has a heat capacity of 150 J/K. It holds 500 g of water. How much heat raises the pot and water together by 10 K?

Show solution
  1. Total heat capacity: $C = 150 + 500 \times 4.2 = 2250\ \mathrm{J/K}$.
  2. $Q = C\,\Delta T = 2250 \times 10$.

$Q = 2.25 \times 10^4\ \mathrm{J}$

Example 4

An object warms by 8.0 K when it receives 1200 J. Find its heat capacity.

Show solution
  1. $C = Q/\Delta T = 1200/8.0$.

$C = 150\ \mathrm{J/K}$

Specific heat here is per gram. Everywhere else in physics, masses are in kilograms, so check the unit of $c$ before putting a mass in. If $c$ is given in J/(kg·K), the mass must be in kg.
For a change in temperature, kelvin and °C are the same size: from −10 °C to 0 °C is a rise of 10 K. Only for an actual temperature (not a change) do you need $T\,[\mathrm{K}] = t\,[^\circ\mathrm{C}] + 273$.
  • Specific heat $c$: heat per gram per kelvin. Heat capacity $C$: heat per kelvin for a whole object.
  • $Q = mc\,\Delta T = C\,\Delta T$, with $C = mc$ (add the parts for a mixed object).
  • Heat is energy (J); temperature is how hot (K).
