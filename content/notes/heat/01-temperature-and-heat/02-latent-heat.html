Latent Heat

While ice melts, heat goes in but the temperature doesn't move

Heat a block of ice from −10 °C. Its temperature climbs to 0 °C and then stops. You keep heating, yet the thermometer stays at 0 °C until every bit of ice has melted. Only then does the water start warming up.

  • During melting, the heat is used to pull the molecules out of their fixed places in the solid, not to make them move faster. So the temperature stays the same.
  • The heat needed to change the state of 1 g without changing its temperature is the latent heat $L$ ("latent" means hidden: it doesn't show on the thermometer).
  • For water: melting (fusion) $L = 336$ J/g at 0 °C; boiling (vaporisation) $L \approx 2260$ J/g at 100 °C.

So a "from ice to warm water" problem has three stages: warm the ice, melt it, warm the water. Work out each one and add them up.

Heat to change the state of mass $m$

$$Q = mL$$

$Q$: heat (J). $m$: mass (g). $L$: latent heat (J/g). During the change of state $\Delta T = 0$, so $Q = mc\,\Delta T$ can't be used for this stage.

  1. Warm the ice from $t_1$ to 0 °C: $Q_1 = m\,c_{\text{ice}}\,(0 - t_1)$.
  2. Melt it at 0 °C: $Q_2 = mL$.
  3. Warm the water from 0 °C to $t_2$: $Q_3 = m\,c_{\text{water}}\,(t_2 - 0)$.
  4. Total: $Q = Q_1 + Q_2 + Q_3$. Ice and water have different specific heats ($2.1$ and $4.2$ J/(g·K)), so the two warming stages must use different $c$.

Heat ice from −10 °C with a 500 W heater

Example 1

How much heat turns 100 g of ice at −10 °C into water at 40 °C? ($c_{\text{ice}} = 2.1$, $c_{\text{water}} = 4.2$ J/(g·K), $L = 336$ J/g.) Give 2 significant figures.

Show solution
  1. Warm the ice: $100 \times 2.1 \times 10 = 2100$ J.
  2. Melt it: $100 \times 336 = 33600$ J.
  3. Warm the water: $100 \times 4.2 \times 40 = 16800$ J.
  4. Total: $52500$ J $= 5.25 \times 10^4$ J.

$Q \approx 5.3 \times 10^4\ \mathrm{J}$

Example 2

How much heat melts 50 g of ice that is already at 0 °C?

Show solution
  1. Only the melting stage: $Q = mL = 50 \times 336$.

$Q = 16800\ \mathrm{J} \approx 1.7 \times 10^4\ \mathrm{J}$

Example 3

A 420 W heater is put into 100 g of ice at 0 °C. How long does the ice take to melt, and how long after that until the water reaches 100 °C? (All the heat goes into the ice/water.)

Show solution
  1. Power is heat per second, so time = heat ÷ power.
  2. Melting: $\dfrac{100 \times 336}{420} = 80$ s.
  3. Warming: $\dfrac{100 \times 4.2 \times 100}{420} = 100$ s.

80 s to melt, then 100 s more to reach 100 °C

Example 4

How much heat turns 10 g of water at 100 °C into steam at 100 °C? ($L = 2260$ J/g)

Show solution
  1. $Q = mL = 10 \times 2260 = 22600$ J.
  2. Compare: warming those 10 g all the way from 0 °C to 100 °C needs only $10 \times 4.2 \times 100 = 4200$ J.

$Q \approx 2.3 \times 10^4\ \mathrm{J}$, over 5 times the heat to warm it from 0 °C

Example 5

Write 52500 J and 0.00336 J in scientific notation with 2 significant figures.

Show solution
  1. The number in front must be at least 1 and less than 10.
  2. $52500 = 5.25 \times 10^4 \approx 5.3 \times 10^4$ (not $53 \times 10^3$).
  3. $0.00336 = 3.36 \times 10^{-3} \approx 3.4 \times 10^{-3}$.

$5.3 \times 10^4$ J and $3.4 \times 10^{-3}$ J

Don't use $Q = mc\,\Delta T$ across 0 °C in one go, as in $100 \times 4.2 \times 50$ for "−10 °C to 40 °C". It misses the melting stage, which is usually the biggest part, and uses the wrong $c$ for the ice.
On a temperature–time graph with a steady heater, a flat part means a change of state. Its length tells you $mL$, and the slope of each sloping part tells you $mc$: a gentler slope means a bigger heat capacity.
  • During melting or boiling the temperature stays the same; the heat needed is $Q = mL$.
  • Ice to warm water: warm the ice + melt + warm the water, each with its own formula.
  • Scientific notation: the front number is at least 1 and less than 10.
