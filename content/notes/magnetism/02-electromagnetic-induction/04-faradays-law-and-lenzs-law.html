Faraday's Law and Lenz's Law

The emf equals the rate of change of flux; its direction always fights the change

Magnetic flux. $B$ tells you how much field passes through each square metre. The flux $\Phi$ is the total through an area: $\Phi = BS$ (with $B$ at right angles to the area $S$). Picture $B$ as the density of field lines and $\Phi$ as the whole bundle. Flux is measured in webers (Wb).

Faraday's law. When the flux through a coil changes, an emf appears. Its size is the number of turns times the rate of change of flux:

$$V = N\left|\frac{\Delta\Phi}{\Delta t}\right|.$$

Textbooks often write a minus sign here. It does not mean the answer is negative; it only says "opposite direction". It is clearer to work out the size from the formula and the direction from Lenz's law.

Lenz's law. The induced emf always tries to drive a current that opposes the change in flux. If the flux increases, the coil makes a field the other way to reduce it. If the flux decreases, the coil makes a field the same way to top it up. A coil hates change.

Using it.

  1. Which way does the flux point, and is it increasing or decreasing?
  2. The coil wants a field that fights this change. Point your right thumb along that field; your fingers give the current direction round the coil.
  3. Draw that coil as a battery driving this current. Its + terminal is where the current comes out.

Faraday's law also covers moving rods: a rod on rails sweeps out area, which changes the flux of the circuit. When $B$ itself changes with time, $vBL$ cannot be used, and Faraday's law is the only way.

Flux and Faraday's law

$$\Phi = BS, \qquad V = N\left|\frac{\Delta\Phi}{\Delta t}\right| \quad(\text{direction: Lenz's law})$$

$\Phi$: flux (Wb). $B$: flux density (T = Wb/m²). $S$: area of the coil (m²). $N$: number of turns. $\Delta\Phi/\Delta t$: rate of change of flux (Wb/s = V); on a graph of $\Phi$ (or $B$) against $t$, it is the slope.

  1. A rod of length $L$ on rails is a distance $x$ from the end of the circuit. The flux through the circuit is $\Phi = BLx$.
  2. Only $x$ changes, and $\Delta x/\Delta t = v$. So $V = 1 \times BL\,\dfrac{\Delta x}{\Delta t} = vBL$, the same as the moving-rod formula.
  3. Moving right, the area grows, so the flux grows. Lenz: the circuit drives a current that makes the opposite field. This gives the same + end as the hand rule.

Example 1

A uniform field of 0.20 T passes at right angles through a loop of area $5.0\times10^{-3}$ m². Find the flux.

Show solution
  1. $\Phi = BS = 0.20 \times 5.0\times10^{-3}$.

$1.0\times10^{-3}$ Wb

Example 2

A 200-turn coil of area $4.0\times10^{-3}$ m² sits in a field along its axis, pointing up. The field rises steadily from 0.10 T to 0.30 T in 0.50 s. Find the emf, and the direction of the current seen from above.

Show solution
  1. $\dfrac{\Delta\Phi}{\Delta t} = S\dfrac{\Delta B}{\Delta t} = 4.0\times10^{-3} \times \dfrac{0.20}{0.50} = 1.6\times10^{-3}$ Wb/s.
  2. $V = 200 \times 1.6\times10^{-3} = 0.32$ V.
  3. The upward flux increases, so the coil makes a downward field. Thumb down: the fingers curl clockwise seen from above.

0.32 V; clockwise seen from above

Example 3

In general, the field through an $N$-turn coil of area $S$ grows steadily from $B_0$ to $B_1$ in time $t_0$. Find the size of the emf.

Show solution
  1. The graph of $B$ against $t$ is a straight line, so its slope is $\dfrac{B_1 - B_0}{t_0}$.
  2. $V = N S \dfrac{B_1 - B_0}{t_0}$. (Constant terms like $B_0$ disappear when you take the rate of change.)

$V = \dfrac{NS(B_1 - B_0)}{t_0}$

Example 4

A magnet's N pole is pushed down towards a coil from above. Which way does the current flow, seen from above, and what does the coil do to the magnet?

Show solution
  1. Field lines leave the N pole, so the flux through the coil points down and increases.
  2. The coil makes an upward field: thumb up, so anticlockwise seen from above.
  3. An upward field makes the coil's top face a north pole. North faces north, so it pushes the magnet away, fighting the change.

Anticlockwise from above; it repels the magnet

Example 5

The same N pole is now pulled away upwards. Which way does the current flow, and what does the coil do to the magnet?

Show solution
  1. The downward flux decreases, so the coil makes a downward field to top it up.
  2. Thumb down: clockwise seen from above. The coil's top face is now a south pole, which attracts the N pole and tries to hold it back.

Clockwise from above; it attracts the magnet

Don't just copy the minus sign from $V = -N\Delta\Phi/\Delta t$ into your answer. If the question asks for "the potential of Q relative to P", the sign depends on which end the coil makes positive. Find that with Lenz's law; it can come out either way.
A rod moving in a steady field: $vBL$ is quickest. A field that changes with time (often given as a graph): use $V = NS\,\Delta B/\Delta t$, with the slope of the graph.
  • $\Phi = BS$; $V = N|\Delta\Phi/\Delta t|$.
  • Lenz: the induced current fights the change in flux.
  • Increasing flux: field the other way. Decreasing: field the same way.
