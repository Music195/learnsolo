Self and Mutual Induction

A changing current makes an emf in its own coil ($L\,\Delta I/\Delta t$) and in a neighbouring coil ($M\,\Delta I/\Delta t$)

Wind two coils on the same core: a primary coil ($N_1$ turns, length $l$, cross-section $S$) and a secondary coil ($N_2$ turns). Follow the formulas you already know, one after another:

  1. field inside the primary: $H = \dfrac{N_1}{l}I$ (turns per metre × current);
  2. flux density: $B = \mu H = \mu\dfrac{N_1}{l}I$;
  3. flux through each turn: $\Phi = BS = \mu\dfrac{N_1S}{l}I$.

If $I$ changes, this flux changes, and by Lenz's law both coils get an emf that fights the change.

  • In the primary itself, this is self-induction: $V_1 = N_1\dfrac{\Delta\Phi}{\Delta t} = \mu\dfrac{N_1^2S}{l}\dfrac{\Delta I}{\Delta t}$. Everything in front of $\Delta I/\Delta t$ is fixed by the coil's shape, so it is collected into one constant, the self-inductance $L$.
  • In the secondary, it is mutual induction: $V_2 = N_2\dfrac{\Delta\Phi}{\Delta t} = \mu\dfrac{N_1N_2S}{l}\dfrac{\Delta I}{\Delta t}$. The constant is the mutual inductance $M$.

Both are measured in henries (H). An emf appears only while the current is changing. A steady current, however large, gives none. This is why a meter needle only flicks while you push a magnet in or pull it out.

Self- and mutual inductance

$$V_1 = L\frac{\Delta I}{\Delta t}, \quad L = \mu\frac{N_1^2S}{l} \qquad\qquad V_2 = M\frac{\Delta I_1}{\Delta t}, \quad M = \mu\frac{N_1N_2S}{l}$$

$\Delta I/\Delta t$: rate of change of the primary current (A/s), the slope of its $I$–$t$ graph. $L$, $M$ in henries (H = V·s/A). Directions: by Lenz's law, the emf opposes the change.

  1. Faraday: $V_1 = N_1\dfrac{\Delta\Phi}{\Delta t}$ with $\Phi = \mu\dfrac{N_1S}{l}I$.
  2. $\mu$, $N_1$, $S$ and $l$ do not change while the current changes. Only $I$ varies, so the rest can be taken outside: $V_1 = \mu\dfrac{N_1^2S}{l}\dfrac{\Delta I}{\Delta t}$.
  3. The secondary has the same flux through each of its $N_2$ turns, so $V_2 = N_2\dfrac{\Delta\Phi}{\Delta t}$, which gives $M$. (If the two coils have different areas, use the area the shared flux actually passes through.)

Drag A and B to shape the primary current. M = 4.0 H; graph below: potential of Q relative to P

Example 1

An air-cored solenoid has 500 turns, length 0.25 m and cross-section $4.0\times10^{-4}$ m². Find its self-inductance. ($\mu_0 = 4\pi\times10^{-7}$ N/A²)

Show solution
  1. $L = \mu_0\dfrac{N^2S}{l} = 4\pi\times10^{-7} \times \dfrac{500^2 \times 4.0\times10^{-4}}{0.25}$.
  2. $= 4\pi\times10^{-7} \times 400 = 5.0\times10^{-4}$ H.

$5.0\times10^{-4}$ H

Example 2

A second coil of 100 turns is wound over the solenoid in Example 1. Find the mutual inductance.

Show solution
  1. $M = \mu_0\dfrac{N_1N_2S}{l}$. Compared with $L$, one $N_1$ is replaced by $N_2$, so $M = L\times\dfrac{N_2}{N_1}$.
  2. $M = 5.0\times10^{-4} \times \dfrac{100}{500} = 1.0\times10^{-4}$ H.

$1.0\times10^{-4}$ H

Example 3

A current of 3.0 A in a 0.20 H coil is switched off and falls to zero in 0.010 s. Find the self-induced emf.

Show solution
  1. $V = L\dfrac{\Delta I}{\Delta t} = 0.20 \times \dfrac{3.0}{0.010}$.
  2. $= 60$ V, from a much smaller supply. This is why switches on coils can spark.

60 V

Example 4

With $M = 4.0$ H, the primary current rises steadily from 0 to 2.0 A in 3.0 s, stays at 2.0 A for 2.0 s, then falls to 0 in 2.0 s. Find the size of the secondary emf in each stage.

Show solution
  1. Slopes: $2.0/3.0 = 0.67$ A/s, then 0, then $2.0/2.0 = 1.0$ A/s.
  2. $V_2 = M \times$ slope: $4.0 \times 0.67 = 2.7$ V, then 0, then 4.0 V.

2.7 V, 0, 4.0 V

Example 5

In Example 4, the secondary's terminal P is the higher one while the primary current is rising. Give the potential of Q relative to P in each stage.

Show solution
  1. Rising: P is higher, so Q is lower: $-2.7$ V.
  2. Steady: no change, no emf: 0.
  3. Falling: the flux decreases instead of increasing, so Lenz's law reverses the emf: $+4.0$ V.

$-2.7$ V, 0, $+4.0$ V (the demo's default graph)

The emf depends on how fast the current changes, not on how big it is. A large steady current gives no emf. A small current switched off suddenly can give a large one.
With an $I$–$t$ graph, just read off the slopes. Each straight piece gives a constant emf: $M$ (or $L$) times the slope. The sign flips between rising and falling pieces.
  • Self: $V = L\,\Delta I/\Delta t$, $L = \mu N^2S/l$.
  • Mutual: $V_2 = M\,\Delta I_1/\Delta t$, $M = \mu N_1N_2S/l$.
  • No change in current, no emf; the emf always opposes the change.
