Transients in RC and RL Circuits

Just after switching, a capacitor acts as a wire and a coil as a break; long after, the other way round

When a switch is closed or opened, capacitors and coils take time to settle. This settling is a transient. Most problems only ask about two moments, and each part has a simple rule for each.

Capacitor ($Q = CV$):

  • Just after closing, an uncharged capacitor has $Q = 0$, so $V = 0$. It acts like a wire, and current rushes in.
  • Long after, it is full and no more charge flows in. It acts like a break, and any resistor in series with it has no voltage across it.
  • So the current comes first and the voltage builds up later.

Coil ($V = L\,\Delta I/\Delta t$): a coil hates change in its current.

  • Just after closing, its current was 0 and it tries to keep it 0. It makes a back emf that stops the current, so it acts like a break.
  • Long after, the current is steady, so there is no emf. It is just coiled wire: a wire.
  • Just after opening, it tries to keep its current flowing. It drives the same current round whatever path is left, even if that needs a large emf.
  • So the voltage comes first and the current builds up later.

In between, the change is gradual. Currents and voltages approach their final values along curves that start steep and flatten out. The time scale is $RC$ for a capacitor and $L/R$ for a coil.

The two moments

$$\begin{array}{lcc} & \text{just after closing} & \text{long after} \\ \text{capacitor (uncharged)} & \text{wire } (V = 0) & \text{break } (I = 0) \\ \text{coil} & \text{break } (I \text{ unchanged}) & \text{wire } (V = 0) \end{array}$$

Just after a switch moves, a capacitor keeps its voltage (its charge cannot jump) and a coil keeps its current. After opening, a charged capacitor acts like a battery of its voltage; a coil acts like a source of its current.

  1. Capacitor: $V = Q/C$, and $Q$ can only change by current flowing for some time. So $V$ cannot jump. Starting at $Q = 0$ means $V = 0$, like a wire.
  2. When it is full, $V$ stops changing, so no more charge flows: $I = 0$, like a break.
  3. Coil: a sudden jump in $I$ would need $\Delta I/\Delta t \to \infty$, which means an infinite emf. So $I$ cannot jump; it keeps its old value for an instant.
  4. When the current is steady, $\Delta I/\Delta t = 0$, so $V = 0$, like a wire.

12 V cell, switch, R₁ = 4 Ω, then R₂ = 8 Ω in parallel with a part. Closed at t = 0, opened at t = 3 s

Example 1

A 12 V cell, a switch and $R_1 = 4.0$ Ω are in series with $R_2 = 8.0$ Ω, and an uncharged capacitor is connected across $R_2$. Find the current through $R_1$ just after the switch closes.

Show solution
  1. The capacitor acts as a wire, so both ends of $R_2$ are at the same potential and $R_2$ carries no current.
  2. All the current goes through $R_1$ and into the capacitor: $I = 12/4.0 = 3.0$ A.

3.0 A

Example 2

Find the current, and the capacitor's voltage, a long time later.

Show solution
  1. The capacitor acts as a break: $I = 12/(4.0 + 8.0) = 1.0$ A through $R_1$ and $R_2$.
  2. The capacitor has the same voltage as $R_2$: $8.0 \times 1.0 = 8.0$ V.

1.0 A; 8.0 V

Example 3

The switch is then opened. Find the current through $R_2$ just afterwards.

Show solution
  1. The cell is cut off. The capacitor still has 8.0 V and acts as a battery, discharging round the loop through $R_2$.
  2. $I = 8.0/8.0 = 1.0$ A. (In general $\dfrac{R_2}{R_1 + R_2}V \div R_2 = \dfrac{V}{R_1 + R_2}$.)

1.0 A

Example 4

The capacitor is replaced by a coil of negligible resistance. Find the current through $R_1$ just after closing, and a long time later.

Show solution
  1. Just after: the coil acts as a break, so the current goes through $R_1$ and $R_2$: $12/12 = 1.0$ A.
  2. Long after: the coil acts as a wire, which shorts out $R_2$: $12/4.0 = 3.0$ A, all through the coil.

1.0 A, then 3.0 A

Example 5

Now the switch is opened. Find the current through $R_2$ just afterwards, and the coil's emf.

Show solution
  1. The coil keeps its 3.0 A flowing, and the only path left is round through $R_2$. So $R_2$ carries 3.0 A, in the opposite direction to before.
  2. The coil's emf is $R_2 I = 8.0 \times 3.0 = 24$ V: twice the cell's voltage.

3.0 A; 24 V

The rules swap between the two parts. A capacitor is a wire at first and a break later; a coil is a break at first and a wire later. Mixing them up is the most common mistake.
Replace each capacitor or coil by a wire or a break for the moment asked, then colour the wires by potential. A resistor with the same colour at both ends carries no current.
  • Capacitor: keeps its voltage; wire at first, break later.
  • Coil: keeps its current; break at first, wire later.
  • Opening the switch: a coil drives its old current through what is left.
