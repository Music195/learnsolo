Impedance and Average Power

In series, the voltages add as arrows, giving $Z = \sqrt{R^2 + (\omega L - 1/\omega C)^2}$; only the resistor uses power on average

Impedance is the combined "resistance" of a whole AC circuit. Find it the same way as the combined resistance of resistors in series:

  1. the same current flows through every part, so take it as $I = I_0\sin\omega t$;
  2. the supply voltage is the sum of the parts' voltages (Kirchhoff's second law);
  3. take out the common factor $I_0$; whatever is left in front is the combined resistance.

The twist is that the voltages are not in step. $V_R$ is in step with $I$, $V_L$ is a quarter cycle ahead and $V_C$ a quarter cycle behind. So they add like arrows at right angles, not like ordinary numbers: $V_L$ and $V_C$ point opposite ways and partly cancel, and the result is combined with $V_R$ by Pythagoras.

Average power. The power in a resistor, $RI^2$, goes up and down twice per cycle, but it is never negative. Its average is half the peak: $\tfrac12RI_0^2$. A capacitor or a coil takes energy in during one quarter cycle and gives it all back in the next, so its average power is zero. However complicated the circuit, only the resistors use power.

Root-mean-square (rms) values. Define $I_e = I_0/\sqrt2$ and $V_e = V_0/\sqrt2$. Then the average power is $RI_e^2$, the same formula as for DC. Meters and mains ratings use these values: "100 V mains" means $V_e = 100$ V, so the peak is about 141 V.

Series R, L, C with current $I = I_0\sin\omega t$

$$Z = \sqrt{R^2 + \left(\omega L - \frac{1}{\omega C}\right)^2}, \quad V_0 = ZI_0, \qquad \overline{P} = \tfrac12RI_0^2 = RI_e^2, \quad I_e = \frac{I_0}{\sqrt2}$$

$Z$: impedance (Ω). $V_0$, $I_0$: peak values. $\overline{P}$: average power (W), used only in $R$. $I_e$, $V_e$: rms values (peak ÷ √2).

  1. With $I = I_0\sin\omega t$: $V_R = RI_0\sin\omega t$, $V_L = \omega LI_0\cos\omega t$, $V_C = -\dfrac{I_0}{\omega C}\cos\omega t$.
  2. Add: $V = I_0\left[R\sin\omega t + \left(\omega L - \dfrac1{\omega C}\right)\cos\omega t\right]$.
  3. Combining sin and cos: $a\sin\theta + b\cos\theta = \sqrt{a^2 + b^2}\,\sin(\theta + \alpha)$. So $V = I_0\sqrt{R^2 + (\omega L - 1/\omega C)^2}\,\sin(\omega t + \alpha)$, which gives $Z$.
  4. Power in $R$: $RI_0^2\sin^2\omega t = \tfrac12RI_0^2(1 - \cos2\omega t)$. The cosine part averages to zero, which leaves $\tfrac12RI_0^2$.
  5. Power in $C$: $V_CI = -\dfrac{I_0^2}{\omega C}\sin\omega t\cos\omega t = -\dfrac{I_0^2}{2\omega C}\sin2\omega t$, which averages to zero. The coil is the same with the opposite sign.

R = 40 Ω, L = 0.20 H, C = 50 μF in series, peak current 0.30 A. Left: voltage arrows at t = 0. Right: power

Example 1

In a series circuit, $R = 30$ Ω, $\omega L = 60$ Ω and $1/(\omega C) = 20$ Ω. Find the impedance.

Show solution
  1. $Z = \sqrt{30^2 + (60 - 20)^2} = \sqrt{900 + 1600} = \sqrt{2500}$.

50 Ω

Example 2

The peak current in Example 1 is 2.0 A. Find the peak voltage across each part and across the whole circuit.

Show solution
  1. $V_R = 60$ V, $V_L = 120$ V, $V_C = 40$ V.
  2. Whole circuit: $V_0 = ZI_0 = 100$ V. Check with arrows: $\sqrt{60^2 + (120 - 40)^2} = 100$ ✓.
  3. The parts' peaks do not simply add ($60 + 120 + 40 = 220$), because they happen at different moments.

60 V, 120 V, 40 V; whole circuit 100 V

Example 3

Find the average power used in the circuit of Example 2.

Show solution
  1. Only $R$ counts: $\overline P = \tfrac12RI_0^2 = \tfrac12 \times 30 \times 2.0^2 = 60$ W.
  2. Check with rms: $I_e = 2.0/\sqrt2 = 1.41$ A, so $RI_e^2 = 30 \times 2.0 = 60$ W ✓.

60 W

Example 4

A household supply is rated 100 V (rms). What is its peak voltage?

Show solution
  1. $V_0 = \sqrt2 \times V_e = 1.41 \times 100$.

About 141 V

Example 5

At what frequency is the impedance of the demo's circuit ($R = 40$ Ω, $L = 0.20$ H, $C = 50$ μF) smallest, and what is it then?

Show solution
  1. $Z$ is smallest when $\omega L = 1/(\omega C)$: the coil and capacitor voltages cancel exactly.
  2. $\omega = \dfrac1{\sqrt{LC}} = \dfrac1{\sqrt{0.20 \times 50\times10^{-6}}} = 316$ rad/s, so $f = \omega/2\pi = 50$ Hz.
  3. Then $Z = R = 40$ Ω. (This is called resonance. Move the slider to 50 Hz.)

About 50 Hz; $Z = 40$ Ω

Never add the peak voltages of R, L and C as plain numbers. They peak at different moments; add them as arrows at right angles.
For average power, ignore every capacitor and coil and use $\tfrac12RI_0^2$ for each resistor. This saves a lot of algebra.
  • $Z = \sqrt{R^2 + (\omega L - 1/\omega C)^2}$, $V_0 = ZI_0$.
  • Average power $\tfrac12RI_0^2$; zero for C and L.
  • rms = peak ÷ √2; mains ratings are rms.
