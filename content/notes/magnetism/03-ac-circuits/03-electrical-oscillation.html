Electrical Oscillation

A coil and a capacitor pass energy back and forth, like a mass on a spring, with period $T = 2\pi\sqrt{LC}$

Join a coil $L$ and a charged capacitor $C$ (or start a current in the coil). The capacitor pushes current through the coil; the coil will not let the current change suddenly, so it keeps the current going after the capacitor is empty and charges it up the other way round. Then the whole thing runs backwards. The current and the capacitor voltage swing back and forth for ever (if there is no resistance). This is electrical oscillation.

It is the same mathematics as a mass on a spring:

  • charge $q$ on the capacitor ↔ stretch $x$ of the spring;
  • current $I$ ↔ speed $v$;
  • coil $L$ ↔ mass $m$ (both resist changes: in current, in speed);
  • $1/C$ ↔ spring constant $k$ (a small capacitor is a stiff spring).

So the spring's $T = 2\pi\sqrt{m/k}$ becomes $T = 2\pi\sqrt{LC}$, and its energy $\tfrac12mv^2 + \tfrac12kx^2$ becomes $\tfrac12LI^2 + \dfrac{q^2}{2C}$. That total stays the same; it just moves between the coil (magnetic energy) and the capacitor (electric energy).

A typical set-up. A battery $V$ and resistor $R$ feed a coil, with a capacitor connected across the coil. After switch $S_1$ has been closed for a long time, the coil acts as a plain wire: the current through it is $I_0 = V/R$ and the capacitor has no voltage. Opening $S_1$ leaves the coil and capacitor alone together, starting with current $I_0$ and charge 0, which is like a spring passing its middle at top speed.

Coil and capacitor alone (no resistance)

$$T = 2\pi\sqrt{LC}, \quad \omega = \frac{1}{\sqrt{LC}}, \qquad \tfrac12LI^2 + \frac{q^2}{2C} = \text{constant}$$

$L$: self-inductance (H). $C$: capacitance (F). $I$: current in the coil (A). $q$: charge on the capacitor (C). Starting from $I_0$, $q = 0$: $I = I_0\cos\omega t$ and the largest capacitor voltage is $I_0\sqrt{L/C}$.

  1. The coil and capacitor share the same two ends, so their voltages are equal. Take $q$ as the charge on the plate the current flows into, so $I = \dfrac{dq}{dt}$.
  2. Going round the loop: $L\dfrac{dI}{dt} + \dfrac{q}{C} = 0$, so $\dfrac{d^2q}{dt^2} = -\dfrac{1}{LC}\,q$.
  3. Compare a spring: $\dfrac{d^2x}{dt^2} = -\dfrac{k}{m}x$. This is simple harmonic motion with $\omega^2 = \dfrac{1}{LC}$, so $T = \dfrac{2\pi}{\omega} = 2\pi\sqrt{LC}$.
  4. Multiply step 2 by $I = dq/dt$: $LI\dfrac{dI}{dt} + \dfrac{q}{C}\dfrac{dq}{dt} = 0$, which is $\dfrac{d}{dt}\left(\tfrac12LI^2 + \dfrac{q^2}{2C}\right) = 0$. The total energy does not change.

V = 6 V, R = 3 Ω, L = 20 mH, capacitor across the coil. S₁ is opened at t = 0 (slowed right down)

Example 1

A coil of 20 mH is joined to a capacitor of 50 μF. Find the period of the oscillation.

Show solution
  1. $LC = 0.020 \times 50\times10^{-6} = 1.0\times10^{-6}$, so $\sqrt{LC} = 1.0\times10^{-3}$ s.
  2. $T = 2\pi \times 1.0\times10^{-3}$.

$6.3\times10^{-3}$ s (about 160 oscillations per second)

Example 2

In the demo's circuit ($V = 6$ V, $R = 3$ Ω, the coil with the capacitor across it), $S_1$ has been closed for a long time. Find the current through the coil and the charge on the capacitor.

Show solution
  1. Once nothing is changing, the coil has no voltage across it: it is just a wire.
  2. The capacitor is across the coil, so its voltage is 0 too: $q = 0$.
  3. All the current goes through $R$ and the coil: $I_0 = V/R = 6/3$.

$I_0 = 2.0$ A; $q = 0$

Example 3

$S_1$ is now opened. With $L = 20$ mH and $C = 50$ μF, find the largest voltage the capacitor reaches.

Show solution
  1. When the capacitor voltage is largest, the current is 0, so all the energy is in the capacitor.
  2. $\tfrac12LI_0^2 = \tfrac12CV_{\max}^2$, so $V_{\max} = I_0\sqrt{L/C} = 2.0 \times \sqrt{\dfrac{0.020}{50\times10^{-6}}} = 2.0 \times 20$.

40 V

Example 4

In Example 3, what is the size of the capacitor voltage at the moment the current has dropped to $I_0/2$?

Show solution
  1. Energy: $\tfrac12LI_0^2 = \tfrac12L\left(\dfrac{I_0}{2}\right)^2 + \tfrac12CV_C^2$.
  2. So $\tfrac12CV_C^2 = \tfrac34 \times \tfrac12LI_0^2$, giving $V_C = \sqrt{\dfrac{3L}{4C}}\,I_0 = \dfrac{\sqrt3}{2} \times 40$.
  3. In letters, with $I_0 = V/R$: $V_C = \dfrac{V}{2R}\sqrt{\dfrac{3L}{C}}$.

About 35 V

Example 5

How long after opening $S_1$ does the current first drop to $I_0/2$?

Show solution
  1. The current starts at its peak: $I = I_0\cos\omega t$.
  2. $\cos\omega t = \tfrac12$ first when $\omega t = \pi/3$, which is one sixth of a full turn: $t = T/6$.
  3. $t = \dfrac{\pi}{3}\sqrt{LC} = \dfrac{\pi}{3} \times 1.0\times10^{-3}$.

$T/6 = 1.0\times10^{-3}$ s (the dashed line in the demo)

When the current is largest the capacitor is empty, and when the capacitor voltage is largest the current is zero. They are a quarter cycle apart; never take both peaks at the same moment.
