Force on a Current

A wire carrying a current across a field feels $F = IBL$: the Lorentz force on all its moving charges added up

The electromagnetic force is the force on a conductor carrying a current in a magnetic field. Read the words again: "magnetic field" gives $B$, "current" gives $I$, "conductor" gives its length $L$:

$$F = IBL.$$

This is just the Lorentz force on every moving charge inside the wire, added up. Only the part of the wire across the field counts; a wire along the field feels nothing.

Direction: use the same hand rule as for a positive charge, with your fingers along the current. (In Fleming's left-hand rule: first finger = field, second finger = current, thumb = force.)

What $B$ really means. Experiments show that the force is proportional to $H$, $I$ and $L$: $F = \mu H I L$. So $B = \mu H$ is the force on 1 m of wire carrying 1 A across the field. That is the definition of the tesla: 1 T = 1 N/(A·m).

Two parallel wires. Each sits in the other's field, $H = I/(2\pi d)$. So each metre of wire feels $\mu I_1 I_2/(2\pi d)$. Currents in the same direction attract; opposite currents repel.

Electromagnetic force

$$F = IBL \qquad\qquad \text{parallel wires, per metre: } \frac{F}{L} = \frac{\mu I_1 I_2}{2\pi d}$$

$I$: current (A). $B$: flux density across the wire (T). $L$: length of wire in the field (m). If the wire makes an angle θ with $B$, use $IBL\sin\theta$. $d$: distance between the parallel wires (m).

  1. Treat the current as positive charges $e$ moving at speed $v$ along the wire. (Really, electrons go the other way, but the force comes out the same.) Each one feels $evB$.
  2. The wire has $n$ charges per cubic metre, cross-section $S$ and length $L$, so it holds $nSL$ charges.
  3. Total force: $F = evB \times nSL = (envS)\,BL$.
  4. The current is the charge passing a point each second: $I = envS$. So $F = IBL$.

Example 1

A 0.50 m wire carries 4.0 A at right angles to a field of 0.20 T. Find the force on it.

Show solution
  1. $F = IBL = 4.0 \times 0.20 \times 0.50$.

0.40 N

Example 2

The same kind of wire, now 0.50 m long with 2.0 A, makes an angle of 30° with a 0.40 T field. Find the force.

Show solution
  1. Only the part across the field counts: $F = IBL\sin30^\circ = 2.0 \times 0.40 \times 0.50 \times 0.5$.

0.20 N

Example 3

A copper wire of cross-section $1.0\times10^{-6}$ m² carries 2.0 A. Copper has $8.5\times10^{28}$ free electrons per m³. Find the electrons' average speed along the wire.

Show solution
  1. $I = envS$, so $v = \dfrac{I}{enS} = \dfrac{2.0}{1.6\times10^{-19} \times 8.5\times10^{28} \times 1.0\times10^{-6}}$.
  2. $= 1.5\times10^{-4}$ m/s: a slow crawl, even though the signal travels almost at the speed of light.

$1.5\times10^{-4}$ m/s

Example 4

Two long parallel wires 0.10 m apart in air each carry 10 A in the same direction. Find the force on each metre of wire, and say whether they attract or repel. ($\mu_0 = 4\pi\times10^{-7}$ N/A².)

Show solution
  1. $\dfrac FL = \dfrac{\mu_0 I^2}{2\pi d} = \dfrac{4\pi\times10^{-7} \times 100}{2\pi \times 0.10}$.
  2. $= 2.0\times10^{-4}$ N per metre. Same direction, so they attract.

$2.0\times10^{-4}$ N/m, attracting

Example 5

Wires A, B, C pass through the page at the corners of an equilateral triangle of side $a$, each with current $I$: A and B out of the page, C into it. Find the force per unit length on B. (Use permeability $\mu$.)

Show solution
  1. At B, the field from A and the field from C each have size $\dfrac{I}{2\pi a}$, along the tangents of circles round A and C.
  2. The angle between these two equal arrows is 120°, so their sum has the same size: $H = \dfrac{I}{2\pi a}$. (Two equal arrows at 120° form an equilateral triangle with their sum.)
  3. $F = IBL$ with $L = 1$ m and $B = \mu H$: $F = I \cdot \dfrac{\mu I}{2\pi a} = \dfrac{\mu I^2}{2\pi a}$.
  4. Check with the attract/repel rule: B is pulled towards A (same direction) and pushed away from C (opposite). Two equal forces at 120° again give the same size. This is what the demo shows.

$\dfrac{\mu I^2}{2\pi a}$ per metre

Electrons really flow against the current, but they also carry negative charge. The two reversals cancel, so you can always use the current direction with the positive-charge hand rule.
For wires, skip the hand rule: same direction attract, opposite repel. Then add the pulls and pushes as arrows.
  • $F = IBL$; it comes from $qvB$ with $I = envS$.
  • $B$ = the force on 1 m of wire carrying 1 A; 1 T = 1 N/(A·m).
  • Parallel wires: $\mu I_1I_2/(2\pi d)$ per metre; same direction attract.
