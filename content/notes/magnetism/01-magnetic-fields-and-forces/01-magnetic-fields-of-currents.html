Magnetic Fields of Currents

A current makes a magnetic field that circles round it; three formulas give its strength

Magnetic field. The magnetic field $H$ at a point is the force a north pole of strength 1 Wb (weber) would feel there. Its direction is the way a compass needle's north end points. This matches the electric field, which is the force on a charge of +1 C. So a pole of strength $m$ feels $F = mH$, just as a charge feels $F = qE$.

Field lines leave a north pole and enter a south pole. Where the lines are crowded, the field is strong.

A current makes a field. Around a long straight wire, the field lines are circles centred on the wire. Use your right hand: point the thumb along the current, and the curled fingers give the direction of the field. At any one point the field is along the tangent to that circle.

On a flat drawing, a wire coming out of the page is drawn ⊙ (an arrow tip coming at you), and one going into the page is drawn ⊗ (the tail feathers). Current out of the page gives an anticlockwise field.

A circular loop: curl your fingers along the current, and your thumb points along the field at the centre. A solenoid (a long coil) is many loops in a row. Inside it the field is uniform along the axis, and outside it looks just like the field of a bar magnet. That is why a coil carrying a current is called an electromagnet.

The field is a vector. When several currents act, add their fields as arrows.

Magnetic field strength (unit A/m)

$$\text{straight wire: } H = \frac{I}{2\pi r} \qquad \text{loop centre: } H = \frac{I}{2r} \qquad \text{solenoid: } H = nI$$

$I$: current (A). $r$: distance from the wire, or radius of the loop (m). $n$: turns per metre of the solenoid (turns ÷ length in m). The unit A/m is the same as N/Wb.

  1. From $F = mH$: $H$ is in N/Wb. From $H = I/(2\pi r)$: $H$ is in A/m. These are two names for the same unit.
  2. A loop is made of many tiny straight pieces. Each piece makes a field that circles it, and at the centre all of them point the same way, so they add up.
  3. A solenoid is a row of loops. Their fields line up along the axis, which gives a strong, uniform field inside.

Example 1

A long straight wire carries 3.14 A. Find the magnetic field 0.50 m from it.

Show solution
  1. $H = \dfrac{I}{2\pi r} = \dfrac{3.14}{2 \times 3.14 \times 0.50}$.

1.0 A/m

Example 2

(a) A circular loop of radius 0.10 m carries 2.0 A. Find the field at its centre. (b) A solenoid has 500 turns over 25 cm and carries 0.40 A. Find the field inside.

Show solution
  1. (a) $H = I/(2r) = 2.0/0.20 = 10$ A/m.
  2. (b) $n = 500/0.25 = 2000$ per metre, so $H = nI = 2000 \times 0.40 = 800$ A/m.

(a) 10 A/m (b) 800 A/m

Example 3

Long wires lie along the $y$-axis (current $I$ in the $+y$ direction) and along the line $x = 3a$ (current $I$ in the $-y$ direction). Find the field at $(a, 0)$.

Show solution
  1. Right hand: the wire on the $y$-axis gives a field in the $-z$ direction at $x = a$. The other wire (opposite current, on the other side of the point) also gives $-z$.
  2. They add: $H = \dfrac{I}{2\pi a} + \dfrac{I}{2\pi(2a)} = \dfrac{3I}{4\pi a}$.

$\dfrac{3I}{4\pi a}$ in the $-z$ direction

Example 4

For the same two wires, find the field at $(5a, 0)$.

Show solution
  1. Now the point is on the same side of both wires. The $y$-axis wire still gives $-z$, but the wire at $x = 3a$ (opposite current) gives $+z$.
  2. The wire at $3a$ is nearer (distance $2a$), so it wins: $H = \dfrac{I}{2\pi(2a)} - \dfrac{I}{2\pi(5a)} = \dfrac{3I}{20\pi a}$.

$\dfrac{3I}{20\pi a}$ in the $+z$ direction

Example 5

To cancel the field at $(a, 0)$ in Example 3, a circular loop of radius $a$ is placed in the $xy$-plane, centred on that point. Find its current and direction.

Show solution
  1. The loop must give $\dfrac{3I}{4\pi a}$ in the $+z$ direction.
  2. $\dfrac{I'}{2a} = \dfrac{3I}{4\pi a}$, so $I' = \dfrac{3I}{2\pi}$.
  3. For a $+z$ field at the centre, the current goes anticlockwise as seen from the $+z$ side.

$I' = \dfrac{3I}{2\pi}$, anticlockwise seen from $+z$

In $H = nI$, $n$ is the number of turns per metre, not the total number of turns. Divide the total by the length in metres first.
