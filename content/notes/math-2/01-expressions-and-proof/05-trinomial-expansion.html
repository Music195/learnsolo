Trinomial Expansion

Counting arrangements of letters

In $(a + b + c)^n$ each of the $n$ brackets gives $a$, $b$ or $c$. The term $a^p b^q c^r$ comes from every arrangement of $p$ letters $a$, $q$ letters $b$ and $r$ letters $c$ in a row, and there are $\dfrac{n!}{p!\,q!\,r!}$ such arrangements.

Coefficient of $a^p b^q c^r$ in $(a + b + c)^n$

$$\frac{n!}{p!\,q!\,r!} \qquad (p + q + r = n)$$

$p, q, r$ are whole numbers $\ge 0$, and $0! = 1$. With only two letters ($r = 0$) this is ${}_n\mathrm{C}_q$, the binomial coefficient.

  1. Treat $a + b$ as one block: $\{(a + b) + c\}^n$ has the term ${}_n\mathrm{C}_r (a + b)^{n-r} c^r$.
  2. Inside, $(a + b)^{n-r}$ has the term ${}_{n-r}\mathrm{C}_q\, a^p b^q$ with $p = n - r - q$.
  3. ${}_n\mathrm{C}_r \cdot {}_{n-r}\mathrm{C}_q = \dfrac{n!}{r!\,(n-r)!} \cdot \dfrac{(n-r)!}{q!\,p!} = \dfrac{n!}{p!\,q!\,r!}$.

Coefficient of $x^p y^q z^r$ in $(\alpha x + \beta y + \gamma z)^n$

Example 1

Find the coefficient of $x^2yz$ in $(x + y + z)^4$.

Show solution
  1. $p = 2$, $q = 1$, $r = 1$: $\dfrac{4!}{2!\,1!\,1!} = \dfrac{24}{2}$.

$12$

Example 2

Find the coefficient of $x^2y^2z$ in $(x + 2y - 3z)^5$.

Show solution
  1. The term is $\dfrac{5!}{2!\,2!\,1!}\, x^2 (2y)^2 (-3z)^1$.
  2. $30 \cdot 4 \cdot (-3) = -360$.

$-360$

Example 3

Find the coefficient of $x^3$ in $(1 + x + x^2)^4$.

Show solution
  1. Term: $\dfrac{4!}{p!\,q!\,r!} \cdot 1^p x^q (x^2)^r$, so we need $p + q + r = 4$ and $q + 2r = 3$.
  2. $(q, r) = (3, 0)$, $p = 1$: $\dfrac{4!}{1!\,3!\,0!} = 4$. $\;(q, r) = (1, 1)$, $p = 2$: $\dfrac{4!}{2!\,1!\,1!} = 12$.

$4 + 12 = 16$

When the letters are powers of the same variable (Example 3), several choices of $(p, q, r)$ can give the same power. Find all of them and add.
Always check $p + q + r = n$ first. If the exponents don't add up to $n$, that term does not appear and its coefficient is $0$.
  • Coefficient of $a^pb^qc^r$ in $(a + b + c)^n$: $\dfrac{n!}{p!\,q!\,r!}$ with $p + q + r = n$.
  • Numbers in front of the letters are raised to their powers too.
  • It is the binomial theorem used twice.
