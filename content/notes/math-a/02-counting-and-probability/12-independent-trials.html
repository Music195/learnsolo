Probability of Independent Trials

Trials that don't affect each other

Two trials are independent when the result of one has no effect on the other: tossing a coin and rolling a die, rolling a die twice, or drawing a ball and putting it back before drawing again.

Then "this happens in the first trial and that happens in the second" has probability equal to the product.

Independent trials

$$P(A \text{ in trial 1 and } B \text{ in trial 2}) = P(A) \times P(B)$$

Works for any number of independent trials: multiply all the probabilities.

Drawing without replacement is not independent: after taking a red ball there are fewer reds left. Use the multiplication rule for dependent events (note 14), or count with combinations.

Example 1

A coin is tossed and a die is rolled. Find the probability of heads and a number $\ge 5$.

Show solution
  1. $P(\text{heads}) = \dfrac{1}{2}$, $\;P(5 \text{ or } 6) = \dfrac{2}{6} = \dfrac{1}{3}$
  2. The trials are independent: $\dfrac{1}{2} \times \dfrac{1}{3}$

$\dfrac{1}{6}$

Example 2

Bag P has 3 red and 2 white balls; bag Q has 4 red and 4 white. One ball is drawn from each bag. Find the probability that the two balls are the same colour.

Show solution
  1. Both red: $\dfrac{3}{5} \times \dfrac{4}{8} = \dfrac{12}{40}$
  2. Both white: $\dfrac{2}{5} \times \dfrac{4}{8} = \dfrac{8}{40}$
  3. The two cases are mutually exclusive, so add.

$\dfrac{20}{40} = \dfrac{1}{2}$

Example 3

A, B and C each try a problem; they solve it with probabilities $\dfrac{1}{2}, \dfrac{1}{3}, \dfrac{1}{4}$, independently. Find the probability that the problem gets solved.

Show solution
  1. "Solved" = at least one person solves it. Opposite: nobody solves it.
  2. $P(\text{nobody}) = \dfrac{1}{2} \times \dfrac{2}{3} \times \dfrac{3}{4} = \dfrac{1}{4}$
  3. $1 - \dfrac{1}{4}$

$\dfrac{3}{4}$

On a tree diagram: multiply along a path (this and then that), add across paths (this way or that way).
  • Independent trials: multiply the probabilities.
  • With replacement → independent; without replacement → not.
  • Combine with the complement rule for "at least one" questions.
