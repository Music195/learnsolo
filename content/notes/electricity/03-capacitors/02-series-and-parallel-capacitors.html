Series and Parallel Capacitors

Parallel capacitors share a voltage and add their charges; series capacitors share a charge and add their voltages

Recognise the connection by potentials, not by how the drawing looks. Colour each wire by its potential, as for resistors.

  • Parallel: both capacitors sit between the same two potentials. So they have the same voltage $V$, and their charges add: $Q = C_1V + C_2V$. Together they act like one capacitor with $C = C_1 + C_2$. It is like making the plates bigger.
  • Series: one capacitor follows the other, with a middle piece of metal between them (the lower plate of the first, the upper plate of the second, and the wire joining them). If that middle piece started uncharged, its two plates hold $-Q$ and $+Q$. So both capacitors carry the same charge $Q$, and their voltages add. Together: $\dfrac1C = \dfrac1{C_1} + \dfrac1{C_2}$. It is like making the gap wider.

These are the opposite of the resistor rules: capacitors add in parallel and use the reciprocal rule in series.

Joining charged capacitors (charge sharing). Two charged capacitors are joined plate to plate. Each joined pair of plates is cut off from everything else, so its total charge cannot change. Charge flows until both capacitors have the same voltage:

$$V = \frac{\text{total charge on the joined plates}}{C_1 + C_2}.$$

Joining $+$ to $-$ means the charges partly cancel, so you subtract them first.

Combined capacitance

$$\text{parallel: } C = C_1 + C_2 \qquad\qquad \text{series: } \frac1C = \frac1{C_1} + \frac1{C_2}$$

Parallel: same $V$, charges add. Series (middle piece uncharged at the start): same $Q$, voltages add. For two in series, $C = \dfrac{C_1C_2}{C_1 + C_2}$.

  1. Parallel: each has voltage $V$, so $Q_1 = C_1V$ and $Q_2 = C_2V$. The total charge is $(C_1 + C_2)V$, so $C = C_1 + C_2$.
  2. Series: the middle piece of metal has total charge 0, so its plates carry $-Q$ and $+Q$. Then each capacitor holds $Q$.
  3. The voltages are $Q/C_1$ and $Q/C_2$. They add up to $V = Q\left(\dfrac1{C_1} + \dfrac1{C_2}\right)$. Comparing with $V = Q/C$ gives the rule.

C₁ = 2 μF charged to V₁, C₂ = 3 μF charged to V₂. Join them

Example 1

Find the combined capacitance of 2.0 μF and 3.0 μF (a) in parallel, (b) in series.

Show solution
  1. Parallel: $2.0 + 3.0 = 5.0$ μF.
  2. Series: $\dfrac{2.0 \times 3.0}{2.0 + 3.0} = 1.2$ μF. This is smaller than either one.

(a) 5.0 μF (b) 1.2 μF

Example 2

The two uncharged capacitors of Example 1 are put in series across a 10 V battery. Find the charge on each and the voltage across each.

Show solution
  1. Combined $C = 1.2$ μF, so $Q = 1.2 \times 10 = 12$ μC. Each capacitor has this same charge.
  2. $V_1 = 12/2.0 = 6.0$ V and $V_2 = 12/3.0 = 4.0$ V. Check: $6.0 + 4.0 = 10$ ✓.

12 μC each; 6.0 V across 2.0 μF and 4.0 V across 3.0 μF

Example 3

A 2.0 μF capacitor is charged to 10 V and a 3.0 μF capacitor to 5.0 V. They are removed from their batteries and joined $+$ to $+$ and $-$ to $-$. Find the common voltage.

Show solution
  1. Charges: $2.0 \times 10 = 20$ μC and $3.0 \times 5.0 = 15$ μC.
  2. The joined positive plates keep their total: $20 + 15 = 35$ μC.
  3. $V = 35/(2.0 + 3.0) = 7.0$ V.

7.0 V

Example 4

The same two capacitors (charged to 10 V and 5.0 V) are joined the other way: $+$ of one to $-$ of the other. Find the common voltage.

Show solution
  1. One joined pair of plates is $+20$ μC and $-15$ μC, so its total is $+5$ μC.
  2. $V = 5/(2.0 + 3.0) = 1.0$ V.

1.0 V

Example 5

You have three 6.0 μF capacitors. Find the combined capacitance with (a) all three in series, (b) all three in parallel, (c) two in parallel, then in series with the third.

Show solution
  1. (a) $\dfrac1C = \dfrac36$, so $C = 2.0$ μF.
  2. (b) $6 + 6 + 6 = 18$ μF.
  3. (c) The pair gives 12 μF. In series with 6.0 μF: $\dfrac{12 \times 6}{12 + 6} = 4.0$ μF.

(a) 2.0 μF (b) 18 μF (c) 4.0 μF

The series rule only works if the middle piece of metal started uncharged. If it already carried charge (for example, a capacitor charged earlier and then connected in), use charge conservation on that piece instead. See the next notes.
Watch the energy in the demo when you join the capacitors: it always drops. The missing energy becomes heat in the wires while the charge rushes across. (See the note on capacitor energy.)
  • Parallel: same $V$, $C = C_1 + C_2$.
  • Series: same $Q$, $1/C = 1/C_1 + 1/C_2$.
  • Joining charged capacitors: the total charge on the joined plates is kept; $V = \text{total } Q / \text{total } C$.
