Capacitors in DC Circuits

Once a capacitor is fully charged, no current flows through its branch: find the potentials of its two ends

Current cannot cross a capacitor's gap. Charge flows into one plate and out of the other only while the capacitor is charging. Two moments are easy to work out:

  • Just after the switch closes, an uncharged capacitor has $Q = 0$, so $V = Q/C = 0$. It behaves like a plain wire for that instant.
  • A long time later, the capacitor is fully charged and the current in its branch is zero. It behaves like a break in the wire.

Method for the long-time (steady) state.

  1. Remove the capacitor branch: no current flows in it.
  2. Find the currents in the rest of the circuit, and from them the potentials.
  3. The capacitor's voltage is the potential difference between the two points its branch joins. Then $Q = CV$.

A resistor in series with the capacitor carries no current in the steady state, so it has no voltage across it. Treat it like a wire when you find the capacitor's voltage.

Steady state (a long time after switching)

$$I_\text{capacitor branch} = 0, \qquad V_C = V_\text{one end} - V_\text{other end}, \qquad Q = CV_C$$

At the instant of switching on, an uncharged capacitor has $V_C = 0$. In the steady state its branch carries no current. The plate joined to the higher potential is positive.

  1. While current flows into the capacitor, $Q$ grows, and so does $V_C = Q/C$.
  2. The current in the branch is driven by the difference between the voltage available and $V_C$. As $V_C$ rises, the current falls.
  3. It stops when $V_C$ equals the potential difference between the branch's ends. Nothing then changes: this is the steady state.

12 V cell with R₁ = 2 Ω, R₂ = 4 Ω, R₃ = 6 Ω in series. Put C = 5 μF across…

Example 1

A 12 V cell drives 2.0 Ω, 4.0 Ω and 6.0 Ω in series. A 5.0 μF capacitor is connected across the 4.0 Ω resistor. Find its charge a long time later.

Show solution
  1. No current in the capacitor branch: $I = 12/(2.0 + 4.0 + 6.0) = 1.0$ A.
  2. Voltage across 4.0 Ω: $4.0 \times 1.0 = 4.0$ V. The capacitor has the same voltage.
  3. $Q = 5.0 \times 4.0 = 20$ μC.

20 μC

Example 2

A 12 V cell drives $R_1 = 2.0$ Ω and $R_2 = 4.0$ Ω in series. Across $R_2$ is a branch made of a 10 Ω resistor in series with a 5.0 μF capacitor. Find the capacitor's charge in the steady state.

Show solution
  1. No current in the branch, so the 10 Ω has no voltage across it.
  2. $I = 12/6.0 = 2.0$ A, so $R_2$ has $4.0 \times 2.0 = 8.0$ V across it. The capacitor has the same voltage.
  3. $Q = 5.0 \times 8.0 = 40$ μC.

40 μC

Example 3

A 9.0 V cell drives 3.0 Ω and 6.0 Ω in series. $C_1 = 2.0$ μF is across the 3.0 Ω and $C_2 = 3.0$ μF is across the 6.0 Ω. Find both charges in the steady state.

Show solution
  1. $I = 9.0/9.0 = 1.0$ A. The voltages are 3.0 V and 6.0 V.
  2. $Q_1 = 2.0 \times 3.0 = 6.0$ μC and $Q_2 = 3.0 \times 6.0 = 18$ μC.

6.0 μC and 18 μC

Example 4

Resistors $R$, $2R$ and $3R$ are in series across a cell $V$. Find the steady charge on a capacitor $C$ connected (a) across $3R$, (b) across $2R$ and $3R$ together.

Show solution
  1. $I = V/(6R)$. The voltages are $V/6$, $V/3$ and $V/2$.
  2. (a) $Q = C \cdot V/2 = CV/2$.
  3. (b) $V/3 + V/2 = 5V/6$, so $Q = 5CV/6$.

(a) $CV/2$ (b) $5CV/6$

Example 5

An uncharged 10 μF capacitor and a 200 Ω resistor are in series with a 6.0 V cell and a switch. Find the current just after the switch closes, and the charge a long time later.

Show solution
  1. Just after closing: $V_C = 0$, so the resistor has all 6.0 V. $I = 6.0/200 = 0.030$ A.
  2. Long after: $I = 0$, so the resistor has 0 V and the capacitor has 6.0 V. $Q = 10 \times 6.0 = 60$ μC.

30 mA at first; 60 μC in the end

Read the question for when: "just after closing" (an uncharged capacitor acts as a wire) or "a long time after" (the capacitor's branch carries no current). The two give completely different currents.
Colour the wires by potential, as in the demo. The capacitor's voltage is just the difference between the colours at its two ends, whatever lies between them.
