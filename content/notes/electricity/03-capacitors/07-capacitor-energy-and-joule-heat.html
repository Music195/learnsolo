Capacitor Energy and Joule Heat

A charged capacitor stores $\tfrac12QV$; the battery gave $QV$, so the other half became heat

Stored energy. Charging a capacitor means moving charge from one plate to the other, against the voltage already there. The first bit of charge moves across almost no voltage, so it costs almost nothing. The last bit moves across the full $V$. The voltage grows in step with the charge ($v = q/C$), so the total work is the area of the triangle under the $V$–$Q$ line:

$$U = \tfrac12QV = \tfrac12CV^2 = \frac{Q^2}{2C}.$$

What the battery does. A battery pushes every bit of charge through its full voltage $V$, so its work is $W = QV$: the whole rectangle, twice the stored energy.

Where the rest goes. Energy is conserved, so $W = U + H$. The difference $H = \tfrac12QV$ is Joule heat in the resistance of the circuit while the current flows.

The surprising part: the heat is half, whatever the resistance. A bigger resistor makes the charging slower, with a smaller current for longer, but the total heat comes out the same.

Use energy conservation for any change: work done by the battery $=$ change in stored energy $+$ heat. When two charged capacitors are joined, no battery is involved, so the drop in stored energy is the heat.

Energy in a capacitor, and the energy balance

$$U = \tfrac12QV = \tfrac12CV^2 = \frac{Q^2}{2C} \qquad\qquad W_\text{battery} = \Delta U + H$$

$U$: stored energy (J). $W_\text{battery} = (\text{charge through the battery}) \times V$. $H$: Joule heat in the resistors. Charging from empty on a battery $V$: $W = QV$, $U = H = \tfrac12QV$.

  1. At a moment when the capacitor holds $q$, its voltage is $v = q/C$.
  2. Moving a little more charge $\Delta q$ across costs $v\,\Delta q$: a thin strip under the $V$–$Q$ line.
  3. The strips fill the triangle from $(0, 0)$ to $(Q, V)$, so $U = \tfrac12QV$. Using $Q = CV$ gives the other two forms.
  4. The battery moves the same charge $Q$ across a constant $V$, which is the rectangle $QV$. The gap between them, $\tfrac12QV$, is the heat.

Charging C = 100 μF from a 10 V battery through R (real time)

Example 1

Find the energy stored in a 200 μF capacitor charged to 10 V.

Show solution
  1. $U = \tfrac12CV^2 = \tfrac12 \times 200\times10^{-6} \times 10^2$.

$1.0\times10^{-2}$ J

Example 2

The capacitor in Example 1 was charged from empty by a 10 V battery through a resistor. Find the battery's work and the heat in the resistor.

Show solution
  1. $Q = CV = 2.0\times10^{-3}$ C, so $W = QV = 2.0\times10^{-2}$ J.
  2. $H = W - U = 2.0\times10^{-2} - 1.0\times10^{-2} = 1.0\times10^{-2}$ J.

$W = 2.0\times10^{-2}$ J, $H = 1.0\times10^{-2}$ J

Example 3

A 12 V cell, a switch S and $R_1 = 4.0$ Ω are in series with $R_2 = 8.0$ Ω. Across $R_2$ is a branch of $C = 50$ μF in series with $R_3 = 2.0$ Ω. With S closed for a long time, find the power used in the circuit and the energy stored in $C$.

Show solution
  1. No current in the capacitor branch: $I = 12/(4.0 + 8.0) = 1.0$ A, so $P = 12 \times 1.0 = 12$ W.
  2. $R_3$ carries no current, so $C$ has the same voltage as $R_2$: $8.0 \times 1.0 = 8.0$ V.
  3. $U = \tfrac12 \times 50\times10^{-6} \times 8.0^2 = 1.6\times10^{-3}$ J.

12 W; $1.6\times10^{-3}$ J

Example 4

S is now opened. The capacitor in Example 3 discharges around the loop through $R_3$ and $R_2$. Find the heat produced in each.

Show solution
  1. All $1.6\times10^{-3}$ J turns into heat.
  2. $R_2$ and $R_3$ are in series, so at every moment they carry the same current. Heat $I^2R$ splits in the ratio $8.0 : 2.0$.
  3. $R_2$: $\tfrac{8}{10} \times 1.6\times10^{-3} = 1.28\times10^{-3}$ J. $R_3$: $\tfrac{2}{10} \times 1.6\times10^{-3} = 3.2\times10^{-4}$ J.

$R_2$: $1.3\times10^{-3}$ J; $R_3$: $3.2\times10^{-4}$ J

Example 5

A 2.0 μF capacitor charged to 5.0 V is joined to an uncharged 3.0 μF capacitor. How much energy becomes heat?

Show solution
  1. Before: $Q = 10$ μC and $U = \tfrac12 \times 2.0 \times 5.0^2 = 25$ μJ.
  2. After: $V = 10/5.0 = 2.0$ V, so $U = \tfrac12 \times 5.0 \times 2.0^2 = 10$ μJ.
  3. Heat $= 25 - 10 = 15$ μJ.

15 μJ

Don't mix up $QV$ and $\tfrac12QV$. $QV$ is the battery's work, because the battery's voltage is constant. $\tfrac12QV$ is the capacitor's energy, because its voltage grew from 0.
Pick the form of $U$ that uses what stays fixed: $\tfrac12CV^2$ when $V$ is fixed (battery connected), $Q^2/(2C)$ when $Q$ is fixed (disconnected).
  • $U = \tfrac12QV = \tfrac12CV^2 = Q^2/(2C)$: the triangle under the $V$–$Q$ line.
  • Charging from a battery: battery $QV$, stored $\tfrac12QV$, heat $\tfrac12QV$, whatever $R$ is.
  • In general: $W_\text{battery} = \Delta U + H$.
