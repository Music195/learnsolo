How a Capacitor Charges

A battery moves charge from one plate to the other, so the plates hold $+Q$ and $-Q$ with $Q = CV$

A capacitor is two metal plates facing each other with a small gap between them. Connect it to a battery and the battery pulls electrons off one plate and pushes them onto the other. One plate ends up with $+Q$ and the other with $-Q$: the same amount, opposite signs. The capacitor as a whole stays neutral. When people say "the capacitor holds charge $Q$", they mean the charge on the positive plate.

Charge flows until the potential difference between the plates equals the battery's voltage $V$. Then the current stops. The more voltage, the more charge, in direct proportion:

$$Q = CV.$$

The constant $C$ is the capacitance, measured in farads (F = C/V). It tells you how much charge the capacitor holds for each volt.

What decides $C$? Only the shape: the plate area $S$, the gap $d$, and what fills the gap. With air or vacuum in the gap,

$$C = \varepsilon_0\frac{S}{d}, \qquad \varepsilon_0 = 8.85\times10^{-12}\ \text{F/m}.$$

Bigger plates hold more charge. A smaller gap also holds more, because the same voltage then gives a stronger field.

Parallel-plate capacitor

$$Q = CV, \qquad C = \varepsilon_0\frac{S}{d}, \qquad E = \frac{V}{d} = \frac{Q}{\varepsilon_0 S}$$

$Q$: charge on the positive plate (C). $V$: potential difference between the plates (V). $C$: capacitance (F). $S$: plate area (m²). $d$: gap (m). $E$: the uniform field between the plates (V/m).

  1. Gauss's law: $Q/\varepsilon_0$ field lines leave the $+Q$ plate. They all go straight across the gap to the $-Q$ plate.
  2. These lines are spread over the area $S$, so the field strength (lines per square metre) is $E = \dfrac{Q}{\varepsilon_0 S}$.
  3. The field is uniform, so $V = Ed = \dfrac{Qd}{\varepsilon_0 S}$.
  4. Rearranged: $Q = \dfrac{\varepsilon_0 S}{d}\,V$. Comparing with $Q = CV$ gives $C = \varepsilon_0 S/d$.

Plates of area 2.0 × 10⁻² m², charged on 50 V at a 1.0 mm gap. Then widen the gap

Example 1

Two plates of area $2.0\times10^{-2}$ m² are 1.0 mm apart in air. Find the capacitance.

Show solution
  1. $C = \varepsilon_0 S/d = 8.85\times10^{-12} \times 2.0\times10^{-2} / 1.0\times10^{-3}$.
  2. $= 1.77\times10^{-10}$ F.

$1.8\times10^{-10}$ F

Example 2

The capacitor in Example 1 is connected to a 50 V battery. Find the charge on the positive plate and the field between the plates.

Show solution
  1. $Q = CV = 1.77\times10^{-10} \times 50 = 8.85\times10^{-9}$ C.
  2. $E = V/d = 50 / 1.0\times10^{-3} = 5.0\times10^4$ V/m.

$8.9\times10^{-9}$ C; $5.0\times10^4$ V/m

Example 3

The capacitor stays connected to the 50 V battery while the gap is widened to 2.0 mm. What happens to $C$, $Q$ and $E$? How much charge flows back through the battery?

Show solution
  1. The battery keeps $V = 50$ V.
  2. $C = \varepsilon_0 S/d$ halves, to $8.85\times10^{-11}$ F. So $Q = CV$ halves, to $4.4\times10^{-9}$ C.
  3. The other half, $4.4\times10^{-9}$ C, flows back off the plates and through the battery.
  4. $E = V/d$ halves, to $2.5\times10^4$ V/m.

$C$, $Q$ and $E$ all halve; $4.4\times10^{-9}$ C flows back through the battery

Example 4

This time the battery is disconnected first (at 1.0 mm), and then the gap is widened to 2.0 mm. What happens to $Q$, $V$ and $E$?

Show solution
  1. The plates are isolated, so the charge cannot change: $Q = 8.85\times10^{-9}$ C.
  2. $C$ halves, so $V = Q/C$ doubles, to 100 V.
  3. $E = Q/(\varepsilon_0 S)$ does not depend on $d$, so it stays $5.0\times10^4$ V/m. Check: $100 / 2.0\times10^{-3} = 5.0\times10^4$ ✓.

$Q$ and $E$ unchanged; $V$ doubles to 100 V

Example 5

How large would the plates need to be for a 1.0 F capacitor with a 1.0 mm air gap?

Show solution
  1. $S = Cd/\varepsilon_0 = 1.0 \times 1.0\times10^{-3} / 8.85\times10^{-12}$.
  2. $= 1.13\times10^8$ m²: a square about 11 km on each side. A farad is a huge unit, so real capacitors are usually measured in μF, nF or pF.

$1.1\times10^8$ m²

Before you calculate, ask what stays fixed. If the battery is still connected, $V$ is fixed and $Q$ changes. If the battery has been disconnected, $Q$ is fixed (the charge has nowhere to go) and $V$ changes.
$E = Q/(\varepsilon_0 S)$ is the quickest way to see what the field does. The field depends only on how much charge sits on each square metre of plate, not on the gap.
  • The plates carry $+Q$ and $-Q$; $Q = CV$.
  • $C = \varepsilon_0 S/d$: bigger plates or a smaller gap give a bigger $C$.
  • Connected: $V$ fixed. Disconnected: $Q$ fixed.
