Kirchhoff's Laws

Current in = current out at a junction; around a loop, the lifts equal the drops

First law (junctions). At any junction, the total current flowing in equals the total current flowing out. If 0.5 A arrives and one branch takes 0.3 A, the other must take 0.2 A.

Second law (loops). Go once around any closed loop. The total of the emfs (cells lifting the potential) equals the total of the voltage drops (resistors lowering it). On the potential ladder: everything that goes up must come back down. An emf and a voltage drop are both potential differences; we call it an emf when the potential rises along our direction, a drop when it falls.

Method when there are two or more cells (and combining resistances won't work):

  1. Choose a direction and a letter for each unknown current ($I_1$, $I_2$, …), obeying the first law at every junction. A wrong guess of direction is fine.
  2. Choose loops and give each a direction of travel (an arrow).
  3. For each loop write: (sum of emfs) = (sum of drops), with these signs:
    • cell: $+E$ if your travel goes from − to + through it, $-E$ if from + to −;
    • resistor: $+RI$ if you travel with its current arrow, $-RI$ if against.
  4. Solve. A negative answer means the current really flows the other way.

Potentials need an earth (0 V). The potential differences are fixed, but the potentials change if the earth is moved.

Kirchhoff's laws

$$\sum I_{\text{in}} = \sum I_{\text{out}} \qquad \sum E = \sum RI \ \ \text{(around a closed loop)}$$

First law: at a junction. Second law: around a closed loop (an open switch breaks the loop). Signs follow the rules above.

  1. First law. Charge cannot pile up at a junction in a steady circuit, so every coulomb arriving must leave. Charge per second in = charge per second out.
  2. Second law. Potential is a height. Start at a point, walk round the loop, and arrive back at the same point: you are at the same height.
  3. So the total rise (emfs) minus the total fall (drops $RI$) is zero, which is $\sum E = \sum RI$.

E₁ = 70 V, R₁ = 10 Ω, R₃ = 10 Ω, R₂ = 20 Ω; bottom wire earthed

Example 1

An 11 V cell drives current through 10 Ω, then through 20 Ω and 30 Ω in parallel. Find the current in each resistor.

Show solution
  1. Parallel part: $\dfrac{1}{R} = \dfrac1{20} + \dfrac1{30} = \dfrac{5}{60}$, so 12 Ω. Total $10 + 12 = 22$ Ω, $I = 11/22 = 0.50$ A.
  2. The 10 Ω drops $5.0$ V. The second law says the parallel pair drops the remaining $11 - 5.0 = 6.0$ V.
  3. $I_{20} = 6.0/20 = 0.30$ A, $I_{30} = 6.0/30 = 0.20$ A. First law: $0.30 + 0.20 = 0.50$ ✓.

0.50 A, 0.30 A, 0.20 A

Example 2

In Example 1, find the potentials of the three different colours (a) with the cell's − terminal earthed, (b) with the junction after the 10 Ω resistor earthed.

Show solution
  1. (a) − terminal 0 V; after the parallel pair, going backwards, up 6.0 V: 6.0 V; + terminal 11 V.
  2. (b) The junction is 0 V. The + terminal is 5.0 V above it: +5.0 V. The − terminal is 6.0 V below it: −6.0 V.

(a) 11 V, 6.0 V, 0 V; (b) 5.0 V, 0 V, −6.0 V

Example 3

The demo circuit with $E_2 = 10$ V. Three branches join a top wire and a bottom wire. Left: 70 V cell (+ upwards) and $R_1 = 10$ Ω, current $I_1$ up. Right: 10 V cell (+ upwards) and $R_2 = 20$ Ω, current $I_2$ up. Middle: $R_3 = 10$ Ω, current $I_1 + I_2$ down. Find the currents.

Show solution
  1. Loop ① (left branch up, middle down): $70 = 10I_1 + 10(I_1 + I_2)$.
  2. Loop ② (outer: left up, right down): the 10 V cell is crossed from + to −, so $-10$; $R_2$ is crossed against $I_2$, so $-20I_2$: $70 - 10 = 10I_1 - 20I_2$.
  3. From ①: $I_2 = 7 - 2I_1$. Into ②: $60 = 10I_1 - 140 + 40I_1$, so $I_1 = 4.0$ A and $I_2 = -1.0$ A.

$I_1 = 4.0$ A up; $I_2 = 1.0$ A down (the minus sign); middle 3.0 A down

Example 4

In Example 3 the bottom wire is earthed. Find the potential of the top wire and of point A, between the right cell and $R_2$.

Show solution
  1. Top wire: up through $R_3$ against its 3.0 A, a rise of $10 \times 3.0 = 30$ V. So 30 V.
  2. A: from the bottom (0 V), the right cell lifts 10 V, so 10 V.
  3. Check: from A to the top through $R_2$, $10 + 20 \times 1.0 = 30$ V ✓.

Top wire 30 V; A = 10 V

Example 5

Two cells, 6.0 V and 3.0 V, are connected + to + through a 2.0 Ω resistor and − to − through a 1.0 Ω resistor. Find the current and its direction.

Show solution
  1. One loop, current $I$ guessed to leave the 6.0 V cell's + terminal.
  2. Going round with $I$: through the 6.0 V cell − to +: $+6.0$; through the 3.0 V cell + to −: $-3.0$; drops $2.0I + 1.0I$.
  3. $6.0 - 3.0 = 3.0I$, so $I = 1.0$ A. Positive, so the guess was right.

1.0 A, out of the 6.0 V cell's + terminal (it charges the 3.0 V cell)

The sign of $RI$ depends on your travel direction compared with the current arrow you drew, not on which way you think the current "really" goes. Follow the rules mechanically, and let the algebra tell you the true direction.
Questions often ask for a potential, not a current. You still need the currents first: get them with Kirchhoff's laws, then colour the circuit and walk from the earth to the point, adding rises and subtracting drops.
  • Junction: current in = current out.
  • Closed loop: $\sum E = \sum RI$, with signs set by your travel direction.
  • A negative current means the real direction is opposite to your arrow.
