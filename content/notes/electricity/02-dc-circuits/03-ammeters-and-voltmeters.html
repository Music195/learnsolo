Ammeters and Voltmeters

A meter should change the circuit as little as possible; extra resistors extend its range

A good meter reads the value the circuit had before the meter was added.

  • An ammeter goes in series, so the current passes through it. Its resistance is made very small (ideal: 0 Ω) so it hardly reduces the current. Connected in parallel by mistake, it would grab most of the current: wrong readings, and possibly a broken meter.
  • A voltmeter goes in parallel across the part being measured. Its resistance is made very large (ideal: infinite) so it takes almost no current.

Inside both is the same delicate part: a coil on a pointer. A current through the coil makes the pointer turn, in proportion to the current. The coil can only take a small current, for example 50 mA at full scale. A voltmeter is really the same meter with a different scale printed on it.

Extending an ammeter: the shunt. To measure up to $n$ times the full-scale current, put a small resistor in parallel with the meter. It carries the extra current around the coil. Parallel parts share the same potential difference, so the currents split in the inverse ratio of the resistances.

Extending a voltmeter: the multiplier. To measure up to $n$ times the full-scale voltage, put a large resistor in series with the meter. It takes most of the voltage. Series parts share the same current, so the voltages split in the ratio of the resistances.

Range ×$n$

$$\text{shunt (parallel): } R_s = \frac{r_A}{n - 1} \qquad \text{multiplier (series): } R_m = (n - 1)\,r_V$$

$r_A$: ammeter's internal resistance. $r_V$: voltmeter's internal resistance. $n$: new full scale ÷ old full scale.

  1. Shunt. At full scale the meter takes $I_0$ and the total is $nI_0$, so the shunt takes $(n - 1)I_0$.
  2. Parallel means equal potential difference: $r_AI_0 = R_s(n - 1)I_0$, so $R_s = \dfrac{r_A}{n - 1}$.
  3. Multiplier. At full scale the meter has $V_0$ across it and the total is $nV_0$, so the multiplier has $(n - 1)V_0$.
  4. Series means equal current, so the voltages are in the ratio of the resistances: $\dfrac{R_m}{r_V} = n - 1$, giving $R_m = (n - 1)r_V$.

Meter G: full scale 50 mA (r = 1.0 Ω) as ammeter, 3.0 V (r = 1.0 kΩ) as voltmeter

Example 1

An ammeter reads up to 50 mA and has internal resistance 1.0 Ω. What resistor, connected how, extends its range to 500 mA?

Show solution
  1. Of 500 mA, only 50 mA may pass through the meter, so the other 450 mA must go round it: a resistor in parallel.
  2. Currents 50 : 450 = 1 : 9, so the resistances are 9 : 1. $R_s = 1.0/9$ Ω.

$\tfrac19$ Ω ≈ 0.11 Ω in parallel

Example 2

Same ammeter. What shunt extends the range to 5 A?

Show solution
  1. 5 A = 5000 mA, so $n = 100$. The shunt takes 4950 mA; the currents are 50 : 4950 = 1 : 99.
  2. $R_s = 1.0/99$ Ω.

$\tfrac{1}{99}$ Ω ≈ 0.010 Ω in parallel

Example 3

A voltmeter reads up to 3.0 V and has internal resistance 1.0 kΩ. What resistors extend its range to 30 V and to 300 V?

Show solution
  1. A parallel resistor can't help: the meter would still have the same voltage. So add the resistor in series.
  2. 30 V: the meter takes 3.0 V, the multiplier 27 V. Ratio 1 : 9, so $R_m = 9 \times 1.0 = 9.0$ kΩ.
  3. 300 V: ratio 1 : 99, so $R_m = 99$ kΩ.

9.0 kΩ and 99 kΩ, in series

Example 4

To measure a 100 Ω resistor, the voltmeter is connected across both the resistor and the ammeter ($r_A = 2.0$ Ω). What value of $R' = V/I$ does this give?

Show solution
  1. The voltmeter reads the drop across both: $V = r_AI + RI$.
  2. $R' = V/I = R + r_A = 100 + 2.0$. This is a series combination, so a small $r_A$ is better.

$R' = 102$ Ω (too large)

Example 5

Now the voltmeter ($r_V = 10$ kΩ) is across the 100 Ω resistor only, with the ammeter outside both. What value of $R' = V/I$ does this give?

Show solution
  1. The ammeter's current $I$ splits: $I = \dfrac{V}{R} + \dfrac{V}{r_V}$.
  2. $R' = \dfrac{V}{I} = \dfrac{Rr_V}{R + r_V} = \dfrac{100 \times 10\,000}{10\,100}$. This is a parallel combination, so a large $r_V$ is better.

$R' \approx 99.0$ Ω (slightly too small)

Don't mix them up: shunt in parallel for an ammeter (to bypass current), multiplier in series for a voltmeter (to take voltage). Each choice comes from what must stay fixed: the meter's own current, or its own voltage.
You don't need to memorise $R_s$ and $R_m$. Ask: "How much must go round the meter (or across the extra resistor)?" Write the ratio, then invert it for parallel or keep it for series.
  • Ammeter: in series, tiny resistance. Voltmeter: in parallel, huge resistance.
  • Shunt (parallel) $R_s = r_A/(n-1)$; multiplier (series) $R_m = (n-1)r_V$.
  • Meter resistances cause small errors: $R + r_A$ or $Rr_V/(R + r_V)$.
