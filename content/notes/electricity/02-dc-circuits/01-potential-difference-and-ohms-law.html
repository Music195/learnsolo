Potential Difference and Ohm's Law

Potential is a height measured from earth; colour the circuit to see the heights

Several quantities are measured in volts: potential, potential difference, voltage, emf and voltage drop. The odd one out is potential. The other four are all differences between two potentials; potential difference and voltage are exactly the same thing.

Picture asking "how high is this desk?". One person says 70 cm (from the floor); another, standing outside the building, says 10.7 m (from the ground). Both are right; they used different zero levels. In a circuit the zero level is the earth (ground), set to 0 V. The potential of a point is its height above earth. A potential difference is the difference between two heights, so it doesn't depend on where the zero is.

The colouring method. Colour every part of the circuit by its potential, using one colour per height:

  • a plain wire never changes colour: everything joined by wire has the same potential;
  • colours change only across components (cells, resistors, capacitors, coils);
  • a cell always has a potential difference between its terminals ($+$ is higher), whether or not current flows;
  • a resistor has potential difference $RI$ (Ohm's law), so it grows with the current. No current through a resistor means no potential difference across it: both ends get the same colour.

Then redraw the colours as a ladder of heights, highest at the top. The cell lifts the charge up; each resistor drops it down. In series the drops add up to the cell's voltage. In parallel, each branch drops the full amount.

Ohm's law and combined resistance

$$V = RI \qquad R_{\text{series}} = R_1 + R_2 \qquad \frac{1}{R_{\text{parallel}}} = \frac{1}{R_1} + \frac{1}{R_2}$$

$V$: potential difference across a resistor (V). $R$: resistance (Ω). $I$: current through it (A). Current flows through a resistor from high potential to low potential.

  1. Series. One wire path, so the same current $I$ flows everywhere. The colours go: top of cell → (drop $R_1I$) → (drop $R_2I$) → bottom of cell, so $V = R_1I + R_2I = (R_1 + R_2)I$. A single resistor $R_1 + R_2$ would do the same.
  2. Parallel. Both resistors sit between the same two colours, so each has the full $V$: $I_1 = V/R_1$, $I_2 = V/R_2$.
  3. The cell supplies $I = I_1 + I_2 = V\left(\dfrac{1}{R_1} + \dfrac{1}{R_2}\right)$, which matches one resistor $R$ with $\dfrac1R = \dfrac1{R_1} + \dfrac1{R_2}$.

R₁ = 4 Ω. Each colour is one potential; the ladder shows the heights

Example 1

A 12 V cell is connected to 4.0 Ω and 2.0 Ω in series. Find the current and the potential difference across each resistor.

Show solution
  1. $R = 4.0 + 2.0 = 6.0$ Ω, so $I = 12/6.0 = 2.0$ A (the same through both).
  2. $V_1 = 4.0 \times 2.0 = 8.0$ V, $V_2 = 2.0 \times 2.0 = 4.0$ V. Check: $8.0 + 4.0 = 12$.

2.0 A; 8.0 V and 4.0 V

Example 2

The same resistors are connected in parallel to the 12 V cell. Find each current, the total current and the combined resistance.

Show solution
  1. Each has the full 12 V: $I_1 = 12/4.0 = 3.0$ A, $I_2 = 12/2.0 = 6.0$ A.
  2. Total $I = 9.0$ A, so $R = 12/9.0 \approx 1.3$ Ω. (Check: $1/R = 1/4 + 1/2 = 3/4$.)

3.0 A, 6.0 A; 9.0 A; about 1.3 Ω

Example 3

In Example 1 the negative terminal of the cell is earthed. Find the potential of the point between the resistors. Then move the earth to that point and find the potentials of both cell terminals.

Show solution
  1. From the + terminal (12 V), the 4.0 Ω resistor drops 8.0 V, so the middle point is at 4.0 V.
  2. Earth at the middle (0 V): the + terminal is 8.0 V above it, so +8.0 V. The − terminal is 4.0 V below it, so −4.0 V.
  3. The potential differences didn't change; only the zero level moved.

4.0 V; then +8.0 V and −4.0 V

Example 4

A 9.0 V cell, a 3.0 kΩ resistor and an open switch are in series. Find the potential difference across the resistor and across the switch.

Show solution
  1. The switch is open, so no current flows: $I = 0$.
  2. Resistor: $V = RI = 0$, so both its ends share a colour.
  3. So the colour changes only at the switch gap, which must take the whole 9.0 V.

0 V across the resistor; 9.0 V across the switch

Example 5

A 50 V cell drives current around an outer loop through a 40 Ω resistor and a variable resistor $R$ in series. A second branch, containing a 20 V cell and a galvanometer G, is connected across the 40 Ω resistor. $R$ is adjusted until G reads zero. Find $R$. (Ignore internal resistances.)

Show solution
  1. No current through G means no current in that whole branch. The branch's only potential difference is the 20 V cell, and it sits between the same two colours as the 40 Ω resistor.
  2. So the 40 Ω resistor has 20 V across it: $I = 20/40 = 0.50$ A, and this current flows round the outer loop.
  3. $R$ takes the rest: $50 - 20 = 30$ V, so $R = 30/0.50 = 60$ Ω.

$R = 60$ Ω

A resistor with no current has no potential difference across it. Don't give its two ends different colours just because a resistor is there. This is the key step in many circuit problems.
Start colouring at the + terminal of a cell and follow the wire until you reach a component. Pick a new colour only after a component. Then put the colours in height order. Most circuit questions become easy once the colours are right.
  • Potential: height above earth (0 V). Potential difference = voltage: a difference of heights.
  • Wires keep their colour; cells always lift; resistors drop $RI$, or nothing if $I = 0$.
  • Series: same $I$, drops add. Parallel: same $V$, currents add.
