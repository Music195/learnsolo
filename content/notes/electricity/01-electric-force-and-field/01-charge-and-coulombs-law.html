Charge and Coulomb's Law

Like charges repel, unlike charges attract, and the force is surprisingly strong

Get the direction right first, before any formula:

  • two positive charges, or two negative charges: they push apart (repulsion);
  • a positive and a negative charge: they pull together (attraction).

Where charge comes from. An atom has a nucleus (protons, positive) with electrons (negative) around it. The nucleus stays put; only electrons move. An object that has lost electrons is positively charged; one that has gained electrons is negatively charged.

So a "positively charged" metal ball still contains huge numbers of both kinds of charge. It just has a few more positive than negative. In the same way, "charge zero" does not mean "no charges". It means equal amounts of + and − that cancel (the object is electrically neutral). Keep this picture in mind; it makes capacitors much easier later.

The size of the force between two small charges is given by Coulomb's law. Charge is measured in coulombs (C). The constant $k$ is about $9.0 \times 10^{9}$, a very large number, so electric forces are far stronger than everyday experience suggests.

Coulomb's law (size of the force)

$$F = k\frac{|q_1q_2|}{r^2} \qquad k = 9.0 \times 10^{9}\ \mathrm{N\,m^2/C^2}$$

$F$: size of the force on each charge (N). $q_1$, $q_2$: the charges (C). $r$: distance between them (m). The two charges feel equal and opposite forces (Newton's third law). Charge of one electron: $-e = -1.6 \times 10^{-19}$ C.

  1. A charged ball hangs on a thread and is pushed sideways by a charge at the same height. Three forces act: weight $mg$ (down), tension $T$ (along the thread) and the electric force $F$ (sideways).
  2. The ball is at rest, so the three forces balance. Add $mg$ and $F$: their sum must be equal and opposite to $T$, so it points along the thread.
  3. $mg$ and $F$ are at right angles and their sum makes an angle $\theta$ with the vertical (the thread's angle), so $\tan\theta = F/(mg)$.
  4. Hence $F = mg\tan\theta$. Then Coulomb's law gives the unknown charge: $q = \dfrac{Fr^2}{k|Q|}$.

Example 1

Charges of $+2.0 \times 10^{-6}$ C and $+3.0 \times 10^{-6}$ C are 0.30 m apart. Find the force between them and say whether it attracts or repels.

Show solution
  1. $F = 9.0 \times 10^{9} \times \dfrac{2.0 \times 10^{-6} \times 3.0 \times 10^{-6}}{0.30^2} = \dfrac{5.4 \times 10^{-2}}{0.090}$.
  2. Both are positive, so they repel.

$F = 0.60$ N, repulsion

Example 2

The distance between two charges is doubled and one of the charges is made 3 times larger. By what factor does the force change?

Show solution
  1. The top of the fraction gets $\times 3$.
  2. $r^2$ gets $\times 4$, so the force gets $\times \tfrac14$.
  3. Together: $3 \times \tfrac14$.

$\tfrac34$ of the original force

Example 3

A neutral metal sphere gains $5.0 \times 10^{12}$ extra electrons. What is its charge?

Show solution
  1. Each electron carries $-1.6 \times 10^{-19}$ C.
  2. $q = 5.0 \times 10^{12} \times (-1.6 \times 10^{-19}) = -8.0 \times 10^{-7}$ C.
  3. The sphere still has its protons and original electrons. They cancel; only the extra electrons show.

$q = -8.0 \times 10^{-7}$ C

Example 4

In a hydrogen atom the electron and the proton are about $5.3 \times 10^{-11}$ m apart. Find the electric force between them.

Show solution
  1. $F = 9.0 \times 10^{9} \times \dfrac{(1.6 \times 10^{-19})^2}{(5.3 \times 10^{-11})^2} = \dfrac{9.0 \times 10^{9} \times 2.56 \times 10^{-38}}{2.81 \times 10^{-21}}$.
  2. $F \approx 8.2 \times 10^{-8}$ N. Opposite signs, so it attracts.
  3. For comparison the electron's weight is about $9 \times 10^{-30}$ N, around $10^{22}$ times smaller.

$F \approx 8.2 \times 10^{-8}$ N, attraction

Example 5

A small ball A of mass 1.0 g hangs on a thread. A fixed charge B $= +1.0 \times 10^{-7}$ C is brought level with it. A is pushed away until the thread makes 30° with the vertical, with A and B 0.10 m apart. Find A's charge. ($g = 9.8$ m/s²)

Show solution
  1. We don't know A's charge yet, so first get the force from the balance: $F = mg\tan 30^\circ = 1.0 \times 10^{-3} \times 9.8 \times 0.577 = 5.66 \times 10^{-3}$ N.
  2. Coulomb's law: $q = \dfrac{Fr^2}{kQ} = \dfrac{5.66 \times 10^{-3} \times 0.10^2}{9.0 \times 10^{9} \times 1.0 \times 10^{-7}} = \dfrac{5.66 \times 10^{-5}}{900}$.
  3. A is pushed away from a positive charge, so A is positive too.

$q \approx +6.3 \times 10^{-8}$ C

Coulomb's law gives only the size of the force. Do not put minus signs into it to get a direction. Decide the direction from "like repel, unlike attract", and draw it on the diagram.
When three forces hold something still, add two of them. Their sum must be equal and opposite to the third. With a right angle in the picture, $\tan$ gives the answer in one line.
  • Like charges repel, unlike charges attract.
  • Only electrons move; "charge zero" means + and − cancel, not "no charges".
  • $F = k|q_1q_2|/r^2$ with $k = 9.0 \times 10^{9}$ N m²/C².
