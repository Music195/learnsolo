Electric Potential

Potential is the potential energy of +1 C, and it adds as plain numbers

Potential energy. Take a charge $q$ from very far away ("infinity", where the force is zero) and push it slowly to a distance $r$ from a charge $Q$. If both are positive, you must push against the repulsion; the work you do is stored as electric potential energy. Taking infinity as the zero level, it works out to $U = kQq/r$. This is the same calculation as for gravitational potential energy.

Potential $V$ is the potential energy that +1 C would have at a point: $V = U/q$, so $U = qV$. Its unit is the volt (V = J/C). For a point charge, $V = kQ/r$.

Potential and energy are scalars: no direction. But a scalar can still be negative, just as a temperature can be −20 °C. So:

  • put charges in with their signs: near a negative charge the potential is negative;
  • with several charges, just add the numbers. No arrows, no parallelogram.

Compare this with force and field. They are vectors: their formulas give sizes only, and you add them as arrows.

Potential energy and potential of a point charge (zero at infinity)

$$U = k\frac{Qq}{r} \qquad V = k\frac{Q}{r} \qquad U = qV$$

$U$: potential energy of $q$ (J). $V$: potential (V). Charges go in with their signs. Moving $q$ slowly from potential $V_A$ to $V_B$ takes work $W = q(V_B - V_A)$ by the outside force. $U = qV$ holds for any field; the $kQ/r$ forms only for point charges.

  1. Put $Q$ at the origin and $q$ on the $x$-axis. Both positive: the repulsion is $kQq/x^2$ in the $+x$ direction.
  2. Move $q$ slowly in from infinity. The outside force must balance the repulsion, so it is $-kQq/x^2$ (pointing in $-x$).
  3. Work done from $\infty$ to $r$: $W = \displaystyle\int_{\infty}^{r} \left(-\frac{kQq}{x^2}\right)dx = \left[\frac{kQq}{x}\right]_{\infty}^{r} = \frac{kQq}{r} - 0$.
  4. This work is stored, so $U(r) = kQq/r$ with $U(\infty) = 0$. Dividing by $q$: $V = kQ/r$.
  5. Memory hook: the energy formulas came from integrating $1/x^2$, so they have $r$, not $r^2$.

Example 1

Find the potential 0.20 m from a charge of $+3.0 \times 10^{-9}$ C.

Show solution
  1. $V = 9.0 \times 10^{9} \times \dfrac{3.0 \times 10^{-9}}{0.20} = \dfrac{27}{0.20}$.

$V = 135$ V

Example 2

A charge of $+2.0 \times 10^{-6}$ C sits at the point of Example 1. Find its potential energy. What if the source charge were $-3.0 \times 10^{-9}$ C instead?

Show solution
  1. $U = qV = 2.0 \times 10^{-6} \times 135 = 2.7 \times 10^{-4}$ J.
  2. With $-3.0 \times 10^{-9}$ C the potential is $-135$ V, so $U = -2.7 \times 10^{-4}$ J. Negative: the charges attract, and you would have to do work to pull them apart.

$+2.7 \times 10^{-4}$ J; $-2.7 \times 10^{-4}$ J

Example 3

How much work must an outside force do to bring $+1.0 \times 10^{-6}$ C slowly from infinity to 0.10 m from a charge of $+2.0 \times 10^{-8}$ C?

Show solution
  1. Potential there: $V = 9.0 \times 10^{9} \times 2.0 \times 10^{-8}/0.10 = 1800$ V. At infinity, 0.
  2. $W = q(V_B - V_A) = 1.0 \times 10^{-6} \times (1800 - 0)$.

$W = 1.8 \times 10^{-3}$ J

Example 4

$-Q$ is at $(-a, 0)$ and $+Q$ is at $(a, 0)$. Find the potential at A $(0, a)$.

Show solution
  1. Both are $\sqrt2\,a$ away. Add the potentials, with signs.
  2. $V = \dfrac{k(+Q)}{\sqrt2 a} + \dfrac{k(-Q)}{\sqrt2 a} = 0$.
  3. The field at A is not zero (it is $\sqrt2 kQ/(2a^2)$ to the left), but the potential is. Zero potential does not mean zero field.

$V = 0$

Example 5

Same charges. Find the potential at B $(2a, 0)$.

Show solution
  1. B is $a$ from $+Q$ and $3a$ from $-Q$.
  2. $V = \dfrac{kQ}{a} + \dfrac{k(-Q)}{3a} = \dfrac{3kQ - kQ}{3a}$.

$V = \dfrac{2kQ}{3a}$

Force and field: sizes from the formula ($|Q|$), add as arrows. Potential and potential energy: put charges in with signs, add as numbers. Mixing these rules is the most common mistake here.
Learn the four point-charge formulas as a 2 × 2 table. $F = kQq/r^2$ and $E = kQ/r^2$ are vectors with $r^2$. $U = kQq/r$ and $V = kQ/r$ are scalars with $r$. In each row, the second formula is the first divided by $q$. Only $F = qE$ and $U = qV$ work for every kind of field.
  • $U = kQq/r$ and $V = kQ/r$, zero at infinity; $U = qV$.
  • Potential is a scalar: signed values, simple addition.
  • Work by an outside force: $W = q(V_B - V_A)$.
