DNA, RNA and DNA Replication

Concept — The Molecules That Carry Life's Instructions

Molecular biology studies the molecular basis of biological activity: how biomolecules are made, changed and interact. Nucleic acids carry information and direct protein synthesis. DNA (deoxyribonucleic acid) is the master blueprint and the genetic material of all free-living organisms and most viruses; RNA (ribonucleic acid) is the genetic material of some viruses and is found in all cells, where it helps make proteins.

  • DNA structure: a double helix of two polynucleotide strands. Each nucleotide = a five-carbon deoxyribose sugar + a phosphate group + a nitrogenous base, linked by covalent bonds. The strands have a sugar–phosphate backbone with the bases pointing inwards.
  • Bases: purines, adenine (A) and guanine (G), have two fused rings; pyrimidines, cytosine (C) and thymine (T), have one ring. A purine always pairs with a pyrimidine, so the width of the helix is constant.
  • Complementary base pairing: A pairs with T by two hydrogen bonds; G pairs with C by three. The sequence of one strand fixes the sequence of the other.
  • Antiparallel strands: carbons of deoxyribose are numbered 1′ to 5′; the phosphate is on the 5′ carbon and the OH on the 3′ carbon. One strand runs 5′ → 3′ and the other 3′ → 5′. A DNA sequence is always written 5′ → 3′.
  • RNA structure: a single-stranded polymer of nucleotides with ribose and the bases A, U (uracil, in place of thymine), C, G. tRNA folds back on itself, held by base pairing within the molecule. All RNA is made from a DNA template in the nucleus. mRNA is the template for translation; tRNA carries a specific amino acid to the mRNA at the ribosome; rRNA reads the codon on the mRNA.
  • Role of DNA: the hereditary material passed from cell to cell and generation to generation (about 3.2 billion base pairs in a mammalian cell, so an almost infinite variety of sequences). It is stable (rarely mutates), its strands are held only by hydrogen bonds (so they can separate), it is very large (much information), the bases are protected inside the helix, and base pairing lets it replicate and pass information to mRNA.

DNA replication makes an exact copy of DNA. It is semiconservative (predicted by Watson and Crick, proven by Meselson and Stahl): each new molecule has one old strand and one new strand.

  1. Replication starts at origins of replication, forming replication bubbles. Helicase unwinds and unzips the helix by breaking hydrogen bonds; single-strand binding proteins hold the strands apart; topoisomerase relieves the strain ahead of the fork.
  2. Each end of a bubble is a Y-shaped replication fork.
  3. Primase makes a short RNA primer, because DNA polymerase III cannot start a new chain. DNA pol III adds complementary nucleotides only to a free 3′ end, so new DNA grows only 5′ → 3′.
  4. The leading strand is made continuously towards the fork. The lagging strand is made away from the fork in short Okazaki fragments (about 100–200 nucleotides), each with its own primer. DNA pol I replaces the RNA primers with DNA, and DNA ligase joins the fragments.
  5. DNA pol I also proofreads (mismatch repair); damaged regions (e.g. a thymine dimer caused by UV) are cut out by a nuclease, refilled by DNA polymerase and sealed by ligase.
  6. Some nucleotides are lost from the chromosome ends at each replication. Eukaryotes protect their genes with telomeres, short repeated sequences made by the enzyme telomerase; they get shorter each time DNA replicates.
Key Rules
$$\mathrm{A} = \mathrm{T} \ (2 \text{ H bonds}) \qquad \mathrm{G} \equiv \mathrm{C} \ (3 \text{ H bonds})$$ $$\text{H bonds in a stretch of DNA} = 2 \times (\text{A–T pairs}) + 3 \times (\text{G–C pairs})$$ $$\text{in double-stranded DNA: } \%\mathrm{A} = \%\mathrm{T}, \quad \%\mathrm{G} = \%\mathrm{C}, \quad \text{purines} = \text{pyrimidines} = 50\,\%$$
  • Complement of 5′-ATGC-3′ is 3′-TACG-5′, written 5′-GCAT-3′
  • After n rounds of replication from one molecule: 2n molecules, only 2 of them still hold an original strand
Common Mistakes to Avoid
  • "DNA is written 3′ to 5′": by convention it is written 5′ → 3′, and new strands also grow 5′ → 3′.
  • "DNA pol III starts new strands": it cannot; primase lays down an RNA primer first.
  • Thymine in RNA: RNA has uracil; thymine is found only in DNA.
  • Telomere sequence: the book prints TAAGGG; the human telomere repeat is usually given as TTAGGG.

Worked Examples — Sample Questions (pp. 36–37)

Example A (pairing): One strand of DNA reads 5′-ATGCGTACCA-3′. Write the complementary strand and find the number of hydrogen bonds holding this stretch together.

Show answer

Pair A–T and G–C: the partner is 3′-TACGCATGGT-5′, written 5′-TGGTACGCAT-3′.

A–T pairs: A, T, T, A, A (positions 1, 2, 6, 7, 10) = 5; G–C pairs: G, C, G, C, C = 5.

H bonds = 2 × 5 + 3 × 5 = 25.

Example B (base percentages): A DNA sample contains 30 % adenine. What percentage is guanine?

Show answer

A = T = 30 %, so A + T = 60 %. The other 40 % is G + C, shared equally.

G = 20 %.

Q1 (i)–(iii): TRUE or FALSE. (i) By convention, the sequence of a DNA strand is always written in the 3′ to 5′ direction. (ii) DNA polymerase III cannot initiate the synthesis of a new DNA strand. (iii) Okazaki fragments are joined into one continuous strand by the enzyme DNA ligase.

Show answer

(i) FALSE: it is written 5′ to 3′. (ii) TRUE: it needs an RNA primer made by primase. (iii) TRUE.

Q2 (i)–(iii): Complete. (i) DNA is a double ___ consisting of two polynucleotide strands twined around each other. (ii) DNA consists of two ___ sugar-phosphate chains. (iii) During DNA replication, the damaged regions of DNA are excised out by DNA ___.

(i) helix; (ii) antiparallel; (iii) nuclease.

(i) D, thymine; (iii) B, helicase.

Show answer

Cytosine and guanine → joined by three hydrogen bonds; adenine → always pairs with thymine; DNA replication → semiconservative method.

Four different nitrogenous bases in DNA can be categorised into two forms: (d) and pyrimidines. The purine bases are adenine and (e). They have two fused rings. The pyrimidine bases are cytosine and (f). They have a single ring."

Show answer

(a) nucleotide; (b) deoxyribose; (c) covalent; (d) purines; (e) guanine; (f) thymine.

Q6 (a): Give a short account of the formation of the leading strand and lagging strand during DNA replication.

Show answer

DNA polymerase III adds nucleotides only to a free 3′ end, so both new strands grow 5′ → 3′. Because the templates are antiparallel, the two strands must be made differently.

The leading strand is made continuously towards the replication fork from a single primer. The lagging strand is made away from the fork in short Okazaki fragments (100–200 nucleotides), each started by an RNA primer; DNA pol I replaces the primers and DNA ligase joins the fragments into one strand.

Q7 (a): Give the structure of the RNA molecule together with its different types and their functions.

Show answer

RNA is a single-stranded polymer of nucleotides containing ribose and the bases A, U, C and G (uracil in place of thymine). It can fold back on itself, held by base pairing within the molecule (as in tRNA).

mRNA: the template for translation. tRNA: carries a specific amino acid to the mRNA at the ribosome. rRNA: reads the codon on mRNA (part of the ribosome).

Q8 (a): Discuss the role of DNA in molecular biology.

Show answer

DNA is the hereditary material passed from cell to cell and generation to generation; its huge length (about 3.2 billion base pairs in a mammalian cell) gives an almost infinite variety of sequences and so genetic diversity.

It is adapted because: it is very stable; its strands are joined only by hydrogen bonds so they can separate for replication and protein synthesis; it is very large and carries much information; the bases inside the helix are protected; and base pairing lets it replicate and pass information to mRNA. Its function depends on its base sequence.

Key Takeaways
  • Nucleotide = deoxyribose + phosphate + base; A–T (2 H bonds), G–C (3 H bonds); antiparallel strands written 5′ → 3′.
  • RNA: single strand, ribose, uracil; mRNA, tRNA and rRNA work in protein synthesis.
  • Replication is semiconservative: helicase, primase, DNA pol III, DNA pol I, ligase.
  • Leading strand continuous; lagging strand in Okazaki fragments. Telomeres protect chromosome ends.
