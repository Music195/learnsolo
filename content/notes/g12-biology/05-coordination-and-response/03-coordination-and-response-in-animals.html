Coordination and Response in Animals: Nervous System

Concept — Nerves, Neurones and Synapses

Coordination is the detection of stimuli by receptors, the integration of the information, and the actions of effectors to produce responses. It involves two body systems: the nervous system (nerve impulses) and the endocrine system (hormones). A stimulus is a change in the environment the body can detect. Animals respond to adapt to the environment, for protection from danger, for safety, and to keep the internal environment steady by homeostasis.

Human nervous system: the central nervous system (CNS: brain and spinal cord) and the peripheral nervous system (PNS: nerves linking the CNS to the senses, muscles, glands and organs).

  • Brain: forebrain (cerebrum, thalamus, hypothalamus), midbrain, hindbrain (cerebellum, pons, medulla oblongata). Protected by the skull and three membranes, the meninges.
    • Cerebrum: the largest part (over four fifths of the brain's weight), in left and right hemispheres; intellect, learning, memory, consciousness, language.
    • Thalamus: "the great relay station" of the brain. Hypothalamus: below the thalamus; regulates the internal environment (blood pressure, heart rate, temperature, thirst, hunger, emotions) and links the nervous and endocrine systems.
    • Midbrain: processes eye, ear and nose information; eye movement. Cerebellum: unconscious coordination of posture, reflexes, body movements and fine motor skills. Pons: relay between the two halves of the cerebrum and the cerebellum. Medulla oblongata: connects the brain to the spinal cord; heart rate, blood vessel diameter, breathing, swallowing, coughing.
  • Spinal cord: outer white matter (myelinated fibres) and a butterfly-shaped core of grey matter (unmyelinated neurones, cell bodies, dendrites), protected by cerebrospinal fluid and the vertebrae.
  • Somatic nervous system (voluntary): 12 pairs of cranial nerves and 31 pairs of spinal nerves (cervical 8, thoracic 12, lumbar 5, sacral 5, coccygeal 1), serving the skin, skeletal muscles and joints. Autonomic nervous system (involuntary): controls glands, cardiac and smooth muscle. Its sympathetic part gives the fight-or-flight response (transmitter norepinephrine); the parasympathetic part gives the rest-and-digest response (transmitter acetylcholine).
  • Reflex arc: a rapid, involuntary response through only three neurones: receptor → sensory neurone → interneurone (spinal cord) → motor neurone → effector, in about 50 ms, before the brain has processed the sensation. Examples: pulling the hand from something hot or sharp, blinking, vomiting.

Neurones are the structural and functional units of the nervous system; glial cells ("glue") nourish and support them. A neurone has a cell body with a nucleus, short branched dendrites (receive impulses; branches of the dendron), and one axon (1 mm to 1 m long) carrying impulses away from the cell body. The myelin sheath, made by Schwann cells, protects the axon, supplies nutrients, insulates it, and increases the speed of the impulse; gaps are the nodes of Ranvier.

  • By structure: multipolar (several dendrites; brain and spinal cord), bipolar (one dendrite, one axon; inner ear, retina, olfactory area), unipolar (one process from the cell body; PNS).
  • By function: sensory (receptors → CNS), interneurones (within the CNS), motor (CNS → muscles and glands).

A synapse is the junction between two neurones or a neurone and an effector (a neuromuscular junction joins a motor neurone to a muscle cell). The gap, the synaptic cleft, is only about 0.02 µm wide. At the synaptic terminal the impulse makes synaptic vesicles fuse with the presynaptic membrane and release neurotransmitters by exocytosis; these diffuse across in about 0.5–1 ms and bind to specific receptor proteins on the postsynaptic membrane, opening ion channels. If excitatory, Na+ flows in and the membrane depolarises (an action potential starts if the threshold is reached); if inhibitory, K+ flows out and the membrane is hyperpolarised. An enzyme then breaks the transmitter down and the parts are reabsorbed. Examples: acetylcholine (excites muscle), dopamine, serotonin, endorphins, norepinephrine.

Pathways
$$\text{receptor} \to \text{sensory neurone} \to \text{interneurone} \to \text{motor neurone} \to \text{effector}$$ $$\text{impulse} \to \text{vesicles fuse} \to \text{transmitter crosses cleft} \to \text{binds receptors} \to \text{new impulse}$$
  • Dendrites carry impulses towards the cell body; the axon carries them away.
Common Mistakes to Avoid
  • "The brain decides in a reflex": the reflex passes through the spinal cord; the brain is told afterwards.
  • "The impulse jumps the synapse": the cleft is crossed by a chemical (neurotransmitter) that diffuses.
  • White vs grey matter: white = myelinated fibres (outer in the spinal cord); grey = cell bodies (inner butterfly).

In a reflex (a) arc nerve impulse is transmitted to the spinal cord by (b) sensory neurones. Inside the grey matter of the spinal cord the impulses are passed on to (c) inter neurones.

Q6 (b): Name the component parts of nervous system in human.

Show answer

Central nervous system (brain and spinal cord) and peripheral nervous system. The PNS has sensory and motor pathways; the motor pathways are the somatic (voluntary) and the autonomic (involuntary) nervous systems; the autonomic system is divided into sympathetic and parasympathetic systems.

Q7 (b): Tabulate the parts of the hindbrain with their structures, locations and functions.

Show answer

Cerebellum: walnut-shaped, below and largely behind the cerebrum. It coordinates posture, reflexes and body movements without conscious thought, and fine voluntary motor skills, and receives information from proprioceptors in muscles and joints.

Pons: above and in front of the medulla oblongata in the brainstem. It is a relay centre between the neurones of the right and left halves of the cerebrum, the cerebellum and the rest of the brain.

Medulla oblongata: at the base of the brainstem, where it connects the brain with the spinal cord. It coordinates many reflexes and automatic body functions that maintain homeostasis: heart rate, constriction and dilation of blood vessels, rate and depth of breathing, swallowing and coughing.

Q7 (c): Describe the nature of myelin sheath and its functions.

Show answer

The myelin sheath is a fatty, insulating layer around the axons of some neurones, made of Schwann cells (a type of glial cell) wrapping themselves around the axon.

Functions: (i) to protect the nerve fibre or axon from injury, (ii) to supply nutrients to the axon, (iii) to act as an insulator to the nerve impulses, (iv) to increase the speed of transmission of the nerve impulses.

Q8 (b): Give an account of the structure and function of synapse.

Show answer

A synapse is the connection between two neurones, or a neurone and an effector (a neuromuscular junction joins a motor neurone and a muscle cell). The neurones are not directly connected; between them is a gap, the synaptic cleft, about 0.02 µm wide, too wide for the impulse to jump.

When an action potential reaches the synaptic terminal of the presynaptic neurone, it makes synaptic vesicles fuse with the membrane and release neurotransmitter into the cleft by exocytosis. The transmitter diffuses across in about 0.5 to 1 ms and binds to specific receptor proteins on the postsynaptic membrane, which open ion-specific channels.

An excitatory transmitter lets Na+ flow in, depolarising the membrane; if the threshold is reached, an action potential starts and travels on. An inhibitory transmitter opens K+ channels, causing hyperpolarisation. An enzyme then breaks up the transmitter and its components are reabsorbed by the presynaptic neurone.

Key Takeaways
  • CNS = brain + spinal cord; PNS = nerves (somatic voluntary; autonomic involuntary: sympathetic and parasympathetic).
  • Cerebrum: thinking; cerebellum: coordination; medulla: heart rate and breathing; hypothalamus: internal environment.
  • Reflex arc: receptor → sensory → interneurone → motor → effector, about 50 ms.
  • Myelin insulates and speeds impulses; synapses use neurotransmitters released by exocytosis.
