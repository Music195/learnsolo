Plant Growth and Development

Concept — Growth, Hormones, Seeds and Light

Growth is an irreversible increase in size of a cell, organ or its parts, the result of all the metabolic activities, which use energy from nutrition. Development is the sequence of changes in an organism's life cycle; it starts from a single diploid cell, the zygote, which divides by mitosis, and the cells then differentiate into tissues.

  • Phases of growth: (1) meristematic (formative) phase at the shoot and root apex: cells isodiametric, thin-walled, with a large nucleus and dense cytoplasm; (2) elongation phase: cells enlarge and are highly vacuolated; (3) maturation phase: cells differentiate into specialised tissue.
  • Conditions for growth: light, an optimum temperature of 20–35 °C, water (turgidity, enzymes, cell elongation), oxygen (respiration), mineral nutrition, growth regulators and genetic factors.

Plant growth regulators (plant hormones, phytohormones) are organic compounds made in one part of the plant, in minute quantities, and translocated to another part where they cause a response. They are made mainly at the apices of root and stem and in leaves; one hormone may regulate several aspects of growth; their synthesis is under genetic control and influenced by the environment.

  • Growth promoters: auxin, gibberellin, cytokinin. Growth inhibitors: ethylene and abscisic acid (dormancy and abscission).
  • Auxin (indole acetic acid, IAA), the first plant growth regulator found, is made in meristems, shoot and root tips, young leaves, flowers and fruits. It causes cell elongation in the shoot, inhibits elongation of primary roots but starts lateral and adventitious roots, causes parthenocarpy (seedless fruit), delays abscission in young parts and stimulates it in mature parts, and promotes cell division in the vascular cambium.
  • Gibberellin (e.g. gibberellic acid), made in young organs and germinating seeds, promotes fruit growth, stem elongation and seed germination (it stimulates hydrolytic enzymes such as amylases and proteases).
  • Cytokinin, a purine (adenine) derivative found where cells divide (root apices; coconut milk is rich in it), promotes cell division, flowering, lateral bud development in dicots, and chloroplast development.
  • Ethylene, the only gaseous hormone (made from methionine), ripens fruits (used for commercial ripening), causes drooping of leaves and flowers, and rapid growth of internodes and leaf bases.
  • Abscisic acid (ABA, abscisin, dormin), the stress hormone made in drought, saline soil, cold and frost, promotes dormancy, causes abscission of leaves, flowers and fruits, and acts against all growth promoters.

Seed dormancy is the internal inhibition of germination of a viable seed even in favourable conditions (hard seed coats, an immature embryo, light-sensitive seeds, inhibitors); it lasts for a dormancy period and is broken by scarification, stratification or hormones. In germination, the seed absorbs water through the micropyle; enzymes (amylase, lipase, protease) mobilise the food store; the radicle grows first, then the hypocotyl or epicotyl, then the plumule.

  • Epigeal: the hypocotyl elongates fast and pushes the cotyledons above the ground (bean, castor). Hypogeal: the epicotyl elongates faster and the cotyledons stay in the soil (mango, coconut). Vivipary: the seed germinates inside the fruit while it is still on the parent (Agave, mangroves).

Photoperiodism is the effect of the relative length of day and night on flowering. Plants flower only when the light period is shorter or longer than a critical period. Short day plants (long night plants, e.g. soybean, onion) need a day shorter than the critical period; long day plants (short night plants, e.g. radish, potato) need a longer day; day neutral plants (tomato, cotton) flower regardless. A hormone called florigen, made in the leaves, is proposed to start flowering.

Photomorphogenesis is light-controlled development. In the dark, seedlings have elongated stems, undifferentiated chloroplasts and unexpanded leaves; light inhibits stem elongation and causes chloroplast differentiation, chlorophyll build-up and leaf expansion. Red, far-red and blue light are detected by photoreceptors: phytochrome (a blue-green protein pigment with a chromophore), cryptochrome and phototropin.

Summary
$$\text{promoters: auxin, gibberellin, cytokinin} \qquad \text{inhibitors: ethylene, abscisic acid}$$ $$\text{short day plant flowers if day} < \text{critical}; \quad \text{long day plant flowers if day} > \text{critical}$$
  • Epigeal: hypocotyl lifts cotyledons up. Hypogeal: epicotyl grows; cotyledons stay below.
Common Mistakes to Avoid
  • "Short day plants need short nights": they need long nights; long day plants are short night plants.
  • Photoperiodism vs photomorphogenesis: the first is about day length and flowering; the second is light controlling development (stems, chloroplasts, leaves).
  • Epigeal vs hypogeal: look at the cotyledons: above ground means epigeal (epi = above).

Q6 (a): Give the characteristics of plant growth regulators.

Show answer

They are essential organic compounds required in very small quantities; they are generally made at the apices of root and stem and in leaves; a single hormone may regulate different aspects of growth; and their synthesis is under genetic control and influenced by environmental factors.

Q7 (a): Give an account of the seed germination.

Show answer

A seed germinates in favourable conditions. It absorbs water through the micropyle, which softens the seed coat. Enzymes such as amylase, lipase and protease mobilise the stored food, which is moved to the embryo. The radicle grows first, followed by the hypocotyl or epicotyl, then the plumule.

Epigeal: the hypocotyl elongates rapidly and pushes the cotyledons above the ground (bean, castor). Hypogeal: the epicotyl elongates faster and the cotyledons stay in the soil or just at its surface (mango, coconut). Vivipary: the seed germinates inside the fruit still attached to the parent tree (Agave, mangroves).

Q8 (a): Differentiate the photoperiodism and photomorphogenesis.

Show answer

Photoperiodism is the effect of the relative length of day and night on flowering. Plants flower only when the light period is shorter or longer than a critical period: short day plants (soybean, onion), long day plants (radish, potato), and day neutral plants (tomato, cotton). A hormone, florigen, made in the leaves, is proposed to start flowering.

Photomorphogenesis is light controlling the development of the plant. In the dark, seedlings have long stems, undifferentiated chloroplasts and unexpanded leaves; light inhibits stem elongation and causes chloroplast differentiation, chlorophyll accumulation and leaf expansion. Light (red, far-red, blue) is detected by phytochrome, cryptochrome and phototropin.

Key Takeaways
  • Growth phases: meristematic → elongation → maturation.
  • Promoters: auxin, gibberellin, cytokinin. Inhibitors: ethylene (gas, ripening) and abscisic acid (stress, dormancy, abscission).
  • Germination: radicle first; epigeal lifts the cotyledons, hypogeal leaves them below.
  • Photoperiodism controls flowering by day length; photomorphogenesis is light-controlled development via phytochrome.
