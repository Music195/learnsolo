Sense Organs: The Eye

Concept — How the Eye Works

Sense organs (eyes, ears, nose, tongue, skin) are organs in which sensory receptors are highly concentrated. When the receptors are stimulated, they convert the stimulus into electrical signals (action potentials), which go to a specific region of the brain for that sense. Signals from the eyes are interpreted as images in the vision region of the occipital lobe.

Parts of the eye:

  • Sclera: tough, fibrous, opaque outer coat; protects and keeps the shape of the eyeball. Cornea: transparent front, continuous with the sclera; light enters through it and it refracts the light. Conjunctiva: thin transparent membrane that protects the cornea.
  • Choroid: middle coat with black pigment (absorbs light, prevents internal reflection) and blood capillaries (oxygen and nutrients for the retina). Ciliary body: thickened front of the choroid; changes the thickness of the lens (accommodation).
  • Iris: ring of circular and radial muscles with pigment (eye colour); changes the size of the pupil, the opening that lets light in.
  • Retina: inner layer with photoreceptors: rods (sensitive in low light; night vision) and cones (colour vision in bright light). Fovea (yellow spot): only cones, the clearest vision, where images are normally focused. Blind spot: where the neurones leave as the optic nerve; no photoreceptors, so no vision there.
  • Lens: flexible biconvex structure behind the iris; its curvature (and focal length) changes to focus near or far objects onto the retina. It divides the eye into the aqueous and vitreous chambers.
  • Aqueous humour: watery fluid in front of the lens; keeps the internal pressure, feeds the lens and cornea, refracts light. Vitreous humour: jelly behind the lens; keeps the eye's shape, refracts light.
  • Suspensory ligaments: attach the lens to the ciliary body. Optic nerve: carries impulses from the retina to the brain.

Two eyes cancel each other's blind spot, give a wider field of view, and each forms a slightly different image; the brain combines them into stereoscopic (3-D) vision to judge distance, depth and speed.

Iris reflex: in bright light the circular muscles contract and the radial muscles relax, so the pupil constricts; in dim light the radial muscles contract and the circular muscles relax, so the pupil dilates.

Accommodation (focusing at different distances): for a distant object, rays are almost parallel; the cornea does most of the refraction. The ciliary muscles relax, the pressure in the eye pushes outwards, the suspensory ligaments are pulled tight and the lens is flatter (less convex); this is the eye at rest. For a near object, rays diverge, so the lens must refract more: the ciliary muscles contract, the ligaments become slack and the elastic lens bulges into a more convex shape.

Two Reflexes of the Eye
$$\text{bright: circular contract, radial relax} \Rightarrow \text{pupil constricts}$$ $$\text{near: ciliary contract} \Rightarrow \text{ligaments slack} \Rightarrow \text{lens more convex}$$
  • Extra lens power needed for an object at distance d (compared with a distant object) ≈ 1/d dioptres, with d in metres (thin-lens model).
Common Mistakes to Avoid
  • "Contracting ciliary muscles pull the lens thin": the opposite. Contracting ciliary muscles make the ligaments slack, so the lens bulges (near vision).
  • "The image forms on the cornea or lens": it forms on the retina (normally the fovea).
  • Rods vs cones: rods for dim light, cones for colour in bright light; the fovea has only cones.
Interactive Demo — Pupil Reflex and Accommodation

How does the iris control the light entering the eye, and how does the lens change to focus near and far objects?

Pupil reflex: change the light level and watch which iris muscles contract (red). Accommodation: move the object nearer or farther and watch the ciliary muscles, ligaments and lens; the rays always meet at the fovea.

Worked Examples — Sample Questions (pp. 101–102)

Q1 (v): True or false? The lens focuses the image on the cornea of the eye.

Show answer

False: the lens focuses the image on the retina.

Q2 (v): Aqueous and vitreous chambers are involved in the ___ of light rays onto the retina.

Show answer

refraction (the aqueous and vitreous humours refract light rays).

Q6 (c): State the two types of photoreceptor cells in retina and their functions.

Show answer

Rod cells are sensitive under low light and are responsible for vision at night. Cone cells are responsible for colour vision under conditions of high light intensity.

Example: Why is there no vision at the blind spot, and why do we not notice it?

Show answer

The blind spot is where the neurones leave the retina to form the optic nerve; it has no photoreceptors, so an image falling there is not detected. With two eyes, each eye covers the other's blind spot.

Example: A student looks up from a book to a distant tree. Describe the changes in the eye. How much less lens power is needed if the book was 0.25 m away?

Show answer

For the tree the ciliary muscles relax, the pressure in the eye pushes outwards, the suspensory ligaments are pulled tight, and the lens becomes flatter (less convex).

In the thin-lens model the extra power needed for the book was 1/d = 1/0.25 = 4 dioptres, so the lens reduces its power by about 4 D.

Key Takeaways
  • Receptors in sense organs turn stimuli into impulses; the brain interprets them (vision: occipital lobe).
  • Retina: rods (dim light), cones (colour); fovea = clearest vision; blind spot = no receptors.
  • Bright light: circular muscles contract, pupil constricts. Dim light: radial muscles contract, pupil dilates.
  • Near: ciliary contract, ligaments slack, lens rounder. Far: ciliary relax, ligaments tight, lens flatter.
