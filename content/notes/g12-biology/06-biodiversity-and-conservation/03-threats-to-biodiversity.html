Threats to Biodiversity

Concept — How Biodiversity Is Lost

Biodiversity sustains all life (abundant crops, fresh air). More than 3 billion people depend on marine and coastal biodiversity and more than 1.6 billion on forests; biodiversity loss affects more than 1 billion people living in dry lands. As the human population grows, takes more resources and makes more waste, ecosystems and species are being lost at an alarming rate, directly and through climate change.

Five major threats: habitat loss and degradation; climate change; excessive use of fertilisers and pollution; overexploitation and unsustainable use of resources; invasive alien species (especially affecting endemics).

  • Habitat destruction has played a key role in extinction. Clearing land for agriculture, housing, transport, leisure and industry removes vegetation, so species lose their habitats completely, or their habitats are divided into small areas: habitat fragmentation. Causes include overpopulation, deforestation, pollution and global warming. Larger species and species at lower latitudes, in forests or in oceans are more sensitive to a loss of habitat area. Major reasons: large-scale industry and commerce, mining, cattle rearing, commercial fishing, dam construction, encroaching, plantation cropping and agriculture.
  • Land-use change: shifting cultivation in upland areas, gold mining, urbanisation and clearing natural forest for agriculture degrade the environment. Habitat loss (natural environments transformed for human needs, e.g. deforestation, damming rivers) is the most significant cause of biodiversity loss globally. Threats to agrobiodiversity: replacing local landraces with modern varieties, expanding farmland, overgrazing, dams and canals, urbanisation.
  • Pollution of soil, water and air: uncontrolled pesticide and herbicide use, factory wastes, rubbish, sewage, radioactive and industrial wastes.
  • Invasive alien species (IAS) are introduced unintentionally (tourists, cargo, pets, plant parts, seeds) or on purpose (research, medicine, ornamental and industrial uses), e.g. water hyacinth and golden apple snails in Myanmar.
  • Genetic pollution: undesirable gene flow from genetically engineered (GE or GMO) organisms into wild populations, e.g. by wind, rain, birds, bees and insects carrying altered pollen to nearby fields. Once released, GE organisms cannot be recalled.
  • Overexploitation (overharvesting) destroys a resource because it cannot replenish itself. Overfishing removes fish faster than the population can replace itself; overlogging causes permanent forest loss; overhunting threatens one-third of endangered vertebrates (as of 2016, 301 terrestrial mammals threatened by hunting for bushmeat; the illegal wildlife trade, excluding fish, is worth $10 billion a year).
  • Climate change alters climate patterns and ecosystems (rising ocean temperatures, shrinking Arctic sea ice, shifting vegetation zones); species that cannot adjust become extinct (e.g. Cyclone Nargis, floods, landslides).
  • Human overpopulation increases demand for food, water, housing, energy and transport, leading to deforestation, pollution, emissions and conflicts.
  • Wildlife trade is commerce in products from non-domesticated animals and plants (skins, bones, tusks, horns, meat, timber), legal or illegal. Illegal trade in neighbouring countries threatens Myanmar's endangered wildlife; poverty worsens it. The most trafficked animal is the pangolin (the only scaly mammal; about 20 % of illegal wildlife trade; over a million poached in a decade). Wildlife markets are linked to zoonotic diseases such as COVID-19, H5N1 avian flu, SARS and monkeypox. Laws: CITES (Convention on International Trade in Endangered Species of Wild Flora and Fauna) and Myanmar's Conservation of Biodiversity and Protected Areas Law (2018).
Two Models Used in the Demo
$$\text{fish growth per year} = rN\left(1 - \frac{N}{K}\right), \qquad \text{largest sustainable catch} = \frac{rK}{4}$$ $$\text{area covered by an invader} = c_0 \times 2^{\,t/T}$$
  • N = fish stock, K = the most the water can support, r = growth rate. Catch more than rK/4 each year and the stock collapses (overfishing).
  • An invader that doubles every T weeks looks harmless for a long time, then covers everything quickly.
Common Mistakes to Avoid
  • "Only the total area lost matters": fragmentation can wipe out large species even when most of the habitat remains, because no single piece is big enough.
  • "Zoonotic diseases are non-infectious": they are infectious diseases passed from animals to humans.

Q1 (iv), (v): True or false? (iv) The destruction of the natural environment leads to habitat loss. (v) The illegal wildlife trade has been linked to the emergence and spread of new non-infectious diseases in humans.

(iv) True. (v) False: it is linked to new infectious (zoonotic) diseases.

C. SARS (severe acute respiratory syndrome).

Natural environments are transformed → F. Habitat loss; Overexploitation → E. Overharvesting.

Q6 (c): How do you understand wildlife trade?

Show answer

Wildlife trade is the commerce of products derived from non-domesticated animals or plants, taken from their natural environment or raised under controlled conditions. It includes living or dead individuals and tissues such as skins, bones, feathers and meat. It can be legal or illegal, and domestic or international. Illegal trade directly causes the loss of species and habitat destruction, and affects local communities.

Q7 (a), (b): State the major threats to biodiversity. Give the reasons for habitat destruction.

Show answer

(a) Habitat loss and the degradation of the environment; climate change; excessive use of fertilisers and industrial and domestic pollution; overexploitation and unsustainable use of resources; and the effects of invasive alien species on native species, especially endemics.

(b) Large-scale industrial and commercial activities, mining, cattle rearing, commercial fishing, dam construction, encroaching, and plantation cropping and agriculture.

Example (model): With growth 0.5 N (1 − N/1000) tonnes per year, what is the largest yearly catch that does not cause collapse?

Show answer

The growth is greatest at N = 500 t: 0.5 × 500 × (1 − 0.5) = 125 t per year (= rK/4). Catching more than 125 t a year removes fish faster than they replace themselves, which is overfishing.

Key Takeaways
  • Five major threats: habitat loss, climate change, pollution, overexploitation, invasive alien species.
  • Habitat loss is the biggest cause; fragmentation leaves pieces too small for large species.
  • Overexploitation takes resources faster than they can be replaced (overfishing, overlogging, overhunting).
  • Illegal wildlife trade (pangolins most trafficked) spreads zoonotic diseases; CITES and Myanmar's 2018 law protect wildlife.
