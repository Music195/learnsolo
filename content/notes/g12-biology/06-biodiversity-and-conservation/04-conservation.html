Conservation

Concept — Keeping What We Still Have

Biodiversity is being reduced by human activities (overexploitation, deforestation, habitat destruction, pollution) and by climate change, but many people work to reverse these trends. Conservation means keeping and protecting a living and changing environment. It is an active process involving an enormous range of projects; reserves must be managed effectively, damaged areas restored, native species protected, and benefits kept sustainable for the present and the future.

Reasons for conservation:

  • Narrowly utilitarian: useful products such as food, fibres, drugs and medicines come from biodiversity.
  • Broadly utilitarian: biodiversity provides ecosystem services such as oxygen, pollination of crops, and control of floods and erosion.
  • Ethical: every living species has an intrinsic value and the right to live, even without direct economic value.

In situ conservation conserves species in their natural habitat, the best way, because all the "life support systems" are provided. Nature reserves (wildlife sanctuaries, national parks, biosphere reserves) maintain habitat and genetic diversity, protect species from predators and from competition with invasive species, and let them keep adapting naturally. Active management involves (1) continuous monitoring, (2) maintaining effective boundaries, (3) helping species complete their life cycles, and (4) restocking and reintroducing species from captive breeding.

Ex situ conservation conserves animals and plants outside their natural habitats, usually as a last resort. Three methods:

  • Captive breeding in zoos and parks, aiming to reintroduce animals into the wild. Techniques: artificial insemination, embryo transfer to a surrogate mother, cryogenics (frozen eggs, sperm and embryos), human-raised young, and keeping a pedigree (to prevent inbreeding). Problems: not enough space or money for every endangered species; the right breeding conditions are hard to provide; the conditions that endangered the species may continue; captive-bred animals may struggle in the wild; reintroduction is expensive, slow and may fail.
  • Seed banks: about 25 % of the world's ~242,000 flowering plant species could disappear within 50 years (about 60,500 species). Seeds are collected, dried, stored at −14 °C in dark conditions (metabolism slows so they do not germinate), tested for germination at regular intervals, and grown into mature plants to collect fresh seed (e.g. Department of Agricultural Research, Yezin). Saved genes help breeders improve resistance to diseases and insects, drought or flood tolerance, and yields and nutrition.
  • Botanical gardens: about 80,000 plant species are grown in gardens and arboretums; they are research centres, keep stocks of native and endangered plants, and are ideal places to integrate the study and conservation of plants.

Protected areas in Myanmar: 42 protected areas safeguard ecosystems and their services (water, food, carbon storage, climate regulation) and spiritual and cultural values. Categories:

  • Scientific reserve: for scientific study, research and education, e.g. Tanintharyi Nature Reserve.
  • National park: set aside by the national government; no settlement or resource harvesting, visitors permitted, e.g. Alaungdaw Kathapa, Namataung National Park.
  • Marine national park: a national park in marine, island and coastal environments, e.g. Lampi Island Marine National Park.
  • Nature reserve: for important flora, fauna or geological features, for conservation, study and research, e.g. Popa Mountain Park, Hkakaborazi National Park.
  • Wildlife sanctuary: animal habitats protected from any disturbance; capturing, killing and poaching strictly prohibited, e.g. Hlawga Park, Chatthin Wildlife Sanctuary.
  • Community forest: introduced in the early 1990s; sustainable forest management by the local community itself, providing subsistence and income while conserving biodiversity.

Common Mistakes to Avoid
  • Swapping the terms: national parks and sanctuaries are in situ; zoos, seed banks and botanical gardens are ex situ.
  • "Seeds are stored warm and in light": they are kept cold (−14 °C) and dark so they do not germinate.

Q8 (b): State the differences between in situ and ex situ conservation methods.

Show answer

In situ methods conserve species in their natural habitat, where all the life support systems are provided. Nature reserves (wildlife sanctuaries, national parks, biosphere reserves) maintain habitat and genetic diversity, protect species from predators and invasive competitors, and let them adapt naturally. Active management includes monitoring, boundaries, support for life cycles, and restocking.

Ex situ methods conserve animals and plants outside their natural habitats, usually as a last resort, when the conditions in the wild have put them under threat of extinction. The three methods are captive breeding of animals, storage of seeds in seed banks, and cultivation of plants in botanical gardens.

Q8 (c): List the categories of protected areas in Myanmar and explain any three of them.

Show answer

Categories: scientific reserve, national park, marine national park, nature reserve, wildlife sanctuary, community forest.

National park: set aside by the national government to preserve the natural environment; no settlement or resource harvesting, visitors allowed (Alaungdaw Kathapa, Namataung). Wildlife sanctuary: animal habitats protected from any disturbance; capturing, killing and poaching strictly prohibited (Hlawga Park, Chatthin). Community forest: introduced in the early 1990s; sustainable forest management by the local community, meeting subsistence needs while protecting forest resources.

Key Takeaways
  • Reasons: narrowly utilitarian (products), broadly utilitarian (ecosystem services), ethical (right to live).
  • In situ: protected areas in the natural habitat. Ex situ: captive breeding, seed banks, botanical gardens.
  • Seed banks store dried seeds at −14 °C in the dark and test them regularly.
  • Myanmar has 42 protected areas: scientific reserves, national parks, marine national parks, nature reserves, wildlife sanctuaries, community forests.
