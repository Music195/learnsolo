Translocation

Concept — Moving Food from Source to Sink

Translocation is the movement of dissolved substances through a plant. Water and dissolved salts travel up from the soil in the xylem vessels; food made in the leaves passes downwards or upwards in the sieve tubes of the phloem.

  • Leaves make carbohydrates by photosynthesis and send them out as sucrose. In the stem it can travel upwards to growing regions, maturing fruits and seeds, or downwards to the roots and underground storage organs. Both directions can happen at the same time in the phloem.
  • Phloem is a complex tissue: sieve tubes, companion cells, phloem parenchyma and phloem fibres. All are living except the fibres.
  • Sieve tubes (sieve cells) have no nucleus; with the companion cells they do the work of translocation. They are cylindrical cells with a layer of cytoplasm just inside the wall. Their name comes from the perforated cross walls, the sieve plates, through which materials pass from cell to cell.
  • Sieve cells survive without nuclei because they are kept alive by the nucleated companion cells beside them, joined by many cytoplasmic connections. Fibre cells strengthen the tissue.
  • Two ideas explain the flow (Figure 3.6): cytoplasmic streaming could carry two substances in opposite directions at once; the pressure flow theory says sugar entering the sieve tubes at a source (leaf) draws water in by osmosis, giving a high turgor pressure; at a sink (growing tip, root) sugar is removed for growth and water leaves by osmosis, giving low pressure. The sugar solution flows from high to low turgor pressure.
Pressure Flow in One Line
$$\text{source: sugar in} \to \text{water in (osmosis)} \to \text{high pressure} \;\Longrightarrow\; \text{sink: sugar out} \to \text{water out} \to \text{low pressure}$$
  • Source = where sugar is made or released (leaf); sink = where it is used or stored (growing tip, root, fruit, tuber)
  • Xylem: one way, up. Phloem: up or down, from source to sink
Common Mistakes to Avoid
  • Phloem flows only downwards: it flows from source to sink, which can be up or down.
  • Sieve tubes are dead like xylem: they are living, but have no nucleus; companion cells keep them alive.

This movement is translocation. Water and dissolved salts travel up from the soil in the xylem vessels; food made in the leaves is carried downwards or upwards in the sieve tubes of the phloem.

Leaves send carbohydrates out as sucrose; in the stem it moves up to growing regions, fruits and seeds or down to roots and storage organs, both at the same time.

Phloem consists of sieve tubes, companion cells, phloem parenchyma and fibres (all living except fibres). Sieve tubes have no nucleus and pass materials through perforated sieve plates; they are kept alive by the companion cells, with many cytoplasmic connections. Fibres strengthen the tissue.

Example: In spring a potato tuber sprouts. Is the tuber a source or a sink now? Explain.

Show answer

A source: its stored starch is changed back into sugar and sent through the phloem to the growing shoots, which are the sinks. (While it was filling up, the tuber was a sink.)

Key Takeaways
  • Translocation moves sucrose and amino acids in the phloem, from sources to sinks, up or down.
  • Sieve tubes: living, no nucleus, sieve plates; companion cells keep them alive.
  • Pressure flow: sugar in → water in → high pressure at the source; sugar out → water out → low pressure at the sink.
