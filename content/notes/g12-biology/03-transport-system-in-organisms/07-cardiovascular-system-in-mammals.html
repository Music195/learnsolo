Cardiovascular System in Mammals

Concept — Pump, Pipes and the Fluid Inside

The cardiovascular system delivers what the cells need and carries away their wastes. It is made of the heart (the pump), a series of blood vessels and the blood (the transport medium). The passage of blood through the vessels is the circulation.

1. The heart is a muscular pump with four chambers: two upper atria (right and left) and two lower ventricles. Both sides work at the same time; the atria have thinner walls than the ventricles.

  • The right atrium receives deoxygenated blood from the superior vena cava (head, neck, arms, chest) and inferior vena cava (lower body). The left atrium receives oxygenated blood from the lungs through the pulmonary veins.
  • The right ventricle sends deoxygenated blood to the lungs through the pulmonary artery; the left ventricle sends oxygenated blood into the aorta and round the body. A thick muscular septum keeps the two sides apart.
  • Cardiac muscle contracts regularly without resting or tiring. It is supplied by the coronary arteries (and drained by coronary veins) and contains much myoglobin, a pigment with a stronger affinity for oxygen than haemoglobin.
  • The left ventricle wall is much thicker than the right: the right ventricle pumps only to the nearby lungs, whose delicate capillaries need low pressure; the left must push blood to all the extremities and overcome the elastic recoil of the arteries.
  • Valves: the right atrioventricular valve is the tricuspid (three flaps); the left is the bicuspid or mitral (two flaps). They stop backflow from ventricle to atrium; their flaps are held by tendinous cords (chordae tendineae) so they do not turn inside out when the ventricles contract. Two pocket-like semilunar ("half-moon") valves at the base of the pulmonary artery and the aorta stop backflow into the ventricles.

2. Blood vessels.

  • Arteries carry blood away from the heart. Almost all carry oxygenated blood; exceptions: the pulmonary artery and the umbilical artery. They branch into smaller arteries and the smallest, arterioles. Walls near the heart have many elastic fibres that stretch with each surge and then recoil, keeping the pressure up and the flow continuous; a thick muscular wall widens (dilates) or narrows (constricts) the vessel to control flow.
  • Veins carry blood back to the heart, mostly deoxygenated; exceptions: the pulmonary vein and the umbilical vein. Venules join into larger veins and finally the vena cava. Veins hold over half the blood (a reservoir), at low pressure; their walls are thinner with less elastic tissue and muscle. One-way semilunar valves close if blood flows backwards; contraction of large muscles helps push blood along.
  • Capillaries have walls just one very thin cell thick, with no elastic fibres, smooth muscle or collagen, so they fit between cells and allow rapid diffusion: oxygen, digested food and hormones out to the cells; CO₂ and wastes back in.

3. Blood is plasma, red cells, white cells and platelets.

  • Plasma (over 50 % of blood volume) carries digested food (glucose, amino acids) from the small intestine to the liver and body; nutrients from stores to cells; excretory products (CO₂, urea) to lungs and kidneys; hormones to their target organs; heat from active tissues to the skin; and it acts as a buffer against pH changes.
  • Erythrocytes (red blood cells): about 5 million per mm³ (4–5 million in women, 5–6 million in men); contain the red pigment haemoglobin; made in bone marrow; mature cells have no nucleus; live about 120 days. The biconcave disc shape gives a large surface area to volume ratio for fast diffusion of oxygen. Each holds 250–300 million haemoglobin molecules and carries about 1000 million oxygen molecules. Haemoglobin also carries some CO₂; the rest is in the plasma.
  • Leucocytes (white blood cells): much larger, but can change shape to squeeze through small vessels; 4000–11 000 per mm³; several types, made in bone marrow (some mature in the thymus). They defend against infection and take part in inflammation; all have a nucleus, and some have granules in their cytoplasm.
  • Platelets: tiny fragments of large bone-marrow cells, megakaryocytes; 150 000–400 000 per mm³; involved in clotting.

4. Clotting seals damaged vessels to limit blood loss and keep pathogens out. When platelets touch damaged tissue (e.g. collagen fibres) they break open and release serotonin (makes the vessel's smooth muscle contract, narrowing it) and thromboplastin (an enzyme that starts clotting). Then: thromboplastin (with Ca²⁺ ions; vitamin K is needed to make many clotting factors) converts the inactive soluble plasma protein prothrombin into the active enzyme thrombin; thrombin converts soluble fibrinogen into insoluble fibrin; fibrin forms a mesh that covers the wound and traps red cells: the clot.

5. The cardiac cycle is the sequence of contraction (systole) and relaxation (diastole) of the four chambers that make one heartbeat, about 0.8 s in humans. Atrial systole comes first; ventricular systole follows about 0.13 s later; then the heart relaxes and fills (diastole). The stages: (1) blood enters the atria; it cannot pass to the ventricles yet; (2) the atria contract, opening the bicuspid and tricuspid valves, so blood fills the ventricles; (3) the full ventricles contract; the pressure closes the bicuspid and tricuspid valves; (4) the pressure keeps rising and opens the semilunar valves; blood is ejected into the pulmonary artery (to the lungs) and the aorta (to the body); (5) the empty ventricles relax; the higher pressure in the arteries closes the semilunar valves, and the cycle restarts as the atria fill.

6. Control of the heartbeat. Cardiac muscle is myogenic: it contracts without any outside stimulus, with its own rhythm (a removed heart keeps beating in a suitable oxygen-rich fluid); the intrinsic rate is about 60 beats per minute. The sinoatrial node (SAN) in the right atrium has the fastest intrinsic rhythm and is the pacemaker: its wave of electrical excitation (depolarisation) makes the atria contract. The wave reaches the atrioventricular node (AVN), which adds a short delay so the atria finish contracting first, then passes it to the bundle of His in the septum, which splits into two branches and carries it to the Purkyne fibres spreading round the ventricles. The ventricles contract from the bottom up, squeezing blood out. These electrical changes are recorded as an electrocardiogram (ECG). The average resting rate is about 70 per minute because nerves and hormones constantly adjust it.

Common Mistakes to Avoid
  • "Arteries carry oxygenated blood": they carry blood away from the heart; the pulmonary artery carries deoxygenated blood.
  • Tricuspid on the left: tricuspid = right; bicuspid (mitral) = left.
  • The AVN starts the beat: the SAN is the pacemaker; the AVN only delays and passes the wave on.
  • Thrombin is in the plasma waiting: the plasma has inactive prothrombin; thrombin is made only at a wound.

Worked Examples — Sample Questions (pp. 59–60)

Example A: One cardiac cycle lasts 0.8 s. Find the heart rate. If exercise shortens the cycle to 0.5 s, what is the new rate?

Show answer

Heart rate = 60 / cycle time = 60 / 0.8 = 75 beats per minute.

60 / 0.5 = 120 beats per minute.

Example B: A red blood cell has 250 million haemoglobin molecules, and each haemoglobin carries 4 oxygen molecules. How many oxygen molecules can the cell carry?

Show answer

250 × 10⁶ × 4 = 1.0 × 10⁹, about 1000 million (as the book states).

Q7 (c): Give a flow diagram of the blood clotting process.

Show answer

damaged tissues → (release) platelets → (release) thromboplastin;

thromboplastin + Ca²⁺ → catalyses prothrombin → thrombin;

thrombin catalyses fibrinogen → fibrin → (forms) clot.

Q8 (b): Give a precise explanation of the plasma.

Show answer

Plasma is the liquid part of the blood, over 50 % of its volume; it carries the blood cells and everything that must be transported.

It carries: (i) digested food (glucose, amino acids) from the small intestine to the liver and then where needed; (ii) nutrients from stores to the cells; (iii) excretory products (CO₂, urea) to the lungs or kidneys; (iv) hormones to their target organs; (v) heat from internal organs or active muscles to the skin; and (vi) it acts as a buffer to regulate pH.

Key Takeaways
  • Four chambers; tricuspid (right), bicuspid (left), semilunar valves; thick left ventricle.
  • Arteries: elastic and muscular, away from the heart; veins: thin, valves, back to the heart; capillaries: one cell thick.
  • Blood: plasma, red cells (haemoglobin, no nucleus), white cells (defence), platelets (clotting).
  • Clotting: thromboplastin + Ca²⁺ → thrombin → fibrin mesh. Cardiac cycle about 0.8 s; SAN → AVN → His → Purkyne.
