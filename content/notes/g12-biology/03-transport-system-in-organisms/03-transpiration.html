Transpiration

Concept — Leaves Lose Water, and That Pulls More Up

Mesophyll cells are loosely packed with air spaces around them. Their walls are wet, and some water evaporates into the air spaces, so the air inside the leaf is usually saturated with water vapour. This air connects with the outside air through small pores, the stomata. Inside the leaf the water potential is higher than outside, so water vapour diffuses out down this gradient. Some water is used (e.g. in photosynthesis), but most evaporates and diffuses out: this is transpiration. Water molecules held together by cohesion (hydrogen bonding) and stuck to the xylem walls by adhesion are pulled up from the roots in a continuous column.

Factors affecting transpiration:

  • Humidity: low humidity makes the water potential gradient steeper, so transpiration is faster than in high humidity.
  • Wind speed and temperature: transpiration increases with wind speed and with rising temperature.
  • Light intensity: in most plants stomata open in the day and close at night, so transpiration is almost zero at night. Stomata must open in the day for CO₂ to diffuse in for photosynthesis, which unavoidably increases transpiration; closing at night, when photosynthesis is impossible, saves water.
  • Very dry conditions: the plant may partly or completely close its stomata to stop its leaves drying out, even though this reduces photosynthesis.

In hot conditions transpiration cools the leaves: evaporating water absorbs heat. If water is lost faster than it is taken up, cells become less turgid and the plant wilts; it also closes its stomata.

Benefits of transpiration: ascent of sap (the transpiration pull, which also helps water absorption); removal of excess water; cooling (lowers leaf temperature by 10–15 °C); development of mechanical tissue; distribution of mineral salts by the rising sap; increased concentration of mineral salts in the plant; better root development; higher ash and sugar content in fruits; hardening and resistance to moderate drought; turgidity of cells; and water for photosynthesis, pulled into the leaf molecule by molecule.

Demonstration of transpiration pull: a thistle funnel full of water is fixed by a rubber stopper to a leafy twig, with its bottom in a bowl of mercury. As the twig loses water by transpiration, it draws water up from the funnel, leaving a vacuum below that is filled by rising mercury. The rise of water and mercury is due to the transpiration pull.

What Speeds It Up
$$\text{transpiration} \uparrow \text{ when: humidity} \downarrow, \;\; \text{wind} \uparrow, \;\; \text{temperature} \uparrow, \;\; \text{light} \uparrow \text{ (stomata open)}$$
  • Water vapour diffuses from high water potential (inside leaf) to low (outside air)
  • Cohesion: water to water; adhesion: water to xylem walls
Common Mistakes to Avoid
  • "More humidity, more transpiration": it is the reverse; humid air makes the gradient shallower.
  • Stomata exist to lose water: they open to let CO₂ in for photosynthesis; water loss is the unavoidable cost.

After some time the mercury rises in the funnel. The twig loses water by transpiration, which draws water up out of the funnel; this leaves a vacuum at the bottom that is filled by the rising mercury. The rise of water and mercury is due to the transpiration pull.

Example: A plant on a hot, windy, dry afternoon wilts although its soil is moist. Explain.

Show answer

High temperature, wind and low humidity make transpiration very fast. When water is lost faster than the roots can take it up, the cells lose turgor and the soft parts droop; the plant then closes its stomata.

Key Takeaways
  • Water evaporates from wet mesophyll walls and diffuses out through the stomata.
  • Faster in low humidity, wind, high temperature and light; slows when stomata close (night, drought).
  • Benefits include the transpiration pull, cooling, mineral distribution and turgidity.
