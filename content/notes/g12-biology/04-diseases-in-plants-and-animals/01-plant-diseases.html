Plant Diseases

Concept — What Makes a Plant Sick

A plant disease is abnormal growth and/or dysfunction of a plant: any abnormal condition that alters its appearance or function. It may reduce the yield and quality of the harvest. The study of plant diseases is plant pathology (Greek pathos, suffering, disease + logos, study).

  • Biotic (infectious) diseases are caused by living pathogens: fungi, bacteria, viruses, nematodes, mycoplasma, viroids, parasitic flowering plants. The agent reproduces in its host and spreads from one susceptible host to another, usually of the same species. Around 85 % of plant diseases are caused by fungi or fungus-like organisms.
  • Abiotic (non-infectious) diseases are caused by non-living conditions: drought stress, frost and freeze injury, sunscald, wind, soil compaction, salt damage, chemical injury, nutrient deficiency, overwatering, air pollution. They cannot spread from plant to plant, often damage many species at once, and show no signs of a pathogen.
  • The disease triangle: disease occurs, and is severe, only when three factors come together: a susceptible host plant, a pathogen, and favourable environmental conditions.

Signs are physical evidence of the pathogen itself: fungal fruiting bodies, mildew, mycelium, mushrooms, spore masses, bacterial ooze, nematode cysts. Symptoms are the visible effects on the plant, its reaction to the agent: blight, canker, chlorosis, dieback, galls, leaf spot, mosaic, wilt, stunting, rot, necrosis. Viruses cause mottling, mosaic patterns, crinkling and stunting; mosaics are light green, yellow or white patches mixed with normal green, and ringspots are chlorotic or necrotic rings on the leaves.

Pathogen groups:

  • Fungi and fungus-like organisms (over 8,000 species cause disease), e.g. Puccinia (rust of cereals), Fusarium (root rot), Rhizopus (soft rot of fruits); Pythium and Phytophthora cause downy mildew.
  • Bacteria (about 200 types cause plant disease, most active in warm, humid conditions; spread by rain splash, wind, birds and insects), e.g. Agrobacterium (crown gall), Erwinia (fire blight of pears and apples), Xanthomonas (citrus canker, blight of rice).
  • Viruses are intracellular pathogenic particles. They are not spread by water or wind but must physically enter the plant, often carried by insects. They are systemic, so infected plants must be removed (rogued) or discarded. Examples: tobacco mosaic, tomato spotted wilt, barley yellow dwarf.
  • Nematodes are tiny, soft-bodied, non-segmented roundworms. Plant parasitic nematodes have a stylet, a piercing mouthpart, which is the key sign that tells them apart, e.g. root-knot nematodes (Meloidogyne).

Control: biological control uses living microorganisms (antagonists that parasitise the pathogen, secrete antibiotics, compete for nutrients and space, or kill it by lysis); physical control uses high or low temperature, removing moisture, irradiation, removing sources of the pathogen and barriers between pathogen and host; chemical control uses bactericides, fungicides and nematicides on seeds, foliage, flowers, fruit or soil.

Key Ideas
$$\text{disease} \;\Leftarrow\; \text{susceptible host} + \text{pathogen} + \text{favourable environment}$$ $$\text{signs} = \text{the pathogen itself} \qquad \text{symptoms} = \text{the plant's reaction}$$
  • Remove any one side of the triangle and the disease cannot develop.
Common Mistakes to Avoid
  • Mixing up signs and symptoms: a mildew growth or bacterial ooze is a sign (the pathogen); yellowing or wilting is a symptom (the plant's response).
  • Thinking abiotic diseases spread: drought or frost damage does not pass from plant to plant; only biotic diseases are infectious.
  • Spraying fungicide on a virus disease: chemicals for fungi, bacteria and nematodes do not cure a virus; infected plants are removed.

Q6 (a): How do you understand the symptoms?

Show answer

Symptoms are visible effects of disease on plants: any detectable change in colour, shape and/or function in response to a pathogen or disease-causing agent. They are the plant's reaction to the causal agent, e.g. blight, canker, chlorosis, wilt, leaf spot, mosaic, stunting.

Q7 (a): Briefly explain the types of plant pathogens.

Show answer

Fungi and fungus-like organisms cause more plant diseases than any other group (over 8,000 species), e.g. Puccinia, rust of cereals. Bacteria: about 200 types, spread by splashing rain, wind, birds or insects, e.g. Xanthomonas, citrus canker.

Viruses are intracellular particles that must physically enter the plant, often through insects, e.g. tobacco mosaic. Nematodes are microscopic roundworms with a piercing stylet, e.g. root-knot nematodes (Meloidogyne).

Q8 (a): Clarify the plant diseases control methods.

Show answer

Biological control uses living microorganisms. The antagonist may parasitise the pathogen, secrete antibiotics harmful to it, compete with it for nutrients or space, or kill it with enzymes (lysis).

Physical control uses the limits of the disease agent: high or low temperature, removing moisture, irradiation, removing sources of the pathogen, and barriers between the agent and the host.

Chemical control uses chemicals that stop the pathogens growing or kill them: bactericides, fungicides and nematicides applied to seeds, foliage, flowers, fruit or soil.

Key Takeaways
  • Biotic diseases are caused by pathogens and spread; abiotic diseases are caused by conditions and do not spread.
  • Disease needs a susceptible host, a pathogen and a favourable environment (the disease triangle).
  • Signs are the pathogen itself; symptoms are the plant's reaction.
  • Pathogens: fungi (most), bacteria, viruses (insect vectors), nematodes (stylet). Control: biological, physical, chemical.
